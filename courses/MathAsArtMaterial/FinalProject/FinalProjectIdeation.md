# Final Project: Ideation Protocol
*Math as Art Material*

Your final project will **not** look the same as anyone else’s. That is expected.

This class has asked you to treat math not only as a school subject, but as a material, a force, a structure, a problem, a pressure, a visual language, a thing that can be cut up, remade, mapped, performed, or pushed against. The final project should grow out of your encounters with the materials, ideas, and experiments of the semester.

The goal of this page is to help you move from **collected course materials** to a **real project direction**.

---

## What you should already have

Before beginning this ideation process, make sure you have gathered the following:

- one graph from the graph code
- one equation from one of our class sketches
- one equation or concept from your Math 1130 course
- at least one page from the non-identity text
- one earlier **Do Something With It** project
- any photos, notes, sketches, blackout poems, maps, circuits, sticky-note graphs, or other materials from this semester that still feel important to you

Keep these in one working folder and in your working document.

Images of all of your physical work can be found here: 

[Link to Photo Album](https://photos.app.goo.gl/ULikrgrA4Eo8paim8)


It is okay if you use an image that isn't exactly your work, but representative of the project or how you feel about the project.

---

## Step 1: Name your lens

Before deciding what to make, spend a little time figuring out what *you* keep noticing.

Write for about five minutes. You can do this in complete sentences, fragments, or a list.

Use these prompts:

- Math feels like...
- Math keeps showing up for me as...
- This semester I kept returning to...
- I am most drawn to...
- I am most resistant to...
- I notice that I keep using or choosing...

Then write one sentence:

> **My current lens is: math as ____________.**

Examples:

- math as pressure
- math as decoration
- math as anxiety
- math as pattern
- math as memory
- math as movement
- math as control
- math as play
- math as rupture
- math as object

You do not need the perfect answer. You just need a useful starting point.

### What strong work looks like
Strong work here is honest, specific, and connected to your own experience in the course. It does not need to sound “academic.”

---

## Step 2: Revisit your materials

Lay out 4–6 items from your collected materials.

For each one, write a few notes:

- What is this?
- Why did I keep it?
- What does it make me notice?
- What feels strange, difficult, or alive about it?
- What does it seem to want me to do with it?
- What resists me?

Do not try to force a final idea yet. This step is about paying attention.

### What strong work looks like
Strong work here goes beyond summary. It notices tension, surprise, resistance, attraction, or contradiction.

---

## Step 3: Make three combinations

Now begin connecting things.

Create **three different combinations** from your course materials.

Each combination should include:

- at least one mathematical item
- at least one text or language-based item
- at least one visual, spatial, or physical item

For each combination, write a short note:

- Why do these belong together?
- Where do they clash?
- What changes when I read one through another?
- What kind of project might come from this combination?

Do not stop at the first combination that seems workable. Try at least three.

### What strong work looks like
Strong work here shows actual experimentation. The combinations should produce a new direction, not just sit beside one another.

---

## Step 4: Test three possible forms

Take your strongest combination and imagine it in **three different project forms**.

Possible forms include:

- sculpture
- collage
- artist book
- installation
- paper circuit
- map
- wearable object
- drawing series
- game
- performance
- video
- sound piece
- digital sketch
- mixed-media object
- interactive piece

For each possible form, sketch or briefly describe:

- what it would be
- what materials it would use
- what the viewer would encounter
- what part of math would be transformed through it

This does not need to be polished. Quick thinking is enough.

### What strong work looks like
Strong work here shows flexibility. You are trying possibilities rather than locking into the first obvious answer.

---

## Step 5: Decide what the project does

Your project should not only **show** something. It should also **do** something.

Write a short paragraph answering some of the following:

- What is my project transforming?
- What part of math is being reworked?
- What will the viewer notice, feel, read, hear, touch, or question?
- Where is the tension?
- Where is the mismatch?
- Where is the pushback?
- What is becoming visible here that was not visible before?

Then complete this sentence:

> **My project works by ____________________.**

Examples:

- turning an equation into an object
- making a graph tactile
- connecting feeling and mathematical structure
- forcing abstraction into material form
- showing where symbolic language does not fully capture lived experience
- treating math like a scene instead of a solution

### What strong work looks like
Strong work here explains the project’s action clearly. It should be possible to tell what the project is trying to do, not just what it is made of.

---

## Step 6: Make a rough prototype

Make a quick, low-stakes version.

Use whatever is available:

- paper
- tape
- string
- foil
- cardboard
- markers
- printouts
- LEDs
- thread
- found objects
- packaging
- audio recording
- slideshow
- quick code test
- rough performance notes

This is not the final version. It is a way of thinking with materials.

Your prototype should help answer:

- What is it?
- What does it use?
- What does the viewer encounter?
- What still feels unresolved?

### What strong work looks like
Strong work here takes a real risk and makes something tangible, even if it is rough or incomplete.

---

## Step 7: Reflect and revise

After making the prototype, write a short reflection.

You can use these prompts:

- What surprised me?
- What worked better than expected?
- What felt flat, too obvious, or too vague?
- What did the materials force me to change?
- What feels more alive now?
- What should become clearer, stranger, larger, smaller, or more focused?

### What strong work looks like
Strong work here is thoughtful and specific. It shows that the prototype taught you something.

---

## If you need to brain storm with me

Bring the following to talk:

- your gathered source materials
- your project lens sentence
- your three combinations
- your three possible forms
- your rough prototype
- your short reflection

From there, we will shape the next version together.

---

## If you feel stuck

Start here:

1. Pick one math item, one text item, and one physical or visual item.
2. Put them side by side.
3. Write what each one does to you.
4. Ask what they become together.
5. Make a rough object, image, map, or experiment from that.

That is enough to begin.

---

## Holistic grading guide

This project will be graded **holistically**, which means I will look at the work as a whole rather than score it as a checklist of separate parts.

In general, stronger projects will show:

- serious engagement with course materials
- evidence of real experimentation
- a clear project direction, even if it is still developing
- thoughtful connections between math, art, text, and material practice
- willingness to take risks, revise, and respond to what the work does back to you
- care in presentation and follow-through

A project does **not** need to be polished, expensive, or technically perfect in order to do well. What matters most is whether it shows genuine thinking, making, and development.


## Final reminder

Your final project does not need to explain math in the usual classroom way.  
It does need to show that you have been thinking with the materials of this course and making something deliberate out of that encounter.
