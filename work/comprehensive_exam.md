### An Introduction to the Questions that Guide my Sharing

When I first began trying to write for academic audiences, to fit all my  
thoughts into twenty-five linear pages of careful citations that moved  
with military precision into the field of battle, where I must fight to  
prove I can make sense (not to my immediate colleagues and mentors, but  
to the academic world at large), I struggled with the enormity of the  
field, the sheer volume of words and ideas and becomings and  
connections. I could see them all, all at once, always. In the first  
chapter of Jane Bennett's Vibrant Matter, she asks how to help people  
learn to see the vibrancy of matter, to open themselves up to the  
agential swarm, the primordial swerve, to notice, to linger in the world  
of material becomings. I was struck and annoyed, because I already live  
in the world of vibrancy and frequently I am exhausted with people who  
don't see as I see, because they frequently have no idea what I am  
going on about, like maybe there is a giant rabbit behind my shoulder  
whispering into my ear (or at least they think that I think there is a  
giant rabbit behind my shoulder whispering into my ear).

After months of wrangling and too many to count false starts in  
organizing my thoughts so they made sense in twenty five linear pages, I  
realized the audience I care most about making sense to is the audience  
I should be writing for, and then I had a vision, a lens, a path that  
was clear. I would write to my students, for my students,  
past/present/future students. The classroom for me, for the past  
twenty-two-ish years (and maybe before that when my hat leaned towards  
student), is like an art gallery (my experience/perception/invention of  
an art gallery). My favorite thing about making art and showing my art  
is when my work, my thought babies, get to go live with other people's  
thought babies and have conversations and make new thought babies.  
Sending my art out to play with other art is like watching my own  
children on the playground exploring their surroundings, testing out  
their limits, timidly for some of my children, and boldly for others,  
making gestures towards the other children, birds, trees, rocks, grass,  
babies bunnies just born under the mulch that the kind grounds keeper  
surrounded with temporary fencing to keep them safe until the mommy  
bunny could move them. Because of the nature of the classroom, or at  
least my classroom, the humans that entered the space, physical or  
metaphysical, brought their own art, their own thought babies. And it  
was my greatest joy and honor to share my thoughts with them and for  
them to bless me by sharing their own. So when I write that I write for  
my students, it is with the same eagerness to show my new friend the  
beautiful caterpillar, the roly poly just unfurled after I dug it from  
the mud under the rock, or to co-create/participate/feel/make the  
connection, see the interrelatedness of all things shimmer and glow and  
our hearts and our brains light up with the happy chemicals of  
connection.

reminded that in the beginning as researchers we lay the foundation,

## Diffracting Theory/Method/Practice

Posthumanism and new materialism are not two names for the same project, nor are they competing frameworks that happen to share vocabulary. They are two distinct critical traditions that developed in opposition to the same historical, cultural, and economic conditions: the Cartesian-Newtonian settlement that rendered matter inert, Enlightenment humanism that installed the sovereign rational subject at the center of knowledge and value, and the industrial and postindustrial orders that depended on both. Posthumanism took aim at what those conditions did to the subject: the figure of "Man," the human universal, the species hierarchy that placed one version of the human above all other forms of life and matter. New materialism took aim at what those conditions did to matter itself: the reduction of the material world to passive substance, available for inscription, extraction, and instrumentalization by an external force. Because they were targeting different aspects of the same formation, they arrived at compatible ontologies from different directions: and because the formation they were opposing was a unity, their critiques converge. That convergence is not coincidence. It is what makes them genuinely twinned frameworks rather than adjacent ones.

The question is answered not by forcing a clean taxonomy --- which would betray what both frameworks insist about the world --- but by tracing the different emphases, genealogies, and methodological centers of gravity that make the two frameworks distinguishable even as they remain entangled. That distinction matters because it determines what kind of lens Part Two and Part Three can offer: the nuance of what each framework contributes to practice depends on being able to say, while approaching specificity, what each one is and what it is for.

For ease of reference, I use **posthumanism (PH)** and **new materialism (NM)** in abbreviated form only in passages where repeated comparison makes the full terms cumbersome. In major claims and transitions, I retain the full terms for readability.

To tease them apart is to follow the diffractive practices we will later discuss, to search for the differences that make a difference (Barad, 2007, citing Bateson, 1972; developed further in [link to Deleuze Called for a Diffractive Practice]). We begin (now it is we because the reader is also participating. As my thoughts unfold/refold so does the reader's and a thought-baby, an entanglement is made) with recognition that all things are co-constituted together through the continuous becomings of the protean flow, zoe, the primordial swerve, the entanglement, the void, the virtual, the immanence, the Wild, non-identity, a life, the absolute, and ever shifting assemblages both cosmic and atomic and everywhere, everything, all at once. These are not synonyms in the strict sense --- each carries the specific weight of the theoretical tradition from which it comes. But they are pointing at the same thing, or rather, at the same absence of a thing, not nothing, not chaos, the deviation from the straight line that generates all actual things, the condition of possibility for all swerving/becoming. These notions form the ground and set the tone, revealing themselves more fully in the sections that follow, and again in Part Two [link], where specific terms are grounded in their theoretical traditions.

The point of this teasing apart is to find a place and time to stand, to stake a position (metaphorical or literal) in the entanglement from which to explore/ponder/make sense (a gathering of diffractions coalesced into an accessible whole --- not too large, not too small) and to most importantly connect to other travelers in the protean flow. Without a position, how would they find you? How would you find them? See Henri Matisse's series of paintings and cut paper compositions from early 1900's Paris, that for many viewers evoke a feeling of human connection in the protean flow. [[Dance I]{.underline}](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/79124)

According to Ferrando (2014, p. 169), new materialism has its own origin story unique from posthumanism, but by the end of the 1990s it had emerged as a specific branch of the posthuman field, and the two now function as necessarily twinned frames through which to navigate the world (Ibrisim, 2020, p. 11, citing Ferrando, 2013, p. 30). They are emerging continuums of difference, co-constituted, and their combined momentum pushes toward new ethics of the what/who/where/when/why counts as the real, counts as agency, and counts as knowledge.

Posthumanism and new materialism share a non-dualistic, non-representationalist, entangled understanding of nature and culture --- a vitalist understanding of matter and an empirical field that no longer takes human exceptionalism as a point of departure (Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 191, citing Coole & Frost, 2010; Bennett, 2010; Braidotti, 2013). Philosophically, posthumanism and the new materialisms presuppose that knowing and being are inseparable: "epistemological practices are necessarily ontological practices; to know is to be part of the world's continual construction" (Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 192, citing Barad, 2007, p. 379). Method must therefore be grounded in ontology. As Wolfe insists, what is needed is "the recognition that 'any notion of the posthuman that is to be more than merely an extension of the human...must be premised upon a mutation that is ongoing and immanent,'" and this means that to become posthuman means to participate in --- and find a mode of thought adequate to --- "'processes which can never be entirely reduced to patterns or standards, codes or information'" (Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii, citing Rutsky). This is the logic Clarke names when quoting Gregory Bateson's suggestion that "'the whole of logic would have to be reconstructed for recursiveness'" (Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii, citing Clarke, citing Bateson, 1972): if the world is recursive, relational, and non-deterministic, then method cannot remain representational, as if it were a neutral mirror standing outside what it studies.

## The Transversality of Posthumanism and New Materialism

Both posthumanism and new materialism are transversal frameworks : they travel across disciplines, paradigms, and institutional boundaries because they enact their own claim of the co-constitution and interrelatedness of all things. Siffrinn and Coda (2025, p. 198) name what enters any field that takes these frameworks seriously: they carry "ontological and epistemological commitments that are distinct from those in other traditions" and demand a rethinking of the field's "most taken-for-granted constructs." Van der Tuin and Dolphijn make the same point structurally: NM "does not add something to thought... It rather traverses and thereby rewrites thinking as a whole, leaving nothing untouched, redirecting every possible idea according to its new sense of orientation" (Dolphijn & van der Tuin, 2012, p. 13). Each theory's respective origins confirm this reach.

Posthumanism can apply to "any field of enquiry, starting from our location as a species, to the individual gaze" (Ferrando, 2014, p. 168). Posthumanism "draws on many different sources, histories and herstories, in an academic attempt of inclusiveness that opens to other species and hypothetical life forms: from non-human animals to artificial intelligence, from aliens to the possibilities related to the physic notions of a multiverse" (Ferrando, 2016, p. 3). In a single volume, Wolfe demonstrates this reach by putting posthumanism to work in animal studies, disability studies, bioethics, literary and cultural studies, architecture, systems theory, and poetry (Wolfe, 2010). Posthumanism's feminist genealogy also runs through avant-garde art movements, performance art, Cyberfeminism, bioart, Afrofuturism and Chicanafuturism across the twentieth century, well before it acquired its theoretical name (Ferrando, 2016).

New materialism's transversality was named and theorized from its first formulations. Félix Guattari coined the term transversality in 1964 as "a means to search for the new : not by critiquing the old, but by radically questioning (or smoothening out) all the barriers that supported its logic" (Guattari, [1964] 1984, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 101). New materialism "has sprung up from various fields including feminism, such as philosophy, science studies, and cultural theory traversing and being intercrossed with both natural sciences and the humanities" (Ibrisim, 2020, p. 11, citing Sencindiver, 2017). Van der Tuin and Dolphijn identify the strength of new materialism as precisely "this nomadic traversing of the territories of science and the humanities, performing the agential or non-innocent nature of all matter" (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 101). That traversal "cuts across or intersects academic (neo-)disciplines (for instance, feminist theory, science and technology studies, and media and cultural studies), paradigms (for instance, the Saussurian/Lacanian linguisticism still prevalent in cultural theory today) and the linear spatiotemporalities conventionally assigned to epistemic trends" (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2010, pp. 158–159). Braidotti names the requirement as a "qualitative leap" towards "creating conditions for the implementation of transversality" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 123, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 100). The empirical reach is documented by Alaimo and Hekman, who find material feminism : one of NM's constitutive strands : operating across "science studies, environmental feminisms, corporeal feminisms, queer theory, disability studies, theories of race and ethnicity, environmental justice, (post-)Marxist feminism, globalization studies, and cultural studies" (Alaimo & Hekman, 2008, pp. 9–10, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 103).

## Origin Stories

Reality is an ongoing becoming. Matter is agentive, relational, and generative : not a passive substance awaiting inscription by an external force. The world is not composed of discrete objects standing in external relations but of entangled phenomena, intra-acting assemblages, folds. Knowledge is not produced by a detached observer standing outside what it studies: it is situated, embodied, produced through the body of the knower and through the specific material-discursive apparatus of the inquiry. The subject is not a bounded individual but a nomadic, non-unitary, multiply-constituted process of becoming : "not one but many" (Ferrando, 2014, p. 169), or as Bennett would say, "walking talking minerals", not one body but an array of bodies (lookup) (Vibrant Matter) : "we have never been human" because "human is a 'human' concept" (Ferrando, 2014, p. 170). The ground of knowledge is relationship. The unit of analysis is phenomenon, assemblage, cartography. Representation is not a mirror of a pre-given world but a cut that produces what it appears merely to describe. (citation?)

These are the affirmations posthumanism and new materialism share. They are not new. They have been made before : in philosophy, in science, in art, in feminist practice, in Indigenous ways of knowing that long predate the Western academic traditions that have most systematically suppressed them. What is new is the moment when enough of these traditions found common vocabulary at once to name what they were doing, to make it speakable as method. That moment was the 1990s. But the conditions for it accumulated across millennia : not as a linear progression toward a predetermined destination, but as a folding, a series of intra-actions in which the same ontological commitment kept resurfacing in different registers, kept being covered over, and kept breaking through.

The problem these frameworks respond to is as old as Leucippus and Democritus: the atomist claim that reality consists of tiny, indivisible, passive particles careening through the void, their shapes and sizes fixed and eternal, their combinations determined by random collision. Ancient atomism gave Western metaphysics its picture of matter as fundamentally inert : moving only when struck, generating only what its pre-given characteristics permit, never surprising itself. Epicurus introduced the clinamen, the swerve, as a small concession to spontaneity: atoms occasionally deviate from their predetermined paths, triggering cascades of changed outcomes. But the deviation is still individual and random, not relational and generative. Lucretius extended Epicurus into the philosophical poem De Rerum Natura and came closer : Gamble, Hanan, and Nail argue that Lucretius anticipated performative new materialism, that he studied matter as something that ceaselessly constitutes novel entities and relations (Gamble et al., 2019, pp. 112–113). But this reading was suppressed for most of Western intellectual history, both by the mechanist tradition that followed and by the Christian theology that preceded it, which needed matter to be dead so that God could be its animator (Ferrando, 2014).

The modern consolidation of that suppression runs through Bacon, Descartes, and Hobbes. Bacon synthesized theology, naturalism, and mechanism into a single framework: God imparts force to matter, sets the laws of nature in motion, and then rests. "The force implanted by God in these first particles makes up all the variety of things" (Bacon, cited in Gamble et al., 2019, p. 115). Matter is what is moved. Force is what moves it. The observer is what understands both. Descartes sharpened this into a hard dualism: mind and matter are categorically different substances, and the mind examines the material world from a position of categorical separation from it. Kant later secured this structure epistemologically: we only ever have access to the correlation between thinking and being, never to either term considered apart from the other. The human subject is the transcendental condition of knowledge. Van der Tuin and Dolphijn name the consequence precisely: "Kant started his philosophy with the human being : or even better, with human thought and its relation to nature. In other words, Kant turned (theological) metaphysics into transcendental anthropology" (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 165). The anthropological turn was a humanist revolution (citation?) : and Foucault could already see, from within it, that it was contingent: "man is an invention of recent date. And one perhaps nearing its end" (Wolfe, 2010, p. xii, citing Foucault). Posthumanism asks what thought looks like once the figure of Man is no longer its organizing center.

Against this tradition : not in direct opposition to it but as an alternative that kept being marginalized : Spinoza had already made the counter-claim in 1677: the mind is the idea of the body. Mind and body are the same thing. God equals nature. What Spinoza calls conatus : the striving of all singular things to persevere in their being : is not something added to matter from outside but immanent to it, the power of all material things to exist and act. "Bodily motions alone" is the starting point (Spinoza, [1677] 2001, E2P49 Schol., cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012). Deleuze later names Spinoza's project "a philosophy of the body" and identifies him as the founding figure of the monist minor tradition (citation?): the tradition Deleuze spent his career recovering, tracing through Leibniz, Nietzsche, Bergson, Whitehead, and Lucretius (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, pp. 95–96). This minor tradition never disappeared. It was, in van der Tuin and Dolphijn's phrase, "freeing itself from the Platonist, Christian, and Modernist rule under which it suffered for so long" (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 95). New materialism is what that freeing looks like when it becomes speakable as method.

Bergson extended the Spinozist commitment into a philosophy of duration. Matter, for Bergson, is not substance but "the aggregate of images" : and perception of matter is "these same images referred to the eventual action of one particular image, my body" ([1896] 2004, p. 8, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012). This is matter given in relation to a body that is itself material, not from a detached point of view. What endures is not Platonic essence but what "diverges and transforms itself with the passage of time" (Grosz, 2005, p. 110, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2010, p. 164). Duration inserted into matter: this is the first articulation of what NM will call the ongoing iterative process, the generative indeterminacy that is not random but more akin to Brownian motion. Merleau-Ponty extended it again: the body is itself a fold, the lived experience of Leibnizean folded ontology : not binary opposition between two fixed terms but a reversibility that always miscarries at the last moment (Coole, 2010, pp. 110–111). His working notes explicitly planned to substitute being-in-the-world for Leibniz's God, "only ridding them of their substantialist and onto-theological elaborations in order to apply them to wild Being" (cited in Coole, 2010, p. 110). The body does not perceive the world from a detached point of view. It does not inhabit a flat, geometrical, fully determinate plane but a milieu : an Umwelt that remains ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant with expressive significance. Cézanne tried to paint this: the object "in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes" (Coole, 2010, p. 104). Merleau-Ponty reads Cézanne's refusal of Renaissance perspective as an aesthetic choice with a phenomenological position : the attempt to suspend cultural habit long enough to see the material world as it actually materializes in perception. The postwar moment introduced a different kind of disruption to the Cartesian observer : not from phenomenology but from physics and systems. The Macy Conferences on cybernetics (1946–1953) gathered Bateson, McCulloch, Wiener, von Neumann, and others (Shannon, physicists?) around a new theoretical model for biological, mechanical, and communicational processes that removed Homo sapiens from any particularly privileged position in relation to meaning, information, and cognition (Wolfe, 2010, p. xii). Bateson's reformulation was characteristically precise: information is "a difference that makes a difference" (1972, p. 459) : a relational definition that makes no reference to a subject who receives it. Wolfe identifies this as the origin point of the posthumanist tradition: the cybernetic moment when the systems that process meaning, pattern, and difference were no longer recognizably human at their core. In quantum physics, Bohr had arrived at the same conclusion from a different direction. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle held that measurement disturbs the particle : a limit on knowledge. Bohr argued this was an epistemic error: the issue is not uncertainty but indeterminacy. "Things are indeterminate; there are no things before the measurement, and the very act of measurement produces determinate boundaries and properties of things" (Barad, in Dolphijn & van der Tuin, 2012, p. 63). Observation is not disturbance of a pre-existing state. It is constitution of the phenomenon. This is not a claim about human psychology or perceptual limitation. It is an ontological claim about the nature of reality.

Feminist science studies arrived at the same claim through a different route, the body. Adrienne Rich named the methodological principle directly in 1984: "Begin not with a continent or a country or a house, but with the geography closest in : the body" (Blood, Bread, and Poetry, p. 212). Braidotti names this "the cornerstone of the neo-materialist feminist project" (Braidotti, New Materialism, p. 22), tracing a direct line from Rich's politics of location through Haraway's situated knowledges into the standpoint debates that shaped NM's early formation. Bennett, tracing the intellectual lineage of Vibrant Matter, identifies "feminist studies of the body" as the larger trend that put "desire and bodily practices back on the ethical radar screen" : the precondition for her own move toward nonhuman vitality (Vibrant Matter, p. xi). Rich's formulation was an onto-epistemological claim : that situatedness is not contamination but the only honest starting place.

> Patriarchal thought has limited female biology to its own narrow specifications. The feminist vision has recoiled from female biology for these reasons; it will, I believe, come to view our physicality as a resource, rather than a destiny. In order to live a fully human life we require not only control of our bodies (though control is a prerequisite); we must touch the unity and resonance of our physicality, our bond with the natural order, the corporeal ground of our intelligence.

Haraway radicalized the epistemological stakes in "Situated Knowledges" (1988): the "god trick" : the view from nowhere, the claim to disembodied universal perspective : is not a neutral description of scientific method but a specific exercise of power, and the alternative is not relativism but a stronger objectivity, one that is partial, embodied, and accountable to its own situatedness. "I am arguing for politics and epistemologies of location, positioning, and situating, where partiality and not universality is the condition of being heard to make rational knowledge claims" (Haraway, 1988, p. 589).

Trinh T. Minh-ha made the same commitment in film practice, working frequently from hybrid sites such as Vietnamese, African, and American crossings. In "Documentary Is/Not a Name" (1990), she names the condition that makes non-totalizing knowledge possible: "meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown," and what persists between meaning and truth is "the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). The interval is the condition of possibility for a knowledge that does not replicate the epistemic violence of the view from nowhere. Two years later, in "The Promises of Monsters" (1992), Haraway introduced diffraction as a critical method in direct engagement with Trinh's formulation of "critical difference within" (Haraway, 1992, pp. 299–300). The genealogy of diffraction begins with a filmmaker, not a physicist. It begins with the refusal of the view from nowhere enacted in form : in the structure of the film : before Barad gave that refusal a quantum-physical name.

Barad's Meeting the Universe Halfway (2007) is where these threads : the ontological, the epistemological, the feminist, the phenomenological, the quantum : are explicitly and systematically braided into method. Agential realism names what this entire genealogy was already doing: the refusal to accept that entities exist prior to the intra-actions that constitute them, the insistence that knowing and being are not separable, the recognition that each cut that produces determinate phenomena simultaneously enacts an exclusion that carries ethical weight. Timeto (2011) traces this as a single continuous argument with three stations that build on each other: Hayles's constrained constructivism establishes that representations are neither mirrors of reality nor arbitrary constructions : they are consistent or inconsistent with the flux, where consistency means local, interactive, instrumentally efficacious engagement. Haraway's situated knowledges extend this by insisting that the partiality of the perspective is not a weakness but the condition of accountability : the partial perspective names where it sees from, and that naming is what makes it answerable. Barad's agential realism takes the final step: it is not only the knowing subject that is situated but the phenomenon itself that is produced through the intra-action of apparatus and matter. Representation is not removed from an originary source : it is the cut that produces the things it appears merely to describe.

The consolidation of the 1990s did not happen in a vacuum. Posthumanism came along within and after postmodernism, generated out of the radical deconstruction of the "human" that began as a philosophical and political project in the late 1960s and turned into an epistemological one in the 1990s (Ferrando, 2013 [thesis], p. 25). In doing so, postmodernism cleared essential ground for what followed: the critique of the "god trick," the refusal of master narratives, the insistence that knowledge is always situated and partial. But postmodernism's annihilation of the dualism nature/culture resulted in a clear preference for the nurtural aspects of it: a wave of constructivist and representationalist radicalization that "somehow lost track of the material" (Ferrando, 2013 [thesis], p. 114). The consequence, as Barad states directly, was that "language matters, discourse matters, culture matters": but "the only thing that does not seem to matter anymore is matter" (Barad, 2003, p. 801, cited in Ferrando, 2013 [thesis], p. 114). New materialisms arose as a reaction to precisely this: not by reinstating nature against culture, but by refusing that dualism entirely: perceiving matter as an ongoing process of materialization that is "dynamic, shifting, inherently entangled, diffractional and performative" (Ferrando, 2013 [thesis], p. 114). Posthumanism inherits postmodernism's deconstruction of the sovereign subject; new materialism corrects postmodernism's disappearance of matter. Together they complete the move postmodernism prepared but did not make. [Amy to supply any additional citations]

The term "new materialism" may have been coined in the 1990s, but the theorists who built it consistently locate its epistemological foundations upstream. What the 1990s coinage names is not a new condition but a consolidation : the moment when these traditions found common vocabulary across disciplines (Ibrisim, 2020, p. 11; van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 13). Not a convergence at a point but a fold in which what had always been folded together became legible as such. Systems of thought that refuse the human/nonhuman divide and insist on the agency and vitality of the material world have long informed Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and valuing (Pennycook, 2024), as well as Spinoza's monism and the idea of multitude as the agent of potentia (Torosyan, 2021, p. 55). The origin story does not begin with DeLanda and Braidotti in the 1990s. It begins wherever someone refused the inherited story and dove toward the thing itself.

*Adrienne Rich named that refusal in 1973. In "Diving into the Wreck," she describes descending not to recover the myth of the wreck but the wreck itself : "the thing I came for: / the wreck and not the story of the wreck / the thing itself and not the myth" (Adrienne Rich's Poetry and Prose, ed. Gelpi and Gelpi, p. 67). This is the epistemological stance the entire genealogy requires: the refusal to accept the inherited story : the dualist framework, the representation standing in for the thing, the concept exhausting the object : and the insistence on returning to the material itself, to what was there before the mythology overlaid it.* Rich was practicing this in poetry two decades before new materialism named it as theory.

The standard account of new materialism's origins runs through feminist science studies (Haraway, Barad), political theory (Bennett, DeLanda), and philosophy (Braidotti, Grosz). This account is accurate but partial. Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) argue that studies of art were not merely retrospective recipients of new materialist theory but "one of the very milieus in which the call to rethink the involvement of matter in human practices and in the more-than-human world arose" (Kontturi and Tiainen, 2021, p. 259): emerging through painting practice, music scholarship, performance studies, and practice-led research in the 1990s and 2000s, in parallel with, and sometimes prior to, the consolidation of NM as a named theoretical position. Yet the standard origin story assigns art a subsidiary role: artworks appear on the covers of foundational NM texts (Cornelia Parker's suspended bricks on Bennett's Vibrant Matter; Thomas Demand's images on Barad's Meeting the Universe Halfway), evocatively illustrating theoretical propositions rather than being recognized as sites where those propositions were first worked out. The consequence, as Kontturi and Tiainen note, is that art theorists are frequently invoked as evidence of NM claims while remaining outside the mainstream discussion currently centered on Barad. For a comps document arguing for new materialist art practice as research, this gap in the standard genealogy is not incidental. It is the gap the document exists to close.

## Critique, Counter-Tradition, and the Question of Matter

The philosophical distinction between posthumanism and new materialism follows directly from their different critical inheritances. Posthumanism's primary target is the sovereign human subject: the humanist "Man" whose rational autonomy was used to authorize species hierarchy, colonial violence, and the exclusion of all that was coded as body, nature, or other. Its philosophical energy concentrates on subjectivity, power, and the deconstruction of that figure: what it suppressed, what it cost, and what becomes possible when it is displaced. New materialism's primary target is the status of matter: the Cartesian reduction of the material world to inert, passive substance waiting to be acted upon by an external force, whether divine, human, or mechanical. Its philosophical energy concentrates on matter's own agency, vitality, and generativity: what matter does when it is not being held still by a framework that needs it passive. These are distinguishable philosophical projects. But they are not incompatible ones, because decentering the human subject and animating matter are two moves in the same direction. You cannot fully decenter the human subject without granting agency to what the human subject was defined against. You cannot fully animate matter without displacing the sovereign observer who was needed to stand outside it. Each critique, followed to its conclusion, requires the other.

Posthumanism tends to begin from a critique of the figure of "Man" (the humanist universal), and it concentrates methodological energy on subjectivity, power, and ethical/political reconfiguration in "posthuman times." Braidotti's "posthuman predicament," for example, explicitly couples posthumanism (critique of "Man") with post-anthropocentrism (critique of species hierarchy and human exceptionalism), and treats the subject as non-unitary, embedded, and in-process.

New materialism tends to begin from a critique of matter as inert and of knowing as representation. It concentrates methodological energy on material agency, assemblage, distributed causality, and nonhuman participation in public life. For Jane Bennett, "vital materialism" is explicitly a worldview of vibrant matter and "always-becoming," and diffractive practice becomes the perceptual-ethical labor required to be faithful to that reality: training perception toward nonhuman vitality, distributed agency, and non-binary emergent causality.

Understanding what new materialism is positioning itself against requires a brief sketch of what it replaces. The dominant modern account of matter: Cartesian-Newtonian in its inheritance: treats matter as passive, inert stuff to be acted upon by an external force, whether that force is the human will, divine providence, or the laws of physics imposed from without. As Coole and Frost describe it, this framework "yields a conceptual and practical domination of nature as well as a specifically modern attitude or ethos of subjectivist potency" (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 9): the observer classifies matter from a distance, aided by theories that enable the manipulation and reconfiguration of matter on an unprecedented scale. The human subject is the agent; matter is the object of that agency. New materialism defines itself precisely as an alternative to this picture: not by reversing its terms, but by refusing the distinction that makes it possible.

Coole & Frost position the posthumanist ontological reorientation similarly as a response to contemporary conditions, where we must "reorient ourselves profoundly in relation to the world, to one another, and to ourselves," naming what is at stake as "nothing less than a challenge to some of the most basic assumptions that have underpinned the modern world including its normative sense of the human and its beliefs about human agency" (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 6). The challenge extends to "material objects and the natural environment": to how we labor on, exploit, and relate to the nonhuman world. Crucially, Coole and Frost insist that calling this turn toward new materialisms does not deny "their rich materialist heritage": many contributors to the project draw inspiration from materialist traditions that long predate the 1990s coinage. The newness is not in the recognition of material entanglement, but in the convergence of tools and vocabularies that traverse the same disciplines.

Bennett (2010) provides an exhaustive, yet not exclusive, accounting of vitalist and materialist movements that predated her own vital materialism. In philosophy and metaphysics, materialism is a form of monism holding that matter is the fundamental substance of nature, so that all things, including mind and consciousness, arise from material interactions and depend on physical processes, including those of the human brain and nervous system (Wikipedia, 2026).

Bennett elaborates on the long view of time and provides a proof from the first single cell organisms to the evolution of homosapiens: we are all made of the same thing, we are boundaryless, and thus humans are walking, talking minerals. If we are all made of the same things, and humans have agency, and humans are things, then things have agency. And vice versa, by inscribing things with thing-power we also elevate the agency of humans as things with more power in the ethical configurations and reconfigurations of reality than humans believe they have. Because Barad grounds herself at the atomic level, we can imagine that we are all permeable and things moving freely through each other at the level of electrons, protons, quarks, and other members of the particle zoo. (add array of things)

The ontological commitments of new materialism have roots in a vitalist tradition whose scope extended well beyond biology. As Coole and Frost trace in their introduction to New Materialisms, critical vitalism posited a generative principle responsible not only for morphological development in organisms but for "the progressive development of personality and history": an isomorphism between physical and psychological orders, matter and meaning, that the dominant mechanist tradition systematically suppressed (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 49). Matter as intelligent, self-organizing, and generative was repeatedly displaced by matter as passive, inert, and awaiting inscription by an external force. The ethico-onto-epistemological insistence of Barad, Bennett, and Braidotti is, among other things, a refusal of that displacement: an insistence that matter is "always already an ongoing historicity" (Barad, 2003, p. 822) and that any ethics adequate to this reality must take the vitality of matter seriously, not as metaphor, but as condition.

Srnicek (2017) groups posthumanism, speculative realism, and object-oriented philosophy as subgroup variants of new materialism, though posthumanism is highlighted as introducing concepts of agency, structure, and epistemology to the umbrella grouping. Smelik (2018), in contrast, situates posthumanism as the context for new materialism, as posthumanism introduced the explicit decentering of the human which made room for things and nature to also have agency. Using Bennett's term, posthumanism horizontalized and flattened the field.

While all posthumanist scholars have moved beyond the modernist reification of Man as uniquely special in the world, Wolfe (2010) reminds us that some scholars still hold on to the notion that man is still special, just less special than we previously believed. Srnicek (2017) notes that some posthumanist approaches maintain important distinctions between human and nonhuman systems, such as the human capacity for self-reflection and intentional action. De Liaño & Fernández-Götz (2021) argue some New Humanism elements may remain valuable for current societal challenges; humanist perspectives need not be wholly rejected. Blok (2024) argues for a lingering human exceptionality by rejecting the idea that conativity and responsivity are shared symmetrically by all material entities; Blok argues instead that some entities are conative (e.g., rocks, sand dunes) but not responsive, limiting hybridisation. This reluctance to completely decenter the human points to the difficulty of heeding the ethico-onto-epistemological call to decenter the self as an individual.

The posthuman framework of new materialism proposes, and this document would assert requires, an entirely non-anthropocentric view, removing the human subject completely from the center and instead focusing on complex assemblages where humans and things interconnect (Smelik, 2018). New materialisms add to posthumanism the adoption of a monist, Spinozian conception of matter (Abadia, 2018). Braidotti (2019) establishes that the critical posthumanities are conceptually founded on a neo-Spinozist monistic ontology that assumes radical immanence and the primacy of intelligent and self-organizing matter. Posthumanism views human life as a decentralized human-technology relation, alliance, or network, rejecting traditional dichotomies between humans and machines and aiming for total symmetry (Blok, 2024).

Not all new materialisms are posthumanist in the sense this document uses that term. Gamble, Hanan, and Rossdale (2019) identify three distinct strands within the broader new materialist field: vital materialism (Bennett, drawing on Deleuze's reading of Spinoza and Leibniz), which treats matter as inherently vibrant and agential; negative materialism, which includes speculative realism and object-oriented ontology (Harman, Meillassoux, Morton), approaches that treat matter as non-relational to thought, with thought having emerged ex nihilo to humans alone; and performative materialism (Barad, Kirby), which centers the intra-active and entangled transformation of observer and observed, where epistemology and ontology are not separate but symbiotic intra-actions. This document draws on vital and performative materialisms and explicitly excludes the negative strand. Speculative realism and OOO reinstall a separation between matter and thought that is the very condition posthumanism and new materialism were built to refuse. The two strands this document uses share the posthumanist ontological commitment to co-constitution: matter is not inert, the human is not sovereign, and the knowing subject is part of what is known. Barad's posthumanist performativity is the precise hinge where these two strands converge: it is, as Militsi (2021) summarizes from Barad (2003, p. 808), a "diffractive elaboration" on Butler's and Haraway's contributions, simultaneously completing posthumanism's material decentering of the human and grounding new materialism's account of matter as co-constitutive rather than merely acted upon.

## The Mechanism of Suppression: How Entanglement Keeps Getting Bracketed Out

The entanglement described above is not a recent insight. It was present in the earliest strata of western thought: in Heraclitus, in the pre-Socratics, in thinkers who were willing to sit with the complexity and flux of the world before a more convenient framework was institutionalized over them. The pattern that follows is consistent across centuries: wherever a thinker pointed toward interrelatedness, indeterminacy, or the porousness of boundaries, the Platonic inheritance arrived to domesticate the insight: extracting the useful notation, stripping the relational ontology, and handing back a binary. This is not incidental intellectual history. Posthumanism and new materialism resubstantiate entanglement, bringing to the fore what kept getting suppressed: grounding theory, method, and practice in the reality that everything is interconnected and always becoming, a configuration that prior counter-movements, however vigorous, had not yet assembled in the specific register of the post-human and vital materialism together (Ferrando, 2019, p. 170; see also van der Tuin and Dolphijn, 2010, p. 95; Haraway, 2016 [page TK]). Ferrando traces these proclivities back to Aristotle, Plato, and the Old Testament, where the world was separated into hierarchies of the ideal and the profane.

The retreat did not end with Plato. It became a repeating pattern, and the examples that follow are not exhaustive: they are chosen because they trace the binary's dominance through domains directly relevant to the concerns of this project: computation, information theory, digital logic, and the ontological assumptions embedded in the tools that now mediate most knowledge work. Thinkers whose work pointed toward entanglement, relation, and indeterminacy get misread or domesticated into the dualist framework that followed them. Leibniz's binary arithmetic: originally a cosmological meditation on generativity, creation from nothing, the infinite combinations arising from two starting conditions: was extracted from its relational context and formalized by Boole into a logic of true/false, yes/no, on/off. The mystical generativity got stripped; the binary stayed. That stripping became the foundation of digital computing, as though Leibniz had meant to say: reality is made of two things. He meant almost the opposite. Shannon's information theory compounds the irony: his core insight was that noise: disorder, uncertainty, the unpredictable: carries the most information. A completely predictable signal carries none. But the computer science built on his foundations embraced boolean either/or and suppressed the noise. And underneath every boolean bit is an analog signal: a continuous voltage: interpreted as on or off by applying an arbitrary threshold. The digital is a reading convention imposed on an analog reality. The entanglement was always there; it kept getting bracketed out. (The connection is not accidental: Leibniz sent his binary system to the Jesuit missionary Joachim Bouvet in 1703, reading it through the I Ching: the relational logic was always in dialogue with non-Western thought before Boole stripped it to boolean arithmetic.)

What Boole's formalization stripped from Leibniz was not incidental surplus but the ontological core of his thought. Coole, following Merleau-Ponty and Deleuze, traces a Leibnizean inheritance that runs directly counter to the binary logic his notation was misread to authorize. For Leibniz, the world is constituted not by discrete units standing in external relations but by folds: matter as endlessly folding and unfolding, generating provisional forms as styles of existing rather than fixed substances. There are no voids in Leibnizean matter: "folds replace holes," and Leibnizean matter offers, as Deleuze glosses him, "an infinitely porous, spongy, or cavernous texture without emptiness," with each body elastic, "pierced with irregular passages," dependent for its coherence on the pressure of external forces (Coole, 2010, p. 111). Deleuze, writing on Leibniz in The Fold, notes that the exemplary science of the fold is not Euclidean geometry but origami: and finds anticipations of Leibnizean folding among the pre-Socratics, as well as within postclassical physics: in Merleau-Ponty's Nature lectures, in Whitehead, and: most strikingly: in the folded proteins of microbiology (Coole, 2010, p. 110). For Merleau-Ponty: who in his working notes explicitly planned to substitute being-in-the-world for Leibniz's God while maintaining "entirely" certain of Leibniz's descriptions, "only ridding them of their substantialist and onto-theological elaborations in order to apply them to wild Being": the body is itself a fold: the lived experience of precisely this Leibnizean folded ontology, not binary opposition between two fixed terms, but a reversibility that "always miscarries at the last moment," producing the productive dehiscence, the difference between identicals, that makes emergence possible rather than only inert repetition (Coole, 2010, p. 107). What the Baroque artists understood, and what Boole stripped out, is that this fold is not a theoretical position but a perceptual and material fact: present in how the body inhabits the world, in how matter coils and hollows and wraps, in how Baroque chiaroscuro renders "clarity endlessly plunging into obscurity" rather than separating light from dark as in Cartesian optics (Coole, 2010, p. 111). Deleuze observes that Descartes tried to move through the labyrinth of matter along rectilinear tracks and failed to grasp its curvature; the Baroque art of Caravaggio and Tintoretto: where contours are effaced, where light vibrates in the folds of matter, where draperies and tessellated fabrics and complex harmonics with multiple rhythms make philosophies that "resolve Cartesian distinctions of mind and matter through physical means": these are not decorative flourishes but ontological propositions enacted in form (Coole, 2010, pp. 111-112). The binary was always a misreading of a thinker who meant something closer to the opposite.

Quantum physics follows the same pattern. Bohr's core claim: that observation and observed are inseparable, that there is no view from outside the phenomenon: was both philosophically and politically threatening. The entanglement that quantum theory places at the foundation of physical reality was domesticated: the equations were kept, the ontological implications were bracketed. The Copenhagen interpretation became the acceptable face of quantum mechanics precisely because it permitted physicists to use the formalism without confronting what it said about the nature of reality. As Barad demonstrates at length in Meeting the Universe Halfway, Bohr was pointing toward something that the dominant framework had no room for: an entangled world in which the observer is always already part of what is being observed, in which there is no prior independently determinate reality waiting to be measured. The retreat from this recognition is the same retreat Plato made. Posthumanism and new materialism are not the first traditions to contest this retreat: Spinoza's monism, Whitehead's process philosophy, and phenomenology each pushed back in their own registers: but they are the tradition that refuses it in the specific configuration of the now: holding the post-human and vital materialism together in a way that earlier counter-movements did not.

The suppression did not go uncontested. While Bohr's entanglement was being domesticated by the Copenhagen interpretation (needs citation and page number from Barad), artists were enacting the same ontology in practice: not as philosophical argument but as perceptual fact. Merleau-Ponty identifies Cézanne as a phenomenologist avant la lettre, and the connection is not incidental. Cézanne was working at precisely the historical moment when both quantum physics and phenomenology were beginning to articulate what classical science and Cartesian painting had systematically suppressed: that the body does not perceive the world from a detached point of view, and that matter does not consist of solid objects with fixed contours awaiting neutral description. When the body moves around in space, it does not perceive things with the relative sizes objective calculation would predict. It does not inhabit a flat, geometrical, fully determinate plane but a milieu, an Umwelt that remains ambiguous, indeterminate, and resonant with an expressive significance that affects the body's perception of spatial relations. Merleau-Ponty discerns Cézanne as trying to capture these perspectival distortions as they occur in perception, in order to convey "an emerging order," where the object is "in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes" (Coole, 2010, p. 104). Cézanne's painting brings the material world alive; it does not measure or represent reality but emulates the way it materializes in perception (Coole, 2010, p. 106). Coole notes that Merleau-Ponty explicitly contrasts this with the Cartesian tradition of classical perspective: what he calls "Renaissance perspective as a cultural fact" and "impoverished" as artistic style precisely because its Euclidean geometry presents nature in fixed, objective coordinates rather than in the ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant field in which it actually appears. Cézanne's refusal of that convention is therefore not an aesthetic choice but a phenomenological position: the attempt to suspend culturally fashioned perception and uncover the "vertical" world of "brute" or "wild" perception as it emerges (Coole, 2010, p. 106). Dada introduces chance, noise, and indeterminacy into artistic practice as ontological statement, not provocation. The Bauhaus and Black Mountain College bring together artists, scientists, musicians, and designers in configurations that refuse disciplinary separation: themselves a kind of institutional enactment of the entangled world. John Cage, working at Black Mountain and drawing directly on the I Ching: the relational cosmology that Leibniz had encountered and stripped to notation: recovers what got bracketed out: indeterminacy, chance, noise as information rather than interruption. His 4'33" is not silence; it is the insistence that the world is always already sounding, that the frame of attention is the apparatus. Robert Rauschenberg's combines: street debris, newspaper, paint, radio: are assemblages in Bennett's sense before Bennett named them: matter with thing-power, distributed agency, the refusal of a sovereign authoring subject. Shannon's suppressed insight that noise carries the most information finds its artistic enactment in all of this: the Dadaist cut-up, the found sound, the accidental score. The counter-tradition is not incidental to the theoretical project of posthumanism and new materialism. It is its genealogy.

The suppression of matter by representation was not only contested in music and physics. Visual art theory ran the same argument, and it ran it with quantum physics as its resource. Barbara Bolt, working from painting practice (in Coleman and van der Tuin, eds., Methods and Genealogies of New Materialism, 2024), identifies the gap that Western visual theory has installed since Plato as its founding premise: representation is second-order data, a substitute that can never be its object, a sign that circulates within social meaning but cannot be in dynamic relation to the materiality of its referent (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 269). The influence of literary theory and cultural studies on visual art deepened the gap: reducing visual representation to a sign-system, a complex of discursive meanings. Bolt traces the limit of even Butler's sophisticated performativity account: Butler can explain how bodies materialize through repetition and discourse, but cannot account for "the materialisation that occurs in the interplay between the matter of bodies, cultural knowledges or discourse, and the materials of production" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 271). Matter slips away back into signification. The return Bolt calls for: "to the energetic potential of matter": runs via Vicki Kirby's (1997) reading of Bohr: for quantum physics, representation is not removed from an originary source, particles are inseparable identities in/of spacetime, and there is no enclosed local context within which causality might be contained. Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) confirm that this made Bolt one of the first art scholars to address quantum theory in relation to visual practice: a move made in parallel with, not downstream from, Barad's development of agential realism from the same quantum sources.

The art history lineage that supports this claim runs deeper than Bolt's individual contribution. Alongside the philosophical counter-tradition traced above: Baroque folding, Cézanne's phenomenological perception, the Black Mountain assemblage: there is a parallel track in which artists and art educators were explicitly drawing on particle physics as a resource for rethinking what art does and how it works. Scholars in art education have traced a lineage in which the quantum overthrow of classical determinism: the insistence that observation is always participatory, that phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatus that produces them: found its cultural articulation in the dematerialization of the art object and the turn toward process, event, and relation (Sullivan, [year and citation TK]). The trajectory runs from quantum indeterminacy (Bohr, Heisenberg) through pedagogical translation at Bauhaus and Black Mountain (process over product, experimentation over mastery) through Cage's chance operations as explicit quantum analogue through conceptual art's refusal of the fixed object through systems art and early computation (Burnham's Systems Esthetics, 1968) to generative and posthuman practice now. Bolt's intervention, originally published in 2003 and republished in Coleman and van der Tuin's Methods and Genealogies of New Materialism (2024), names the same quantum resources from within painting practice rather than conceptual art: arriving at the same conclusion from a different studio position. The two lines: conceptual art via Sullivan and painting theory via Bolt: converge on the same claim that Barad will later formalize philosophically: representation is not a second-order picture of a pre-given world but a co-constitutive intra-action with it.

## The Ontological Ground

Before moving further, one concept needs grounding, because it surfaces under many names throughout this text and the accumulation is intentional but not arbitrary. The Wild. The void. The absolute. Non-identity. A life. The primordial swerve. Zoe. The virtual. The plane of immanence. These are not synonyms in the strict sense: each carries the specific weight of the theoretical tradition from which it comes. But they are pointing at the same thing, or rather, at the same absence of a thing. What they name is the ontological ground that exists before and after any specific intra-action, outside any particular agential cut, beyond the reach of any apparatus that would make it measurable. It is the field of virtual potential from which phenomena emerge when conditions for their emergence are met, and into which they dissolve when the apparatus that held them in place is released.

Critically, this vocabulary is not describing a new condition. It is naming what has always been operating. The Dada artists, Rauschenberg, Cage, Cézanne: they did not anticipate posthumanism. They were enacting this ontological ground before anyone had vocabulary for it. Rauschenberg's combines were distributed assemblages of thing-power before Bennett named thing-power. Cage's 4'33" was an apparatus making visible the world's always-already sounding before Barad formalized what an apparatus does. The vocabulary reaches backward as well as forward: it is retrospective and prospective simultaneously. This is why we can look across the history of art, science, and philosophy and recognize practitioners of what we are only now formalizing. The terms do not inaugurate the condition. They make the condition legible across time. The same retrospective illumination is available to students encountering this vocabulary for the first time: they are not learning something new about the world. They are receiving language for what their own making has always already been enacting.

The words will be used more or less interchangeably throughout this text except where their specific theoretical origins require distinguishing, and each is developed fully in Part Two [link]. What follows is orientation, not definition.

It is not nothing. It is not chaos. It is, in Bennett's framing through Lucretius, the condition of possibility for all swerving: the clinamen, the deviation from the straight line that generates all actual things. It is what Adorno names when he insists that concepts are ouroboran: that all concepts live somewhere between the absolute and our more traditional human definition of knowing, because the absolute is real, generating, and permanently beyond our grasp.

Bennett borrows the phrase "a life" from Deleuze: a life, not my life or your life, but the impersonal generative force that moves through all living things and is not exhausted by any of them. When she admonishes Lucretius through Gertrude Stein: "there is no there, there": she is not saying the void is empty. She is saying it has no fixed location, no stable identity, no address you can return to. It is everywhere the formed thing is not yet, and everywhere the formed thing has already dissolved back into becoming. It is always already operating. It cannot be measured, only approached: which is why Bennett names lingering as the most vital step: to stay long enough in the encounter that the shimmer of the thing's participation in this larger field becomes briefly perceptible.

Barad reaches the same ground through quantum physics. Before measurement, before the agential cut that produces a phenomenon, there is superposition: not multiple things coexisting but a state of ontological indeterminacy in which the boundaries and properties of the "thing" have not yet been enacted. The apparatus does not reveal what was already there. It brings forth what was not yet determined. Between measurements, between intra-actions, what exists is this: the indeterminate field, the real-but-virtual, the condition from which specific configurations of time/space/matter emerge and into which they return. Braidotti calls it zoe: life as a cosmic force that precedes and exceeds any individual subject, any bounded organism, any particular moment of consciousness. The subject is a temporary intensification of this force, not its owner. "The life in 'me' does not answer to my name: 'I' is just passing" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 211). Braidotti names this force zoe : non-human life, the generative substrate : in explicit distinction from bios, the politically qualified, institutionally recognized, humanist life. Bios is the life the institution counts: the life that can be named, evaluated, granted rights, assigned authorship. Zoe is the life that was always already happening before the institution arrived to count it : the life of bacteria, of weather, of the clinamen, of the material processes that make the fog chamber possible. Posthumanism's decentering of the human subject is not nihilism; it is the recognition that bios has been mistaken for the whole of life, and zoe for mere background. New materialist art practice as research works in the register of zoe : attending to what is happening before and beneath the humanist account of what counts.

The closing sentence of the Transversality section: "we know nothing of the body until we know what it can do": opens here onto the full account this ground requires. That claim is Spinoza's, restated. It is the methodological consequence of everything above: if matter is generative rather than inert, if bodies are defined by their capacities rather than their essences, then the question is never what something is but what it does, what it enters into, what it makes possible. And the unit of reality adequate to that question is not the thing but the phenomenon: the material-discursive arrangement produced through the intra-action of apparatus and matter, which precedes and constitutes the things that appear within it. Performativity, in this frame, is not performance. It is the ontological claim that reality is constituted through specific, accountable, intra-active arrangements rather than pre-existing them. Affect is the register in which bodies know and are known before representation catches up: the change or variation that occurs when bodies come into contact, prior to its naming. These terms are developed fully in Part Two [link].

## What Counts as Research, Evidence, Knowledge

If there is no there, no constant state but in a constant change of modes (Bennett, citing Lucretius?), but instead everything is co-constituted through intra-actions in a material discursive arrangement of ongoing becomings, then how could we continue to reject knowledge not constituted as a closed field, continue to accept research and evidence that claims generalizability to space, place, time (when none of those things pre-exist or have stable existence after their co-constitution)?

Siffrinn and Coda (2025, p. 198) call for a cessation to the impetus towards "imposing higher order meaning on that which is already recognizable in data." Instead, as posthumanist and new materialist entities we should "focus on the forces and sites of transformations and potentialities spurred on by data" and to allow the process/intra-action/assemblage to be generative, moving with/by/through the iterative process, honoring its "capacity to affect and move the research entanglement in unanticipated directions."

What that operationalization looks like in practice is a shift from representational to relational units of analysis. In applied linguistics contexts, Siffrinn and Coda (2025) work primarily through assemblage thinking: arguing that once the assemblage is taken as the basic unit of inquiry in that field, "there cannot be a dividing line between word and thing, nor can there be an autonomous agent who stands outside of an empirical field imposing higher order meaning on an assemblage or its constituent parts" (p. 195). The specific unit varies across the broader field: for Bennett it is the thing and its distributed agency within the assemblage; for Barad it is the phenomenon: the material-discursive arrangement produced through the agential cut, which precedes and constitutes the things that appear to interact within it; and for Braidotti the unit of study is the nomadic subject in the context of and movement through cartographies and transpositions. What these diverge on is where to locate the basic unit. What they share is the refusal of any autonomous agent standing outside and imposing meaning from above. Canagarajah (2018a) describes this in terms of diverse semiotic resources working together: playing "a collaborative role," shaping "each other," capable of being "agentive" (p. 39): and scholars across applied linguistics, education, and environmental research are using relational thinking as their analytical frame rather than discrete, pre-given objects (Appleby & Pennycook, 2017; Weideman, 2017).

Affect is central to what this shift makes visible. Where representational methods attend to what data means, relational methods attend to what data does: and what it does often operates below the threshold of representation. Affect, as Colman (2010) puts it, is "the change, or variation, that occurs when bodies collide, or come into contact" (p. 11, cited in Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 196), enabling attention to the multisensory, intensive dimensions of an assemblage that conventional methods cannot register. Data, in this frame, has "an active materiality whose vibrancy might be to perturb rather than to be seen" (Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 197, citing Frimberger et al., 2018). The point is not that findings become invisible but that what counts as a finding shifts: research "can function as an event of becoming, one in which 'decisions have to be made for the moment, by the moment'" (Thrift, 2007, p. 217, cited in Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 198). The researcher is not separate from what is being studied: she is part of the apparatus that produces the phenomenon she is studying (Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 198): which is the empirical confirmation of what Barad names theoretically as intra-action.

## Method Follows Theory: A Family of Oriented Practices

If the world is constituted through intra-action, if agency is distributed, if matter is agentive and the observer is always already part of what it observes : then method cannot proceed as if it were a neutral instrument applied to a pre-given field. The ontological commitments of posthumanism and new materialism generate a specific family of methodological orientations, each derived from the same ground, each inflecting it differently. These are not separate methods to choose between. They are the posthumanist feminist situated lens refracted through different disciplinary locations and different research problems : arriving, convergently, at compatible structures because they are responding to the same suppression.

The family shares five characteristics. Methods are cartographic rather than classificatory: they map forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold rather than assigning phenomena to pre-existing categories. They are accountable to the apparatus: the instrument of knowing is not neutral with respect to what it knows but constitutive of it, and rigor consists in naming and specifying the apparatus rather than pretending it is absent. They are affirmative rather than only critical: following Braidotti's insistence that critique without construction is an incomplete project, these methods orient toward what is being produced and what is becoming possible, not only toward what is wrong. They refuse exhaustive capture: generativity is the condition of accountable knowledge in a world of ongoing becoming, not a methodological limitation to be corrected. And they are situated: every account is grounded in the specific political and material coordinates of the subject's position, not in a claim to universality that suppresses the apparatus that produced it.

Within this shared ground, the specific practices take different forms depending on entry point. Barad enters through physics and the apparatus : specifying agential cuts, tracing marks on bodies, holding the researcher accountable for constitutive exclusions. Haraway enters through feminist technoscience and figuration : partial perspectives, situated knowledges, the cyborg as methodological figure for the practitioner who refuses both the view from nowhere and the fantasy of a pre-given identity. Braidotti enters through continental philosophy and nomadic ethics : transpositions as creative leaps across imaginary formations, cartography as the tracking of becoming rather than the mapping of what is fixed. Bennett enters through affect and perception : the four Adornian techniques, lingering, the cultivation of enchantment as the perceptual ground for a politics of distributed agency. Trinh enters through film practice : speaking nearby, holding the interval, the entire onto-epistemological stance materialized in the structure of the work itself.

These methods share an ontological commitment worked out from different starting positions. A practitioner drawing on this framework does not choose between them. She lets each contribute what it does best: Barad for apparatus specification, Haraway for situatedness and partial perspective, Braidotti for the creative leap and cartographic movement, Bennett for perceptual and ethical retraining, Trinh for the practice that performs what the others theorize. The family is not a canon to be applied. It is a set of orientations that open different aspects of the same relational world to inquiry.

## Theory into Method

The methodological distinction between posthumanism and new materialism follows the same logic as the philosophical one. Because posthumanism's critical inheritance centers the deconstruction of the humanist subject, its methods concentrate on subject-formation and power in the present: cartographic, genealogical, figural, affirmative. Braidotti's cartography is explicitly presented as a grounded, selective mapping of forces with a nomadic subject as the protagonist: tracking what we are ceasing to be and what we are becoming, using concepts as navigational tools rather than final definitions. Braidotti insists that critique not stall at diagnosis, asserting an affirmative pivot: giving the thinking entity something to do with the theory, a way to be, a position from which to move (Wolfe, 2010). Because new materialism's critical inheritance centers the animation of matter, its methods train perception toward nonhuman agency and distributed emergent causality: assemblage, attunement, lingering, the cultivation of enchantment as perceptual and ethical retraining. Things have agency and their own thing-power whether humans recognize it or not. New materialism is the most relevant philosophical framework for rethinking the relationship to the complex environmental and social ecologies that define the posthuman era (Braidotti, 2023). These methodological emphases are compatible for the same reason the philosophical ones are: both are derived from the same ontological ground. A practitioner working within this framework does not choose between them. She draws on each for what it does best: PH methods for tracking subject-formation and power, NM methods for training perception toward material agency: because they are responding to the same formation from different angles, and the world requires both.

A further trait of the new materialisms that distinguishes them from posthumanism's more oppositional orientation is their antipathy toward oppositional ways of thinking itself. As Coole and Frost observe, new materialist exponents "generally decline to locate themselves explicitly through critiques of ontological dualism such as one finds in Cartesianism: they prefer a creative affirmation of a new ontology" (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 9). The prevailing ethos of new materialist ontology is consequently "more positive and constructive than critical or negative: it sees its task as creating new concepts and images of nature that affirm matter's immanent vitality." Such thinking is accordingly post- rather than anti-Cartesian. It avoids dualism or dialectical reconciliation by espousing a monological account of emergent matter --- drawing inspiration from alternative ontologies such as Spinoza's, developed less contemporaneously with Cartesianism than alongside it, yet which "until recently enjoyed a far more subterranean or subjugated existence" (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 9). This Spinozian undercurrent --- matter as self-organizing, intelligent, immanently generative --- is precisely the tradition that Braidotti names as the neo-Spinozist monistic ontology at the foundation of the critical posthumanities.

This is not a claim unique to posthumanism or new materialism. Science and technology studies arrives at the same conclusion from a different direction. Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch (2020), in their introduction to Dialogues between Artistic Research and Science and Technology Studies, state it directly: "research methods do not only observe and represent materials, issues, and events but in fact act upon and intervene in these materials, issues, and events" (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 7). Research not only analyzes, documents, and informs : it performs realities and ontologies, and reforms and transforms them through the act of researching. This is the STS version of what Barad names theoretically as the apparatus: the instrument of knowing is not neutral with respect to what it knows but constitutive of it. The convergence of PH/NM and STS on this point is significant. It means the claim is not a philosophical preference or a political commitment specific to feminist theory. It is what careful empirical study of how research actually works shows to be the case, across disciplines, when the backstage labor of science and research is subjected to scrutiny. Method is always already intervention. The question is whether the researcher acknowledges it.

Posthumanism is generated from postmodernism and, as Ferrando states, "seems to resist the notion of 'method', and it actually does" (Ferrando, 2012, p. 10). This resistance is principled, not evasive. A posthumanist methodology inherits the postmodern critique of objective knowledge and absolute truth but does not reproduce postmodernism's structure: rather than oscillating between the absolute and the relative, it dismisses that dualism and operates instead as a perspectivism: situated, embodied, multiple, constitutively open (Ferrando, 2013 [thesis], pp. 98–102). Its value is, in Feyerabend's terms, "merely transitional and contingent" (Feyerabend, 1975, cited in Ferrando, 2012, p. 10): "in no way definitive, but dynamic, mutant, shifting": aware of the state of things in order to acknowledge current challenges and remain open to possibilities (Ferrando, 2012, p. 10). It does not recognize any primacy to the written text: "Posthumanism can be performed in many ways" (Ferrando, 2012, p. 10): and this is the theoretical ground from which art practice as research draws its legitimacy as a mode of posthumanist inquiry.

The methodological commitments of posthumanism and new materialism are not separable from how the research is conducted. Ferrando names the failure mode directly: "hegemonic essentialism": the widespread habit of producing a posthumanist text in content while framing it entirely within the hegemonic traditions posthumanism was built to refuse. "A text written by such standards might claim a posthuman content, but does not appear posthuman in its praxis. Its inner contradiction is obvious: while attempting to produce a discourse critical of humanism, it uncritically frames itself within those same traditions from which humanism developed" (Ferrando, 2013 [thesis], pp. 183–184). The methodology must enact what the theory claims, or the theory undermines itself. This is not a stylistic preference. It is the ethico-onto-epistemological requirement Barad names: the apparatus through which knowledge is produced is not separate from what it produces, and a posthumanist inquiry that reproduces hegemonic apparatuses of citation, genre, and epistemic authority is producing something other than posthumanist knowledge regardless of what it says. Siffrinn and Coda confirm the same requirement from the applied research side: PH and NM carry "ontological and epistemological commitments that are distinct from those in other traditions" and demand a rethinking of the field's "most taken-for-granted constructs": including "how they have come to structure our research practices and their ensuing dissemination" (Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 198). The consequence is methodological: these theories "invite more experimental research practices" and require "theoretico-methodological experimentation" precisely because the onto-epistemological arrangements they enact cannot be carried out through methods designed for a different set of commitments (Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, pp. 191, 198). The researcher working within this framework is asked to do research differently, not merely to adopt different theoretical vocabulary while preserving the same research apparatus. Kontturi and Tiainen extend this argument historically: art practice was not a late recipient of new materialist theory but "one of the very milieus in which the call to rethink the involvement of matter in human practices and in the more-than-human world arose" (Kontturi and Tiainen, 2021, p. 259). The enactment preceded the articulation. If posthumanist methodology must perform its own claims, and if art practice was one of the founding sites of that performance, then art practice as research is not a marginal application of posthumanism and new materialism. It is one of its origin points.

The transversality van der Tuin and Dolphijn trace through new materialism : its tendency to cut across disciplines, paradigms, and temporal boundaries rather than settling into any one territory : finds empirical confirmation in what is happening in applied research fields right now. Siffrinn and Coda's (2025) survey of posthumanist and new materialist research in applied linguistics finds that "these theories are being operationalized by scholars in the field on a larger scale than is being returned in research databases" (p. 194) : which means the transversal movement has already outrun its own documentation. The theories travel faster than the institutional categories built to classify them.

## The Researcher as Apparatus

The critique of researcher bias has traveled a specific arc across fields. The positivist ideal: the view from nowhere, the neutral observer, the God's trick Haraway names: gave way, under pressure from feminist, postcolonial, and interpretive traditions, to the demand for positionality disclosure. The researcher was asked to name her location: her race, gender, institutional affiliation, theoretical commitments. This disclosure was framed as a corrective: a way of flagging potential distortion so that readers could adjust. The assumption remained that bias was a contamination to be acknowledged and, where possible, minimized. Research was still understood as something that would ideally happen without a situated subject in the way. (needs citation?)

Posthumanist and new materialist frameworks move from the historical critique of bias towards an embracing of the situated knower/nomadic subject. If the world is always-becoming through intra-action, if there is no phenomenon prior to the apparatus that produces it, if matter and meaning co-constitute each other through specific material-discursive arrangements: then there is no view from outside. The researcher is not adjacent to the research. She is part of the apparatus. Her situatedness does not introduce error into an otherwise clean process; it is constitutive of the process itself. Barad is unequivocal: the apparatuses that produce phenomena are not separate from what they produce. The agential cut that makes a phenomenon legible is always already enacted from within the entanglement, never from outside it. On this account, the demand that a researcher minimize her bias is a demand that she pretend to be something the ontology says she cannot be.

This move: from situatedness as contamination to situatedness as constitutive: was worked out first in feminist practice. Rich's "politics of location" is its founding gesture: the demand to begin not with a pretense of neutrality but with the body, the specific geography closest in, as the only honest epistemological starting point. Braidotti traces the line from Rich directly into new materialism's situated epistemology (New Materialism, p. 22), and Mąkowska (2022) shows that what Rich was enacting in her poetics: mapping her own flesh as medical history, sociopolitical inscription, and epistemological ground simultaneously: is structurally isomorphic to what Barad will later formalize as the researcher's position within the apparatus. The apparatus argument is feminist body theory formalized through quantum physics. Haraway's "God's trick" critique is the same critique Rich was making in 1984 when she insisted that there is no speaking from outside one's location, only from within it. What changes between Rich and Barad is vocabulary and philosophical frame. The underlying claim: that the knower is always already inside what she studies: is a continuously worked position/condition.

The artist-researcher takes this one step further, but the move is not toward greater centrality: it is toward a more precise account of her position within a distributed apparatus. Braidotti's cartography is not the map one neutral observer makes of a pre-existing terrain. It is the specific trajectory of a specific nomadic subject moving through a specific configuration of forces, in intra-action with the other human and nonhuman actants that constitute the apparatus alongside her. The knowledge produced is not generalizable in the traditional sense. It does not claim to represent what any researcher would find. It claims something more precise and more honest: this is what became thinkable through this particular assemblage: this subject, this material, this set of encounters, this configuration of time/space/matter: and the research artifact produced through it is offered as evidence to other entities capable of perceiving it. The situated cartography is not the packaging around the findings. It is the findings.

What the artist-researcher brings specifically is a transposition framework: the unique mediation through which the assemblage produces a research artifact that can be shared as evidence with other perceiving entities. She is one actant within the apparatus, not its sovereign. She does not produce the research phenomenon alone; she enters into intra-action with materials, spaces, other bodies, institutional structures, and the accumulated force of prior encounters, all of which participate with equal ontological weight in what gets made. Her situatedness is the particular node through which the apparatus becomes this inquiry, generating this knowledge, for this community of reception. Specificity is not a limitation on generalizability. It is the condition of possibility for any knowledge at all.

Positionality and accountability for one's unique nomadic position is not a methodological add-on. It is constitutive of how artists especially do research and participate in knowledge work. The artist-researcher is always already an embodied transpositional apparatus: as Rauschenberg was, as Cage was, as Cézanne was: enacting the ontological ground before and beyond any vocabulary available to name it. What changes with the vocabulary is not the practice but its legibility: to other practitioners, to institutions, to the broader knowledge community.

## Deleuze Called for a Diffractive Practice

In Cheah's essay "Non-Dialectical Materialism" (in Coole and Frost, 2010) on the philosophical juxtaposition between the materialist views of Derrida and Deleuze, he focuses on their shared agreement "on the coming of the new: the advent of the entirely other that disrupts presence or the opening of actuality to multiple becomings." The effectivity of both materialisms lies in the urgency of rethinking the ontological bases of, well, everything everywhere all at once. Deleuze explicitly called for a diffractive practice.

Cheah identifies in Deleuze a third emphasis beyond the critique of representation and the affirmation of assemblage: the "(quasi-)transcendental ground" (Cheah, 2010, p. 89) that "indexes virtual singularities prior to their actualization as forms, and to the in-between of already actualized forms that are always pulsing with singularity and virtual force" (p. 88). This ground is the condition of possibility for the actual: not a stable substrate beneath events but the generative field from which events arise and into which they dissolve. There is no intentional subject or object here; both are always contingent, always in a state of becoming. The subject is generated from this transcendental field, made up of pre-individual and impersonal singularities (Cheah, 2010, p. 85). This is the same condition the document's opening section names under multiple portals: the Wild, the void, zoe, a life, the plane of immanence. Deleuze arrives at it through what he calls the virtual: and he does so in explicit dialogue with Kant's noumenon, the thing-in-itself that exists beyond what any apparatus of measurement can reach. Where Kant treats the noumenon as permanently unknowable and therefore methodologically irrelevant, Deleuze refuses that move: difference is not phenomenon but "the noumenon closest to the phenomenon" (Cheah, 2010, p. 84, citing Deleuze, p. 222). The virtual is real. It generates the actual. It cannot be measured, only approached: which is why, in the language this document has already established, lingering is the practice it calls for.

For Deleuze, matter as the plane of immanence is a dynamism of differentiations, speeds, and flows prior to any organized form. Following Hjelmslev, Deleuze and Guattari define matter as "the unformed, unorganized, nonstratified, or destratified body and all its flows: subatomic and submolecular particles, pure intensities, prevital and prephysical free singularities" (cited in Cheah, 2010, p. 87). The material body is not an organism but an aggregate whose elements vary according to their connections and relations of movement and rest: a body without organs whose constitution is always provisional, always in process. "Materiality is nothing other than the plane of immanence" (p. 86). Every actual object carries a virtual content; actualization is not the realization of a pre-formed possibility but the expression of a difference that was real before it was determinate. The virtual "must be defined as strictly a part of the real object: as though the object had one part of itself in the virtual into which it is plunged as though into an objective dimension" (Cheah, 2010, p. 84, citing Deleuze, p. 209). The actual object is not the destination of a one-way process. It emits new virtuals as it actualizes, bringing itself back into relation with the field of differential relations in which it can always dissolve and become otherwise.

Bennett renders Deleuze's virtual in her own key: "a life": not my life or your life, but what she calls "a pure event, freed from the subjectivity and objectivity of what happens," "a subjective current visible only fleetingly," inhabiting "that uncanny, non-time existing between the various moments of biographical or morphological time" (Vibrant Matter, p. 53). It is "a restless activeness, a destructive creative force present that does not coincide fully with any specific body" (p. 53). Through Nietzsche, Bennett names its character: "a monster of energy that does not expend itself but only transforms itself, a play of forces and waves of forces, at the same time one and many, a sea of forces flowing and rushing together, eternally changing" (Nietzsche, cited in Bennett, p. 53). A life "tears the fabric of the actual without ever fully coming out in a person placed or thing"; it is "matter of movement or matter energy, a matter in variation that enters assemblages and leaves them" (p. 53). It is, finally, "a vitality, proper, not to any individual but to pure eminence: that is not actual though it is real" (p. 53). Vibratory. Liquid. Virtual. A particle force without a position. This is the same condition Deleuze names through the noumenon, Braidotti through zoe, Barad through superposition. The words are different portals into the same field: and diffractive practice is the attempt to cultivate enough attentiveness to feel the field moving beneath the things.

This is why diffractive practice asks not what differences exist between things but what differences are being made, and what those differences make possible. Bateson named this the ground of information itself: a difference that makes a difference. Where comparative analysis maps differences as properties already residing in discrete objects, diffractive analysis traces the patterns that emerge when differences intra-act: attending to what the encounter produces rather than what the objects were before it. For Barad, diffraction patterns are precisely "patterns of difference that make a difference": not a record of where things sit in relation to one another, but a tracing of the effects difference generates as it moves through a field. The question shifts from "what is X" to "what does this configuration of differences produce, and what does it foreclose." This is Deleuze's dice throw made methodological: Being is constituted each time by events of chance, throws that give rise to different singularities or commencements (Cheah, 2010, p. 83). Ontology is the chaosmos from which the cosmos emerges. The researcher does not stand outside this process as its describer. She is one of the throws.

## Closing of Part One

The distinction between posthumanism and new materialism established across Part One is a theoretical distinction: it maps different emphases, genealogies, and methodological centers of gravity. What confirms it from the direction of practice is that the protocols researchers have developed under the posthumanist and new materialist umbrella reproduce exactly the division of labor the theoretical account predicts. Lambert (submitted) uses NM concepts: intra-action, assemblage, spacetimemattering: to operationalize the PH ethical stance of refusing the humanist reflective practitioner. Hill (2017) frames the three modes of becoming-diffractive in explicitly PH terms: the decentered subject, the refusal of the individual teacher as autonomous agent: but operationalizes them through NM conceptual tools: intra-action, becoming-with, assemblage, affect. Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025) develop their six transposition practices on the PH side of the distinction: critique of Man, nomadic subject, affirmative ethics: but anchor them in a NM ontology of relational becoming. In each case, PH provides the critical-ethical vector that says why the practice matters and what is at stake, and NM provides the material-ontological vector that says what the world is made of and how phenomena are constituted. Neither framework can do the other's work. The practice texts confirm the theoretical distinction not by citing it but by enacting it.

This is where Part One opens onto Part Two. If posthumanism and new materialism together establish that the world is relational, processual, and virtual before it is actual, then the question becomes how to inhabit and work within that world as a researcher and practitioner. Diffractive practice is the response to that question: and it is the subject of what follows.

## Part Two: Diffractive Practice

This section answers the second question: how have posthumanist theorists conceptualized practice that honors the ontological commitments of posthumanism and new materialism? The original framing of this question named "diffractive practice" as its target. That framing remains useful but requires widening. A Google Scholar search for texts addressing diffractive practice and transpositions together returns zero results: the two are not yet named as a joint category in the literature. What the literature does show is a family of protocols and orientations developed under different labels: diffractive practice (Barad, Haraway), transpositions (Braidotti), becoming-diffractive (Hill), diffractive watching (Militsi), mosaic listening (Clark and Moss, extended by Dudley): that share the same ontological ground without sharing a common name. The umbrella that holds them together is not a label but a set of commitments: rejection of representationalism, decentering of the human, distributed agency, accountability to the apparatus rather than to an external canon, and ethics as ongoing negotiation rather than fixed rule. Under this umbrella, what Part One established as the PH/NM distinction maps onto a methodological division of labor: posthumanism provides the critical-ethical vector: what is at stake, who is centered, what counts as justice: while new materialism provides the material-ontological vector: what the world is made of, how phenomena are constituted, where agency is distributed. Protocols labeled as diffractive practice or transpositions are what happens when both vectors are put to work simultaneously. Part Two traces how different theorists have developed that simultaneity, and what it looks like when it is operationalized in research and art practice.

### Relational Worlds Need Relational Methods

If the world is constituted through relation and intra-action: if phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, if agency is distributed across assemblages of human and nonhuman actants, if matter is vibratory and generative rather than inert: then methods that treat phenomena as discrete, stable, and pre-given do not merely produce incomplete findings. They produce a systematically distorted account of what the world is. The mismatch between a relational ontology and a representationalist method is not a technical problem to be corrected through rigor. It is an ethical problem: to impose a subject-object framework on a relational world is to enact the very suppression of vitality, agency, and entanglement that posthumanism and new materialism were built to refuse.

Bennett names the perceptual root of the problem directly. Without a cultivated, countercultural practice of attending to what exceeds the subject-object split, "the world appears as if it consists only of active human subjects who confront passive objects and their law-governed mechanisms" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. xiv). This appearance is not a neutral description. It is the product of trained perceptual habits: habits that are, as Bennett insists, politically consequential. A world that appears to consist only of active subjects and passive objects is a world that authorizes domination, extraction, and the suppression of the distributed agency that runs through every assemblage. Relational methods are not a preference or a philosophical style. They are the attempt to correct a trained perceptual distortion that has been doing political damage for as long as the subject-object split has been the default frame.

Developing the capacity for relational perception requires what Bennett calls foundational comportments: an open-minded, open-ended willingness to suspend the pre-conceived; an anticipatory readiness to encounter thing-power before it has been named or categorized; and "a perceptual style open to the appearance of thing-power" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 5). This last phrase matters because it frames the researcher's orientation not as neutrality but as a trained receptivity: a readiness cultivated in advance of any specific encounter. Bennett traces this orientation to Thoreau's instruction to practice "the discipline of looking always at what is to be seen" (p. 5): not looking for what you expect, not looking to confirm what you already know, but sustaining the open discipline of looking itself as a practice.

This perceptual discipline connects directly to van der Tuin and Dolphijn's (2010) argument that new materialism requires cartography rather than classification as its methodological posture. Classification is a territorializing gesture: it assigns new phenomena to pre-existing categories, confirms what is already known, and forecloses what the encounter might otherwise disclose. Cartography is generative: it maps forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold, producing a "living map": Braidotti's phrase: rather than a fixed taxonomy. The shift from classification to cartography is the methodological consequence of the ontological shift from substance to relation. Relational worlds cannot be classified into stability. They can only be mapped in motion.

Fox and Alldred's (2021) work in applied research domains: education, health, sustainability: demonstrates what this looks like in practice. Diffractive method is particularly generative precisely in research situations that are already assemblages of institutions, bodies, technologies, and affects, because it refuses to quarantine any of those elements as mere context. The research situation itself: the classroom, the clinic, the policy environment: is treated as an active participant in what gets known, not as a neutral container for findings that then travel elsewhere. The question of how findings travel into policy and practice contexts is itself a question about assemblage: what configurations make certain knowledge mobile, and which suppress it.

### A Shared Commitment to an Affirmative Ethics

Bennett, Braidotti, and Barad share an ethical orientation that follows directly from their ontology: which is to say, ethics in these frameworks is not a set of principles applied to research from outside but the practical consequence of taking seriously what the ontology says about the world. If agency is distributed, if matter is vibrant, if phenomena are constituted through intra-action: then the ethical question is not "what are the rules" but "what does this specific configuration produce and who and what does it include or foreclose." The three share a name for this stance: affirmative ethics. Not optimism, and not the suspension of critique, but the refusal to let critique be the final word: the insistence that diagnosis must be followed by construction, that naming what is wrong must open toward imagining what could be otherwise.

Bennett's ethics are grounded in the understanding that if humans are also things, then what we do to things, we do to ourselves. This is not a sentimental claim. It is the ethical consequence of the ontology: if agency is distributed across the assemblage, if matter is vibrant and generative rather than inert, then the human is not the only ethical agent in the configuration and cannot be the only ethical addressee. Bennett hopes through this realization that humans will decenter themselves and take more care in their situatedness in the world and ecology. What this produces methodologically is the practice of attentiveness: lingering long enough in the encounter with matter that the thing's own vitality becomes perceptible, and allowing that perception to reorient the ethical imagination toward nonhuman agency and distributed consequence.

Barad's ethics are inseparable from her epistemology and ontology: the hyphenation ethico-onto-epistemology is not decorative. The agential cut is simultaneously a cognitive act (it produces determinate phenomena from indeterminate fields), a material act (it leaves marks on bodies), and an ethical act (it forecloses what it does not produce, and the researcher is response-able for those constitutive exclusions). Accountability, in this framework, is located precisely in the cut: naming the apparatus, specifying what it enacts, tracing the marks it leaves. The researcher who does this is doing ethics in the Baradian sense: not as a separate activity from research but as the same activity seen from the angle of responsibility.

For Braidotti, the raison d'etre is affirmative ethics. The ontological grounding for Braidotti's affirmative ethics is zoe: life as a cosmic force that exceeds and precedes any individual subject. The subject is "an autopoietic machine fuelled by targeted perceptions" functioning as "the echoing chamber of zoe." This nonanthropocentric view "expresses both a profound love for Life as a cosmic force and the desire to depersonalize subjective life-and-death. This is just one life my life. The life in 'me' does not answer to my name: 'I' is just passing" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 211). Affirmative ethics is not optimism. It emerges from this radical depersonalization: because the individual "I" is not the center of life but a temporary passing of it, the logic of retribution, rights-claims, and compensation: all premised on a stable individual subject: dissolves. What replaces it is an "ethics of affirmation" that "suspends the quest for both claims and compensation and resists the logic of retribution or rights" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 214). The wound is real. The response is to depersonalize it, move through it, and affirm the generative force of life continuing beyond it. Bennett names the same insistence from a different angle: critique without construction is the explicit failure she positions vibrant materialism against. The political ecology of things is not a theory of what is wrong with the world. It is a practice of attending to what is generative in it: enchantment as the perceptual ground for a politics that can imagine differently rather than only diagnose more precisely.

The three ethics converge on the same refusal: the refusal of a sovereign subject at the center of the ethical universe, and the insistence that accountability is distributed across the assemblage, enacted through the apparatus, and oriented toward the generative force of life rather than the stable claims of the individual. This is what it means for ethics to follow from ontology rather than being imposed on it from outside.  
### Bennett, Braidotti, Barad: Ontology

"These theories have ontological and epistemological commitments that are distinct from those in other traditions." (Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 198)

Bennett, Braidotti, and Barad are not the inventors of the ontological ground Part One has been tracing. They are its current formalizers: the thinkers who, working from different disciplinary positions in the same historical moment, gave the inherited vocabulary its most precise and methodologically generative articulation. Bennett arrives through political theory and Thoreau, Lucretius, Adorno, and Spinoza; Braidotti through continental philosophy, feminist theory, and Deleuze; Barad through quantum physics and feminist science studies. Each inherits traditions that long predate the 1990s. What they share is the project of making that inheritance speakable as method.

All three describe a universe where there are no essential enduring qualities of any thing, thus the thing itself is not completely knowable. Because reality is created through specific configurations of space and matter that only exist as they are in one unit of time, as soon as we think we have divined some essential nature, some knowable character, the configuration is gone. Bennett stages us in assemblages of agential swarms of thing-power flowing in and out of the primordial swerve. Braidotti connects Bennett's always-becoming assemblages as situated cartographies from and through which nomadic subjects enact affirmative ethics. Through these entangled assemblages and cartographies and unique material discursive engagements we enact the agential cuts through embodied apparatus and transpositions.

An assemblage is a unique configuration of things and actants that come together in a particular space/time/matter configuration. The things act on each other through a mechanism of call and response. Agency is distributed, thus an assemblage can also be imagined as an agential swarm. The foundational unit of reality for Bennett is the thing or actant.

For Barad, phenomena: unique material discursive arrangements: are the primary building block of reality. Agential realism is her term for how this reality works: phenomena are the basic unity formed through the frame of an apparatus to create intra-actions of things through material discursive arrangements. Barad created the term "intra-action" as a response to the inadequate and misleading term interaction, which implies a pre-given body with inherent qualities. Intra-action defines agency as a mutually constituted, distributed, emergent force (Barad, 2007, p. 141). Apparatuses are phenomena, not unique stable things, constituted as "specific agential practices... through which specific exclusionary boundaries are enacted," and as "dynamic (re)configurings of the world." As each apparatus is a unique material-discursive arrangement, so are the cuts they make, drawing unique distinctions between subject/object/instrument. Different marks on bodies indicate configurations of apparatus.

Objectivity can only be obtained through accountability to the boundaries we made through the agential cut and the marks we left on bodies. Barad rejects the "God's trick" highlighted by Haraway (1988, p. 581), that outcomes or measurements are ever neutral. These commitments generate a set of orienting questions that function as a diffractive practice notebook: not a checklist but a recursive accountability structure the researcher returns to throughout the inquiry: What is the apparatus: the specific material-discursive arrangement through which this phenomenon is being produced? What agential cut does it enact: what boundaries does it draw, what relata does it constitute? What/who does the cut exclude: what is rendered invisible or inaudible by this specific configuration? What marks are left on bodies: what material traces does the encounter produce in the researcher, the participants, the site, the objects? These questions are answerable. They do not demand a generalizable finding. They demand specification of the apparatus and accountability for its cuts: which is a more rigorous standard than the demand for a transferable conclusion, because it names what the specific encounter actually produced rather than what a decontextualized claim extracted from it might assert.

Wolfe (2010) posited that a new understanding of the world's becoming and ontology required a new epistemology: a way of knowing that matched the world as it is. Barad instantiated the term *ethico-onto-epistem-ology* (2007, p. 90) to elucidate the necessarily entangled ontologies, epistemologies, and ethics of engagement with the world. See the all-encompassing nature of [[Kudzu]](https://www.nature.org/en-us/about-us/where-we-work/united-states/indiana/stories-in-indiana/kudzu-invasive-species/). Fox & Alldred (2021, need page) summarize Barad's ethico-onto-epistemology as the inseparability of "responsibility for the practices of research and how its outputs make a difference (ethics), that which is observed (ontology), and the research apparatus... (epistemology)."

Bennett and Braidotti share that stance. The foundational goal of Bennett's 2010 *Vibrant Matter: A Political Ecology of Things* was the hope for a new ethics of entanglement, where humans realized that if they are made of the same stuff as all other things, if humans are "walking, talking matter" (*Vibrant Matter* [page TK]), then maybe "we are not one body or embodied, rather we are an array of bodies," and therefore "we must cultivate a talent for remembering it, because this grounds us in an emergent reality" (Bennett, *Vibrant Matter* [page TK]).

The move from representationalism to relational ontology consistently generates one objection: that in rejecting absolutes, posthumanism and new materialism embrace relativism. If there is no view from nowhere, if knowledge is always situated, if the apparatus is always part of what it produces: then how is any account better than any other? This is the charge that sank postmodernism's ethical project, and both Barad and Braidotti are aware of it. The answer runs through three moves made in sequence.

The first is Hayles's constrained constructivism (1997): representations are neither mirrors of an independent reality nor purely arbitrary constructions. They are consistent or inconsistent with what Hayles calls "the flux": the real but unreachable ground of things. Consistency means local, interactive, instrumentally efficacious engagement; it replaces the true/false binary with a not-true/not-false relation that "rides the cusp" between the world as it is and the world as we can construct it (Hayles, 1997, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 157). This is not relativism: it is accountability to the constraints the world imposes on representation, even when those constraints cannot be specified in advance.

The second is Haraway's situated knowledge: the partial perspective is not a weaker objectivity but a stronger one, because it is accountable for the position from which it sees. The god-trick: the view from nowhere: is not neutral; it is a specific and interested position that refuses accountability for the cuts it makes. Situated knowledge names the cut and owns it.

The third is Barad's agential cut as the locus of ethical accountability. The cut is not arbitrary: it is specified by the apparatus, produces determinate marks on bodies, and can be described, compared, and held accountable. Braidotti's nomadic cartography enacts the same move at the level of subject formation: the nomadic account is grounded not in a universal standard but in the specific political and material coordinates of the subject's location. Neither framework produces relativism. Both produce accountability without universalism: which is precisely what the ethico-onto-epistemological framework names: responsibility is located in the intra-action, not outsourced to an external canon.  
### Refusal of Exhaustive Capture

Braidotti states this as a methodological principle of cartography:  
"Being grounded, any cartographic account is necessarily selective, partial and never exhaustive" (Braidotti, 2018).

This matches van der Tuin & Dolphijn's argument that  
classificatory/territorial logics can't "capture" theory-formation's  
nonlinearity; new materialism instead de-territorializes and becomes  
"cartographical."

Once method is recognized as boundary-making, exhaustiveness becomes not  
only impossible but also undesirable as a governing fantasy.  
Boundary-making is "a doing" that entails "constitutive exclusions," and  
that is precisely why diffractive practice must treat partiality as a  
form of rigor.

And the project's curatorial framing (Kontinuum) ties the  
"human/non-human" debate to matter as meshwork, and frames artistic  
research as engaging a "material continuum" of  
impurities/mixtures/singularities that resist categorization: "special cases and exceptions... are the rule" (Kirschner, 2016).

The refusal of exhaustive capture is not only a posthumanist or new  
materialist methodological principle. It originates in artistic and  
feminist practice. Trinh T. Minh-ha articulates it in 1990, working as a  
filmmaker and theorist, nearly two decades before Barad's agential  
realism consolidates the argument philosophically. In "Documentary  
Is/Not a Name," Trinh insists that "meaning should be prevented from  
coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown" and that what  
persists between the meaning of something and its truth is "the  
interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth  
congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). This interval is not a methodological  
deficiency. It is the condition of possibility for knowledge that does  
not replicate the epistemic violence of totalization. Her 1992 interview  
develops this directly: speaking nearby rather than about means "a  
speaking that does not objectify, does not point to an object as if it  
is distant from the speaking subject or absent from the speaking place.  
A speaking that reflects on itself and can come very close to a subject  
without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, p. 87). This is  
a practice of partial, situated, non-exhaustive knowledge: prior to  
and independent of Haraway's situated knowledges (1988) and Barad's  
agential cuts (2007), not derived from them.

The result of this citational movement is that an insight first articulated by an artist-filmmaker working in 1990 becomes legible within the NM theoretical canon only when it has been absorbed into feminist science studies. Trinh is acknowledged in secondary literature; her practice is the origin point. This is one instance of the broader pattern Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) identify: art and artists are frequently cited as evidence of NM claims while remaining outside the mainstream theoretical discussion centered on Barad. Restoring Trinh to her place in the genealogy is not a corrective addition. It changes what the refusal of exhaustive capture is: from a theoretical position derived from quantum physics and philosophy to a practice that filmmakers were enacting before theorists named it.

### Navigating the Field

Nothing exists as an individual entity until the agential cut, when we  
experience a unique material discursive arrangement thru a particular  
apparatus that allows for the measurement of phenomena, not things, but  
the intra-action of things in their ongoing becoming.

Bennett spoke of Derrida's call and response as how we intra-act with  
things in the agential flow. Barad points to particle flows propelled by  
the mechanisms of intra-action of electrons and their kin.

Agential swarms/assemblages need a mechanism of intra-action. Actants  
are the individual elements that have the capacity to affect other  
entities and themselves be affected.

As navigators we are the nomadic subjects, moving through a cartography  
of reality/the Zoe as it is in its specific time/space/matter  
configuration. Our movement along a path is guided by our affirmative  
ethics.

Trinh T. Minh-ha is among the most precise illustrations of this figure.  
As a Vietnamese filmmaker working in Senegal, the United States, and  
across the postcolonial world, she is not metaphorically nomadic. She  
inhabits multiple positions simultaneously without resolving them into a  
stable identity. She describes her practice as "a constant flow from the  
inside out and outside in," and insists that the challenge lies in  
"materializing a reality" in which the personal is not naturally  
political but every form of knowledge carries a specific location  
(Trinh, 1992, p. 82). Her films: Reassemblage (1982), Naked Spaces:  
Living is Round (1985), Surname Viet Given Name Nam (1989): do not  
represent nomadism as subject matter. They enact it as method: refusing  
the position of the expert-observer who speaks about, insisting instead  
on a "speaking nearby" that "reflects on itself and can come very close  
to a subject without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, p.  
87). The nomadic cartography does not claim to map a fixed territory. It  
traces a movement through a territory that is itself in motion,  
attending to what becomes visible from this location at this moment:  
and offering that trace as evidence rather than conclusion. Fuser (2019)  
reads Trinh's entire cinematic project through Braidotti's nomadic  
subjectivity, arguing that her aesthetics produce a "politics of  
speaking nearby" that structurally destabilizes the gaze of alterity and  
challenges the epistemic authority of the Western observer (Fuser, 2019,  
pp. 107--121). The nomadic subject, in Trinh's practice, is not a  
philosophical figure. It is a filmmaker with a camera, a specific body,  
a specific history, working in specific material conditions, refusing  
exhaustive capture.

Braidotti's cartography is "a non-hierarchical and non-linear mapping"  
where "any form of encounter with the world... constitutes a legitimate  
resource of meaning." Forgetting to Forget, on Rosi Braidotti

Cartography as method is explicitly a politics of location as much as a  
navigation tool. "Situated locations draw a cartographic map of power  
relations and thus can also help identify possible sites and strategies  
of resistance. Nonlinearity and a nonunitary vision of the subject do  
not necessarily result in either cognitive or moral relativism or social  
anarchy": Braidotti frames them instead as "significant sites for  
reconfiguring feminist political practice" (Braidotti, 2011, p. 12).  
What connects Braidotti's cartography to the Adornian three steps that  
follow is precisely this: both name the ethical labor of navigating a  
world without stable ground. Adorno's ouroboran concept-making, the  
willingness to be the clown, the practice of lingering: these are the  
comportments of the nomadic subject moving through a cartographic field,  
refusing the fantasy of a view from above, staying inside the  
entanglement. Cartography is not a map you consult before entering the  
terrain. It is what you produce by moving through it accountably.

### Why We Need the Metaphor/Phenomena of Diffraction

The representationalist trap, as Barad names it, is the assumption that research is essentially a practice of reflection: the researcher holds a mirror to the world and produces an accurate image of what was already there. The mirror is a bad metaphor for research because it implies a stable, pre-given world on one side and a faithful reproduction of it on the other: with the researcher as the transparent medium between them. Every methodological framework built on this assumption inherits its ontological errors: the pre-given object, the detached observer, the neutral instrument, the result that travels from context to context because it was never really produced by any specific context at all. Diffraction is the alternative metaphor: and for Barad, more than a metaphor.

Reflection and diffraction are both optical phenomena, but they work differently. Reflection displaces the same: the image moves but does not change. Diffraction produces interference patterns: when waves pass through an apparatus, they meet, combine, and produce patterns that are not present in any single wave alone. The pattern registers the history of the encounter, not a representation of what was there before the encounter happened. As Barad writes, diffraction "does not produce 'the same' displaced, as reflection and refraction do" (Barad, 2007, citing Haraway). What it produces is an interference pattern: a mapping of the effects of differences, not a mapping of where differences are located in things.

Haraway had already named the choice between reflection and diffraction as a political and epistemological one. Reflexivity, she argues, "like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere, setting up the worries about copy and original and the search for the authentic and really real." What is needed instead is to "diffract the rays of technoscience so that we get more promising interference patterns on the recording films of our lives and bodies" (Haraway, 1997, p. 16). The shift from reflexivity to diffractivity is not a technical upgrade. It is a change in what research is understood to do: from reproducing a pre-given world to participating in the production of the world it is studying.

The phenomenon: Barad's basic ontological unit: is what makes this participation concrete. Phenomena are not objects observed by a detached researcher. They are material-discursive arrangements produced through specific intra-actions of apparatus, matter, and meaning. Relata do not pre-exist these arrangements; they emerge within them. The diffraction pattern is what the phenomenon looks like from inside the apparatus. There is no view from outside. The interference pattern: the research finding: is always a pattern produced by a specific encounter between specific forces at a specific moment. It is not less rigorous for that. It is more precisely accountable: accountable to the apparatus that produced it, to the cuts it enacted, to the marks it left.

### How Each Theorist Conceptualizes Diffractive Practice

The umbrella framing established above names a family of protocols that share ontological commitments without sharing a label. Within that family, each theorist has developed a distinct version of what the practice requires. The distinctions matter: not because they produce incompatible frameworks but because each version emphasizes different aspects of the same ground, and understanding what each one contributes makes the whole framework more navigable for a practitioner.

**Trinh T. Minh-ha: diffraction as filmmaking practice, before the name.** Trinh T. Minh-ha is treated in full in the previous section, as the practitioner whose film work is the origin point of the diffraction concept: the figure Haraway was reading in 1992 when she introduced diffraction as a feminist critical method. Her account there addresses all three questions simultaneously, confirming that Q1, Q2, and Q3 name not three separate domains but three angles of vision on a single practice. The practitioners gathered here extend that demonstration in studio, site, workshop, and collective registers.

Trinh is not a case study in the conventional sense. She does not appear in the diffraction literature as a theorist who conceptualized diffractive practice: she appears as the practitioner Haraway was reading when she introduced diffraction as a feminist critical concept. The genealogy runs: Trinh (filmmaker, 1982–1992) → Haraway (situates it theoretically, 1992–1997) → Barad (grounds it in quantum ontology, 2003–2007). That Trinh is a theorist-practitioner rather than a pure theorist does not diminish her position in this lineage. It confirms it. Diffraction begins where practice always begins: in the work itself, before the vocabulary exists to name what the work is doing.

**Barad: diffraction as material-discursive apparatus work.** For Barad, diffractive practice is inseparable from her concept of the apparatus. The apparatus is not a tool the researcher picks up and puts down. It is the material-discursive arrangement: the configuration of instruments, concepts, bodies, institutions, and practices: through which phenomena are produced. The researcher is always already part of the apparatus; there is no view from outside. Diffractive practice, in Barad's version, means making the apparatus explicit: naming it, specifying the agential cuts it makes, and tracing the marks it leaves on bodies. The agential cut is not a free interpretive choice: it is the determinate event through which boundaries are enacted, relata emerge, and accountability is located. Diffractive practice is rigorous precisely because it specifies these cuts rather than naturalizing them. The question it asks is not "what does this mean" but "what does this configuration of differences produce, and what does it foreclose." Its ethical force comes from the insistence that exclusions are constitutive: what the apparatus does not produce is as significant as what it does: and that the researcher is response-able for those constitutive exclusions (Barad, 2003; 2007).

**Haraway: diffraction as figuration and situated partial perspective.** Haraway introduces diffraction as a feminist optical metaphor before Barad formalizes it philosophically, and she does so in explicit dialogue with Trinh T. Minh-ha. In "The Promises of Monsters" (1992), Haraway quotes Trinh's formulation of "difference" as "a 'critical difference within,' and not as special taxonomic marks grounding difference as apartheid," and extends it: "Diffraction does not produce 'the same' displaced, as reflection and refraction do. Diffraction is a mapping of interference, not of replication, reflection, or reproduction. A diffraction pattern does not map where differences appear, but rather maps where the effects of differences appear" (Haraway, 1992, pp. 299–300). This is the origin of the diffraction metaphor in feminist theory: generated from a reading of Trinh, not from quantum physics alone. By 1997, in *Modest Witness*, Haraway develops diffraction into what Barad calls a "fourth semiotic category," connecting it to her broader critique of reflexivity: "reflexivity, like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere," whereas diffraction produces genuinely new interference patterns (Haraway, 1997, p. 16). Haraway's version of diffractive practice is inseparable from her commitment to figuration: representations that are "performative images that can be inhabited," partial maps accountable to their situatedness (Haraway, 1997, p. 11). Barad in turn takes Haraway's diffraction metaphor and grounds it in quantum field theory, making the metaphor into an ontological claim: phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, and diffraction is not only a way of reading but a description of how the world is constituted (Barad, 2003, citing Haraway, 1992, p. 300).

**Braidotti: transpositions as nomadic creative leap.** Braidotti does not use the term "diffractive practice" as her primary label, but her transpositions enact the same ontological commitments through a distinct vocabulary and procedural emphasis. Where Barad's version centers the apparatus and the agential cut, and Haraway's centers the situated partial perspective and figuration, Braidotti's centers movement, becoming, and the creative reconfiguration of imaginary formations. Transposition is drawn from music: the operation of playing the same piece in a different key, producing displacement and dissonance that opens new possibilities: and from genetics: the capacity of elements to move across and transform the field they enter. The transponent is a hybrid agent of change whose communication is transformative, actualizing latent/virtual affinities and altering the field it enters (Braidotti, 2006; 2011). Diffractive practice in Braidotti's version is nomadic: it is a refusal of fixed identity, a cartographic tracking of what we are ceasing to be, and an affirmative orientation toward what is becoming possible. The accountability structure is the nomadic ethics of location: the account is grounded in the specific political and material coordinates of the subject's position, not in an external canon of verification. Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025) operationalize this as six practices drawn from Braidotti's oeuvre: embracing a relational ontology of becoming, critiquing imaginary formations, preparing the creative leap, accounting for location and positioning, storying the in-between space, and developing new frames of resonance. These six practices are the authors' own operationalization: Braidotti does not prescribe this sequence herself: but they are accountable to Braidotti's theoretical commitments and serve as one worked example of what transpositions look like when converted into research methodology.

**Bennett: diffractive practice as perceptual and ethical retraining.** Bennett does not use the term "diffractive practice" either, but her account of what a vital materialist methodology requires is the clearest statement in the literature of what the practice demands as a personal and political orientation. For Bennett, the central obstacle is perceptual: the trained habit of seeing the world as consisting only of active human subjects and passive objects. Correcting this requires the four Adornian techniques: making conceptualization itself an explicit object of thought, attending to the qualitative singularities of the object, exercising the utopian imagination, and admitting the clownish element: as well as the specific comportments of lingering, openness, and anticipatory receptivity (*Vibrant Matter*, pp. 5, 16–18). Bennett's version of diffractive practice is the most explicit about the affective and ethical dimensions: the goal is not only epistemological accuracy but a reconfigured relation to the world, one that produces what she calls "enchantment": not a naive romanticism but the productive disorientation of discovering that the world is stranger and more agentive than the familiar frame allowed. This enchantment is the experiential condition for the political consequences that follow: if things have vitality, if agency is distributed, then the politics of domination that depends on a world of passive objects and active subjects is no longer self-evidently rational.

**Where they converge and where they diverge.** The four versions share an ontological ground (relational, material-discursive, distributed agency, ongoing becoming), a methodological posture (refusal of exhaustive capture, accountability to the apparatus and its cuts), and an ethical orientation (responsibility is located in the intra-action, not outsourced to an external canon). Where they diverge is in emphasis and entry point. Barad enters through physics and the apparatus: the most rigorous account of how phenomena are produced. Haraway enters through feminist technoscience and figuration: the most politically explicit account of whose knowledge counts and from where. Braidotti enters through philosophy and nomadic ethics: the most explicit account of how the subject navigates and transforms imaginary formations. Bennett enters through affect and perception: the most explicit account of what the practice demands of the practitioner as an embodied, sensing being. A researcher using this framework does not have to choose between these versions. She draws on all four, letting each contribute what it does best: Barad for apparatus specification and accountability, Haraway for situatedness and partial perspective, Braidotti for the creative leap and cartographic movement, Bennett for the perceptual and ethical retraining that makes any of the others possible.

### How Do We Comport Ourselves for this Reality

For Bennett, diffractive practice becomes a discipline of attending to thing-power, broadening the concept of self-interest, and cultivating a more horizontal account of human and nonhuman actants: because otherwise we remain trapped in the politics of domination produced by the subject-object split. Baradian diffraction pressures both posthumanism and new materialism to refuse representational shortcuts. The question then is not whether to comport ourselves differently but how.

Adorno, in Bennett's reading of Negative Dialectics, did not only diagnose the problem of nonidentity: the permanent gap between any thing and any concept of it. He laid out specific techniques for training oneself to detect and accept that gap rather than suppress it. These are not theoretical positions but practical comportments, intellectual and aesthetic exercises for navigating a world that will never go entirely into its concepts.

The first technique is to make conceptualization itself an explicit object of thought: to become aware that the process of concept-formation automatically obscures the inadequacy of its own products. Critical reflection can expose this cloaking mechanism. The exposure intensifies the felt presence of nonidentity and works against the hubris of conceptualization, producing what Bennett describes as a recursive or metacognitive thinking style: habitually questioning one's own thinking, remaining attentive to what any concept is leaving out. This is the ouroboran move: using reflection on conceptualization to loosen the grip of conceptualization.

The second technique is to attend to the qualitative singularities of the object: to resist flattening the thing into what the concept needs it to be, and instead stay with the specific, irreducible ways in which it pushes back. This is where the shift from reflection to diffraction lives: not mirroring the object back through a pre-given frame, but registering the interference pattern produced when the object's specific qualities meet the apparatus of attention.

The third technique is to exercise what Adorno calls the utopian imagination: to imaginatively recreate what conceptualization has suppressed, to recover "the possibility of which reality has cheated the objects and which is nonetheless visible in each one" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 16). The task is to take note of the invisible field that surrounds and infuses the world of objects and to return those denied possibilities to view.

The fourth technique is to admit a playful element into inquiry: to be willing, as Adorno puts it, to adhere to one's "clownish traits." The clown is both humble and brave: willing to present reality in configurations that others might read as nonsense, willing to be wrong in public, willing to loosen the prestige of mastery as a posture. This is the courage that queering, making-strange, and worrying-the-concept all require. Together, Bennett summarizes, the four techniques constitute "the self-criticism of conceptualization, a sensory attentiveness to the qualitative singularities of the object, the exercise of an unrealistic imagination, and the courage of a clown": a practice designed to replace the rage against nonidentity with a respect for it, a respect that chastens the will to mastery and can transmute the anguish of nonidentity into ameliorative political action (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 16).

The ethical consequence of these techniques is what Bennett calls lingering. Vital materialists "try to linger in those moments during which they find themselves fascinated by objects, taking them as clues to the material vitality that they share with them. This sense of a strange and incomplete commonality with the out-side may induce vital materialists to treat nonhumans: animals, plants, earth, even artifacts and commodities: more carefully, more strategically, more ecologically" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 18). Lingering is not passivity or delay. It is the active refusal to rush past an encounter before the thing has had time to register its own pressure on the inquiry. It is holding space for the object: deferring the imposition of higher-order meaning long enough for the assemblage to disclose what it is doing. The political stakes are direct: a diffractive practice that lingers is one that keeps noticing distributed agency rather than quarantining it inside the human, that resists the moralized politics of binary good and evil by staying with the complexity of confederate causation.

Guattari widens this from a practice of individual perceptual retraining to a structural claim about the conditions under which ecological thinking becomes possible at all. His argument in *The Three Ecologies* is that the problem we face is not simply environmental decay but a disease afflicting all three "ecological registers" simultaneously: the environmental, the social, and the mental (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 114). These are not discrete domains. They are, as Guattari insists, "interchangeable lenses or points of view" that together form a single whole: and any attempt to address one while leaving the others intact will not reach the depth required. The greening of the economy, the redistribution of wealth, the extension of rights: none of these can develop beyond the superficial without new modules of subjectification, new "micropolitical and microsocial practices, new solidarities, a new gentleness, together with new aesthetic and new analytic practices regarding the formation of the unconscious" (Guattari, qtd. in *Vibrant Matter*, p. 114). Guattari calls this thinking transversally: fixing the mind's eye on the interlacing of the mechanosphere, the social sphere, and the inwardness of subjectivity simultaneously, because these three worlds form a single whole.

Diffractive practice is the cultivation of that traversal style. It is not a method applied to a pre-given object. It is a trained orientation: perceptual, intellectual, aesthetic, and political at once: adequate to a world where the environmental, the social, and the mental are always already intra-acting, and where the source of effects is never a single agent but an assemblage whose distributed agency calls for a distributed response.

Adorno (in Bennett), in his Negative Dialectics, outlined specific steps  
necessary to navigate in a world where there is no essential nature,  
there is only an absolute that you will never reach. If all things/all  
arrangements of time/space/matter lack an identity, then they  
affirmatively do contain a non-identity. Their essential nature/identity  
is that they do not have one.

Step One is to realize that thought, or the creation of concepts, is a  
circular/ouroboran practice where the concept is a consequence of its  
own making, thus all concepts live somewhere between the absolute and  
our more traditional human definition of knowing.

Imagining yourself as the clown is Step Two. The clown is both humble  
and brave, willing to make themselves the fool by presenting reality in  
such wild configurations that others might construe it as nonsense. See  
queering, making strange, worrying, weirding.

Step Three is the most vital, as it is a change that must be made in a  
person's entire relation to the world. A person must be open to  
knowing, to noticing, what Bennett calls lingering. If a person does not  
wait with patience and instead participates in the endless already  
always knowing, they will miss any insights there are to be had. Bennett  
explicitly connects ethical behavior to "moments of sensuous  
enchantment" as catalysts. These moments aren't possible without an  
openness to lingering.

Training attention (perceptual discipline; cultivated sensory  
attentiveness)

Bennett explicitly frames method as requiring trained perception: she needs "a cultivated, patient, sensory attentiveness to nonhuman forces operating outside and inside the human body," and warns that without this "countercultural kind of perceiving," the world appears as only active human subjects confronting passive objects (Bennett, 2010). Part of the required method is a willingness "to theorize events (a blackout, a meal, an imprisonment in chains, an experience of litter) as encounters between ontologically diverse actants, some human, some not, though all thoroughly material" (Bennett, 2010).

### Differences That Make a Difference

Diffraction is the mapping of effects of difference rather than the locating of difference in things (Barad, 2003). This distinction: between difference as a property of objects and difference as the pattern produced by their intra-action: is what separates diffractive practice from comparative analysis. Comparison asks: how do these things differ? Diffraction asks: what interference pattern is produced when these things are read through one another, and what does that pattern make visible that neither could produce alone?

Bateson's formulation is the methodological anchor: information is "a difference that makes a difference" (1972, p. 459). Information is not content extracted from a source; it is the relational event of one difference registering as significant within another system. This is the same claim Barad makes about phenomena: the boundaries and properties of things are not given in advance but enacted through specific material-discursive arrangements. The diffraction pattern does not show what the things are. It shows what the encounter produces: which includes what it forecloses.

For research practice this means the unit of analysis is never a single text, dataset, or object in isolation. It is always the encounter: what happens when this theoretical framework is read through this practice, when this body of work is read through that ontological commitment, when this site is held against that history. The pattern that emerges from the encounter is where the knowledge lives. Bennett's actants, Barad's phenomena, Haraway's situated knowledges, Braidotti's transpositions: each names a version of the same methodological ground: the differences that matter are the ones the encounter produces, not the ones the researcher brings to it already formed.

### Protocols Under the Umbrella

The thinker-by-thinker account above describes how each major theorist has conceptualized diffractive practice at the level of orientation and commitment. What the practice looks like when operationalized as a protocol: when a researcher actually tries to work diffractively: has been developed by a range of practitioners working across fields, each arriving at similar structures through different routes.

Lambert (submitted) develops five conceptual entry points for diffractive practice in teacher education: affect, intra-action, becoming-with, assemblage, and spacetimemattering. Each is a conceptual tool derived from posthumanist inquiry that reorients the practitioner away from the humanist, linear, product-oriented logic of reflective practice and toward an "otherwise" practice adequate to the complex, relational, non-linear nature of teaching encounters. Crucially, Lambert does not offer these as a protocol to replace reflection and explicitly avoids a recipe for implementation. They are entry points: ways of beginning to think differently, not steps to follow. Diffraction in Lambert's framing is an orientation, not a procedure: "not an 'either/or' alternative but an 'otherwise', expanded way of thinking" (Lambert, submitted, p. 3). This is consistent with the broader literature: every serious account of diffractive practice warns against treating it as a recipe precisely because a recipe reinstalls the linear, product-oriented, closure-seeking logic the practice is designed to resist.

Hill (2017) makes the most useful structural distinction in the literature for practitioners: the difference between **diffractive reading** and **experimenting with a diffractive apparatus**. Diffractive reading occurs outside the event: it is a way of analyzing or interpreting material after the fact, reading texts, data, or experiences through one another to trace the interference patterns they produce. It is, Hill argues, analogous to Schön's reflection-on-action: retrospective, outside the immediate encounter, producing new understandings that may then inform practice. Experimenting with a diffractive apparatus occurs in situ: it is the practitioner's intra-action with other bodies during an educative or research event, contributing to particular configurations of reality as they unfold. This is analogous to reflection-in-action: embedded in the encounter, unfolding in real time, attending to what intra-action is producing rather than representing what was produced. Both are material-discursive practices that configure the world in particular ways; neither is merely interpretive. The distinction matters because it maps onto different modes of being a researcher: one retrospective and analytical, one embedded and generative. Hill identifies three modes of becoming a diffractive practitioner: becoming-with the world, displacing and diffracting the selves who teach, and embracing difference and interference (Hill, 2017, pp. 6–14). These three are not sequential steps but orientations that interpenetrate: each requires the others, and all three together constitute what it means to shift from a reflective to a diffractive stance.

Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025), working in organizational studies, operationalize Braidotti's transpositions as six practices: embracing alternative ways of seeing and understanding the world, assessing and critiquing imaginary forms, preparing to make the creative leap, accounting for locations and positioning, storying the in-between space, and developing new frames of resonance. As noted in the thinker-by-thinker account above, these six practices are the authors' own derivation from Braidotti's oeuvre: they are not steps Braidotti herself prescribes. This is an important methodological point: scholars build protocols from theoretical frameworks all the time, and the test of such a protocol is not whether the theorist endorsed it but whether it remains accountable to the theoretical commitments of the framework it derives from. Martin et al.'s practices do: each is grounded in Braidotti's relational ontology of becoming, her critique of imaginary formations, her nomadic subject position, and her affirmative ethical orientation. The protocol is an operationalization, not a transcription.

The Mosaic Approach (Clark and Moss, 2001), developed for listening research with young children, represents a different kind of case: a protocol that satisfies the umbrella criteria without using NM vocabulary and without having been derived from NM theory. It predates Barad's *Meeting the Universe Halfway* by six years and developed independently through three roots: participatory rural development, Reggio Emilia's pedagogy of listening and relationships, and a rights-based view of young children as experts in their own lives. Yet it enacts the same ontological commitments: knowledge is co-constructed between researcher and participant through multiple simultaneous modes; no single method or perspective has priority; the non-verbal, embodied, and material are data alongside the verbal; the research is ongoing and open-ended rather than closure-seeking; and the researcher is a visible part of the process rather than a neutral observer. Dudley (2024) brings the Mosaic Approach explicitly into contact with Braidotti's nomadic ethics and Barad's ethico-onto-epistemology, using the Mosaic tiles as a material-discursive apparatus and nomadic ethics as the accountability framework. That convergence is possible precisely because the Mosaic Approach was already doing diffractive practice before the label existed. This is the same pattern the document has traced elsewhere: practice arrives at the ground that theory then names. The Mosaic Approach is another instance of the argument that what posthumanism and new materialism discovered was not new: what they did was finally refuse to suppress it.

The convergence of these protocols under the umbrella: Barad's apparatus work, Haraway's figuration, Braidotti's transpositions, Bennett's perceptual retraining, Lambert's five entry points, Hill's two modes, Martin et al.'s six practices, Clark and Moss's mosaic listening: is not accidental. It is the signature of a shared ontological ground. Each arrived at similar structures from different domains, different vocabularies, and different institutional contexts. What they share is the refusal to treat knowledge as extraction from a pre-given world, and the insistence that accountability is located in the specific, specifiable apparatus of the inquiry rather than in correspondence to an external canon of truth. Research does not need to be checked against some outside verifiable standard. It needs internal consistency: coherence with its own ontological commitments and accountability to the apparatus, the cuts, and the marks it leaves on bodies (Militsi, 2021; Timeto, 2011).

### Diffractive Practice in Artistic Contexts

Parts One and Two have established the theoretical and methodological ground: a relational world that resists representationalism, and a family of diffractive practices adequate to inhabiting it. Before turning fully to the question of art practice as research, it is useful to pause over what these operators look like when the medium is not text or data but matter, encounter, installation, studio process, and making. In artistic contexts, diffraction, transposition, intra-action, making strange, queering, and seeding do not function as abstract concepts alone. They become compositional and methodological acts. The artist-researcher builds or enters an apparatus, works with matter already in motion, and learns from the interference patterns that emerge.

Parts One and Two have established the theoretical and methodological ground: a relational world that resists representationalism, and a family of diffractive practices adequate to inhabiting it. Part Three asks what it means to work as an artist-researcher from within that ground: what the studio and the workshop become as research apparatuses, what kinds of knowledge practice can produce, and how the specific operators of diffraction, transposition, intra-action, making strange, and seeding function when the medium is not text or data but matter, encounter, and making.

The entry point is ontological. Matter is not passive or awaiting signification from an external source: it is "always already an ongoing historicity" (Barad, 2003, p. 822). It does not require the mark of culture or history to complete it. The implication for art practice as research is immediate: working with matter: attending to it, making with it, being rearranged by it: is already a form of knowing. The artist-researcher does not impose meaning on inert material. She enters into an intra-action with matter that was already in motion.

Language and representationalism carry too much institutional power in the evaluation of research. The assumption that understanding a thing requires its abstraction into a claim: its reduction to a signifier that can travel from context to context: is the assumption new materialist APAR is built to complicate. Knowledge is phenomenological, participatory, open-ended, emergent, entangled, generative. What grounds this is not a preference for the experiential over the propositional. It is ontology: if the world is constituted through intra-action, then any knowledge adequate to it must be produced through intra-action, not extracted from it after the fact.

The sections that follow map this terrain through the questions that organize new materialist APAR: What is research? What is art practice as research? What is new materialist art practice as research specifically? What do the practitioners who work within this ground actually do, and how do they articulate what their doing produces?

#### Transpositions: Braidotti, Schwab, Pirrò, Kirkkopelto, and Martin et al.

Transposition is the operator that does what diffraction cannot do alone. Diffraction reads interference patterns: it attends to what a relational arrangement produces, traces the effects of differences rather than differences themselves, and names the apparatus that generated the phenomenon. Transposition builds the conditions that make new patterns possible. It is the operator that actualizes latent and virtual affinities in the surrounding field, transforming what enters rather than merely mapping what is. Together, diffraction and transposition name the two movements new materialist art practice as research requires: finding what the apparatus produces, and becoming the apparatus that produces something new.

Braidotti develops transposition in the "Transformations" chapter of the Transpositions volume through its double etymological root. The first is musical: as a term in music, transposition indicates variations and shifts of scale in a discontinuous but harmonious pattern, and it is "created as an in-between space of zigzagging and of crossing: nonlinear, but not chaotic; nomadic, yet accountable and committed; creative but also cognitively valid; discursive and also materially embedded: it is coherent without falling into instrumental rationality" (Braidotti, 2018, p. 27). The second is genetic: in molecular biology, transposable elements are sequences that move across and transform the genome, neither copying nor simply relocating but introducing change by entering a new context. Together the metaphors name the same operation: a cross-boundary transfer that is not merely quantitative multiplication but qualitative transformation, playing "the positivity of difference as a specific theme of its own" (Braidotti, 2018, p. 27). The ethical dimension is inseparable from the methodological. Braidotti's non-unitary subjectivity: "a nomadic, dispersed, fragmented vision, which is nonetheless functional, coherent, and accountable, mostly because it is embedded and embodied": finds its methodological expression here: transposition deals with the implications of that vision in terms of accountability, ethical and political agency (Braidotti, 2018, p. 26).

Schwab's Introduction to the volume identifies what distinguishes transposition in artistic research from its uses elsewhere. The logic of representation is singular, remaining the same across different instances. The logic of transposition is multiple, needing to be transposed from instance to instance: a positional specificity determined in space, time, or otherwise, which explains both why transpositional operations have been so difficult to approach philosophically and why artistic research, which is sensitive to the specifics of what is at hand, may present new options (Schwab, 2018, pp. 7–8). The key claim is about the point of origin: other researchers: Rheinberger, Malabou: need historically established concepts to work from, foregrounding deconstructive approaches while keeping a certain degree of distance. Artistic researchers have the option to "jump right in, simply appropriating or inventing concepts whose plasticity can be supplied by artistically staging them as epistemic things. If they work, new understanding will be gained; if they fail to work, we may encounter forced acting, incoherent scripts, and pointless monologues. In the absence of deeper historical grounding, there is no other choice but to take this risk" (Schwab, 2018, pp. 8–9). What changes when research moves into art is not the operations themselves but their point of origin. This is why Schwab frames artistic research as increasingly needed in other research domains: today something "artistic" seems needed to realize the complicated relationships we have with knowledge objects (Schwab, 2018, p. 9). The operations themselves may not be exclusive to artistic research: but the willingness to stage the concept as a thing and live with what the staging produces is.

Kirkkopelto's chapter "Abandoning Art in the Name of Art" develops transposition as a methodological hypothesis for artistic research: the logic of what actually happens when an artistic procedure, technique, or concept is shifted from the arts field where it was developed into another field where it has no institutional status. The initiating act requires an expert: the artist who recognizes, whether by foresight or accident, an affinity between two relatively autonomous fields. What matters in transposition is not to break the limits but to discover affinities "that are not based on identity": material, compositional, medial similarities; ways of producing and reproducing reality and encountering other realities (Kirkkopelto, 2018, p. 37). Through active maintenance, the transposed element charges itself with energy and becomes the transponent: and agency transfers from the transposer to the transposed concept or technique. Within its new field, the transponent is at first a stranger, even an intruder, but carries something unheimlich: strangely familiar, attractive. The surrounding field may reject the transponent but cannot reject the affinity it establishes, which already has a foothold in the new terrain. The constant tension between belonging and not belonging both protects the transponent against assimilation and provides it with energy. Its communication is not informative but transformative: "whatever the transponent 'says,' or what is 'said about it,' changes the surroundings. Neither is it a medium: it does not translate the events of one field into the events of another. It does not bring messages or represent the fields that it puts in indirect communication" (Kirkkopelto, 2018, p. 38). The changes that follow are unpredictable. In accordance with Deleuzian modal logic, the transponent makes features appear from the surrounding field that until that moment had been virtual and latent; the explication of that virtual affinity, its actualization, necessarily transforms the field (Kirkkopelto, 2018, citing Deleuze, 1968, pp. 269–276). The transposer becomes artist-researcher as a consequence of the act, not as a precondition for it. That is unavoidable: the consequences of the transpositional act always remain to be seen, explained, and expressed: and expressing them requires research.

Pirrò's "Staging Collisions: On Behaviour" sharpens the apparatus side. He opens with diffraction as a physical phenomenon: waves colliding with an obstacle, losing coherent image, reconstructing as interference patterns: and then applies this to the concept of transposition itself: "I regard this text as a diffraction. The object being diffracted is the concept of transposition and the obstacle, the slit that it encounters, is the need to formulate this very text" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 136). His most precise contribution is the *facta* argument. Data are not given: they exist only in relation and interaction with the experiment performed, the research questions, the particular measuring devices used, and the analysis methods applied. From this perspective, data is less a stable result and more a complex compound of all those factors that cannot be separated from it: data might as well have a double nature of *facta*, "created, produced, and emerging from the interactions between all the technological and theoretical tools that scientists employ" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 145). Data has a double nature as facta because it is simultaneously the output of a material process and the expression of the theoretical and instrumental framework that produced it. Transposing a dataset therefore means recovering its context and the net of interactions so that the transposed becomes an active phenomenon emerging from and affording new relations. The resulting transpositions resist integration: they cannot be brought into a univocal relationship with the conditions that generated them, which cannot be entirely known. They resist stabilization into a finite propositional formulation. Relations and differences remain a network of interacting positions that are "incompressible; they continue to be a network of differential interacting positions" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 144). There is no defined common basis linking one transposition to another or to its original material: it is not translation into a shared metric but a conversion of the conditions of experience itself.

Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025) operationalize Braidotti's transpositions as six practices in the context of organizational studies and instructional learning, drawing on feminist posthumanist methodology for research into workplace culture, leadership formations, and organizational change. Their account is the fullest systematic derivation of transpositions as research protocol in the current literature, and the six practices they identify are worth unpacking in full: not as prescriptive steps but as a map of the intellectual and methodological work the framework requires.

The first practice is embracing alternative ways of seeing and understanding the world: reorienting before inquiry begins. An ontology of becoming implies that it is the nature of all life to become and to keep doing so through the embedded and embodied relations that produce affects. Affect is "the change, or variation, that occurs when bodies collide, or come into contact": an "experiential force" that propels processes of becoming and that is not limited to human bodies (Colman, 2010, pp. 11–12, cited in Martin et al., 2025). The challenge this practice names is that humanist dualisms and a unitary vision of the autonomous subject continue to "act with force in the context of everyday organizational relations" even when they are not consciously endorsed: they are embedded in the stories people tell, the terms they use, the identities they value. The first practice is the decision to see this: and to see differently.

The second practice is assessing and critiquing imaginary formations: identifying "the choreography of constraints and entitlements" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 86) that imaginary forms: constellations of images, ideas, values, representations, and narratives: materialize for differently historied bodies. The assessment is not of the imaginary form directly but of its effects: what ways of relating it endorses or precludes, what identities it confers or regulates, how it increases or restricts the relational capacities of subjects within their specific locations. Martin et al.'s exemplar is "heroic leadership" as an imaginary formation sustained by masculinist assumptions even in contexts where men are physically absent.

The third practice is preparing to make the creative leap: cultivating "generative cross-pollinations" across multiple and often seemingly unconnected theoretical domains, genres, modes of apprehension, and discursive communities (Braidotti, 2017, p. 83). The researcher embodies the identity of the nomad: a traveler who suspends attachment to established discourses and follows "a map of her own making" (Braidotti, 2011, p. 165). This is the "consumption of the old in order to engender the new" (Braidotti, 2011, p. 68): not inversion or replacement of existing forms but movement into genuinely different ontological premises, where a fertile imagination and a clear ethico-political stance are the operative tools.

The fourth practice is accounting for locations and positioning. Braidotti's phrase "history tattooed on your body" (2003, p. 54) names the multi-faceted embeddedness of historicity: characterized by different forms and experiences of oppression, empowerment, and difference: that the researcher brings to any inquiry. A feminist politics of location insists on the multiplicity of intersecting differences within and between subjects: race, ethnicity, class, sexuality, age, dis/ability, lifestyle, religion. The dimensions the researcher chooses to focus on are always already influenced by her positioning and specific concerns.

The fifth practice is storying the in-between space: the work of experimentation and creation. The researcher brings her "hybrid mixture of codes, genres, or modes of apprehension" (Braidotti, 2011, p. 225) to story different, more empowering rhythms for existing cultural formations: through new coordinates that enable "a deflection and broadening, an opening up rather than a closing down" of existing forms (Grosz, 2005, p. 165, cited in Martin et al., 2025). Martin et al. use feminist speculative fiction: Le Guin, Griffith: as the site where transpositions practices are embedded in narrative form. Speculative fiction is significant here not as decoration but as a research mode: it "defamiliarizes our normative conceptions of existence, nature and the human" (Grebowicz & Merrick, 2013, p. 113) in exactly the way Making Strange does as an art operation.

The sixth practice is developing new frames of resonance: proposing more adequate frameworks for imaginary formations that reflect the creative leaps made in the storying practice. New words, terms, and frames "unsettle the 'old' or accepted meanings and practices" associated with imaginary forms and "signal the possibilities for other ways of thinking, acting and responding" (Martin et al., 2025, p. 513, citing Truman, 2019). Transposition practices produce "generative cracks" in hegemonic systems and in dominant social imaginaries, bringing forth affirmative alternatives for thought and practice (Martin et al., 2025).

The institutional significance of the Martin et al. contribution should not be understated. They demonstrate that transpositions methodology works in organizational studies and instructional learning contexts: domains not traditionally associated with artistic research. This is the transversal movement at work: transpositions travel across disciplines because they enact their own claim of the co-constitution of all things. The six practices are fully portable. They can be used to generate the new and to produce "generative cracks" wherever imaginary formations are shaping what is possible and what is not: including in institutions that evaluate what counts as research.

#### Making Strange, Queering, and the NM Extension of Defamiliarization

Making strange has a specific theoretical genealogy that runs through literary theory, theater, and into new materialist art practice. Viktor Shklovsky (1917) introduced ostranenie: defamiliarization: as the defining operation of art: art exists to restore the experience of life, to make us feel things rather than merely recognize them. The technique of art is to make objects unfamiliar, to make forms difficult, to increase the length and difficulty of perception. What has become automatic and invisible is rendered strange so that it can be seen again. Bertolt Brecht translated this into theatrical practice as the Verfremdungseffekt: the estrangement effect: a set of techniques for preventing the audience from losing itself in the illusion of the performance, keeping them aware that they are watching a construction, forcing critical distance rather than identification. For Brecht, estrangement is not a failure of art but its most powerful political tool: to make the familiar strange is to reveal that it could be otherwise, that what appears natural and necessary is in fact historical and contingent.

New materialist art practice inherits and extends this tradition but shifts its target. Where Shklovsky and Brecht use making strange primarily to defamiliarize human social relations and cultural forms, new materialist making strange extends the operation to the nonhuman. This is not a minor adjustment. The NM extension changes what the operation is for: the goal is not critical distance from a cultural illusion but perceptual access to matter's own agency. Making strange in the NM register is what clears the trained perceptual habits: Bennett's "already-always-perceiving of objects": that suppress the vitality, the thing-power, the ongoing becoming that was always there. Andersen names this directly: making things stranger creates space to inhabit, space she claims as personal, as her own: the possibility space in which something new becomes thinkable (Andersen, 2016, p. 47). The strange object is not an end. It is the condition of the fog chamber: a mentally and emotionally fluid environment, a readiness for encounter.

Adorno supplies the fourth technique that connects this practice to the broader methodological framework. The clownish willingness to present reality in configurations others might read as nonsense: to admit that the ordinary is not the necessary, to hold the world's contingency in view long enough for it to begin to look like the possibility space it actually is: is the ethical and perceptual ground from which making strange operates. Bennett's cultivation of enchantment is the same move: the productive disorientation of discovering that the world is stranger and more agentive than the familiar frame allowed. Enchantment is not naive. It is the practiced suspension of the default frame that suppresses the vitality of matter. Making strange is how that suspension is enacted in art practice.

Schwab's multistable-image logic extends this further. Representation can be treated as a breakdown that collapses multiple potentials; artistic research operates in the intertwinedness of knowledge and non-knowledge, emphasizing the "active state": operations: rather than result. A transpositional shift produces an encounter that cannot be reduced back into stable representation: no shared coordinates, no common basis. Making strange is therefore not an aesthetic strategy applied after the research is done. It is an epistemic operator. Defamiliarization of anthropocentric assumptions about nonhuman matter is simultaneously the epistemological intervention and the political one.

Pereira and Bruggave's *Becoming a Tomato*: discussed in the Torosyan et al. track: demonstrates what this looks like when the target is colonial and anthropocentric scientific thinking about plant life. Using Latour's notion of blackboxing to render "opaque" the agency of nonhuman matter, the work incorporates computational art, performance, and implosive writing as a multidisciplinary framework (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 58). Motivated by Haraway's proposal to rewrite the story of the world, and resonant with the Brechtian Verfremdungseffekt in reversing the roles of nature and history, eternity and causality, the work aims at the disentanglement of bodily knowledge "where the binary borders between human and non-human, self and other, natural and cultural are blurred in a process of co-becoming" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 58). Making strange here is the research operation itself.

Queering: reading texts against their grain, refusing the expected protocol of engagement, letting materials speak in registers the method was not designed to register: is making strange applied to the research encounter itself. Andersen describes not reading texts "properly" and not being sure she knows how to. For her, texts "are like images, like scraps of colour and texture": they have thing-power, carried around for years as if in an always-wearing pocket, taken out, re-examined, reconsidered. Over time and space and matter the texts come back like curses, the meaning changes as she "gropes toward the preponderance of the object," lingering, as the *there, there* of the text recedes into the agential swarm (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). This is not a deficient reading. It is a methectic one: the text encountered as material that does things to the reader rather than as a container of meanings the reader extracts.

Franinović queers the text-material boundary in the opposite direction: preparing seeds for material events by mapping concepts, materials, and techniques onto each other, choreographing timing so that material and conceptual processes co-emerge rather than one governing the other. The workshop becomes a third space where diverse parties produce shared knowledge through a shift away from purely verbal techniques toward non-linguistic collaborative experience with physical artifacts (Franinović, 2016). The question shifts from what does the text mean to what can this material seed.

Making strange and queering are, in this framing, two aspects of the same onto-epistemological refusal: the refusal to let the familiar protocol exhaust what the encounter can produce. Both operate on the assumption that the most interesting knowledge lives in what the default frame cannot register: in the interval Trinh holds open, in the possibility space Andersen builds, in the agential swarm that Bennett cultivates the readiness to notice. The NM extension of defamiliarization is the discovery that the world has been strange all along, and that the work of art practice as research is to build the conditions for noticing it.

Read this way, artistic practice does not sit outside the methodological field traced by Barad, Haraway, Braidotti, and Bennett. It intensifies that field by rendering diffractive and transpositional operations materially palpable. The studio, workshop, performance site, and installation become places where interference, estrangement, and relational becoming are not only described but enacted.

### Limitations and Cautions

Fox & Alldred also provide an essential caution: diffractive methodology  
can become highly researcher-centric and context-dependent if analytic  
cuts are treated as interpretive license rather than accountable  
apparatus-work. They note that DM may be "researcher-centric and  
context-dependent" and that this raises concerns about transferability  
and applicability in policy/practice contexts.

Their abstract similarly points to the challenge of elucidating how  
analytic diffractions influence findings.

This caution is not a reason to abandon diffractive practice. It is a  
reason to insist on the strictest version of diffractive accountability:  
specify the apparatus, specify the cuts, and trace the effects, so that  
situatedness does not become a free pass and context sensitivity does  
not become methodological opacity.

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn warn against a second risk: that bringing new materialism into a discipline as if both are pre-existing tends to undo transversality, producing "inter- rather than intra-acting" relations and reinstalling a transcendental schism.

The methodological demand this raises is not abstract. If diffractive practice must be genuinely transversal rather than merely interdisciplinary, then its passage into artistic practice should not be treated as an illustrative afterthought. Artistic contexts make the stakes of apparatus, situatedness, and material co-constitution especially visible. Even so, a second distinction remains necessary. Part Two has addressed the family of methods and operators adequate to a relational world. Part Three turns more specifically to the next question: how new materialist theorists conceptualize art practice itself as research: what counts as inquiry, evidence, and knowledge when the researcher is also a maker.

## Part Three: New Materialist Art Practice as Research

Part Three narrows the frame from methodological family to specific epistemic claim. The question is no longer only how diffractive or transpositional practice works, but why art practice itself counts as research under new materialist conditions. If matter is active, if the apparatus is constitutive, and if knowledge is produced through intra-action rather than detached representation, then the studio, workshop, performance, and installation are not merely sites where theory is illustrated after the fact. They are research environments in their own right.

The sections that follow therefore focus less on naming operators and more on specifying artistic research as a material-discursive mode of inquiry: what research is, how art practice becomes research, what kinds of knowledge such practice can produce, and how artists articulate that knowing without collapsing it back into a purely representational model.

### What is Research?

Fox and Alldred (2021) define research as an assemblage made of tools, recording and analysis technologies, software and hardware, theoretical frameworks, literatures, researchers, editors, ethics committees, end users, and the data outputs: where outputs "derive from the multiple affects between these human and non-human materialities." Under an agential realist account, this definition of research gains  
additional precision. Phenomena: the basic units of reality for Barad:
"are not the mere result of laboratory exercises engineered by human  
subjects. Nor can the apparatuses that produce phenomena be understood  
as observational devices or mere laboratory instruments" (Barad, 2003,  
p. 815). Research is not the neutral observation of pre-existing objects  
but the co-constitution of phenomena through specific  
material-discursive arrangements. The apparatus is not separate from  
what it produces; it is part of the phenomenon itself. This means that  
the decisions a researcher makes: what apparatus to build, what cut  
to enact, what to attend to and what to exclude: are not  
methodological conveniences but ontological acts. Research, on this  
account, is world-making.

Agency, correspondingly, is not something the researcher possesses and  
applies. "Agency is a matter of intra-acting; it is an enactment, not  
something that someone or something has" (Barad, 2003, p. 826). It is  
"about the possibilities and accountability entailed in reconfiguring  
material-discursive apparatuses of bodily production including the  
boundary articulations and exclusions that are marked by those practices  
in the enactment of a causal structure." Research as assemblage: the  
Fox & Alldred formulation: and research as agential intra-action:  
the Baradian formulation: are naming the same condition from  
different angles: the researcher is inside the phenomenon, not above it.

Rheinberger frames research as an "epistemic encounter" within  
experimental systems where the object of inquiry is not fully known in  
advance: what he calls "epistemic things": and where research  
trajectories are non-teleological: driven from behind rather than  
oriented toward a predetermined omega (Rheinberger, Raw Flows, p. 17).  
Studios and laboratories are, in this account, comparable working  
environments: not identical, but analogous in their capacity to allow  
the fruitful interaction between the known and the not-yet-known that  
characterises research proper. What becomes knowable is not independent  
of the material arrangements that make inquiry possible; it depends  
entirely on how instruments transform matter to render phenomena  
accessible. In X-ray crystallography, fluid biological material must be  
made crystalline to enter the research regime. In electron microscopy,  
vacuum conditions force liquids to evaporate and require elaborate  
preparation that produces replicas rather than originals. The  
boundary-work: wet/dry, hard/soft, living/nonliving: is not  
incidental to the research; it is where the research happens. For new  
materialist art practice as research, the implication is immediate: the  
studio is not a private expressive room waiting to be supplemented by  
scholarly commentary. It is an experimental system that materially  
conditions what can appear, be handled, and be known.

Moñivas Mayor pushes this apparatus logic into explicitly studio-based  
terrain by arguing that when the object of inquiry is fluid processes  
and fluid imaginations, method must itself become fluid. The studio/lab,  
in her account, is an ecology: a permeable environment of maps, notes,  
vessels, instruments, and experiments always in readiness, where the  
methodological inventory includes testing observation points,  
researching theoretical sources, experimenting with documentation  
formats (field notebook, sketch, photo, video), and long durations of  
waiting, meals, and walks that contextualize what gets produced (Moñivas  
Mayor, Raw Flows, p. 112). Her key methodological claim is that fluid  
rhythms and unpredictability do not merely complicate research: they  
reshape how practice must be conceptualized and how it must be analyzed.  
She draws on the Deborah number to show why conventional observation  
regimes produce "solids" out of processes: normal timeframes make a  
solid of any substance, which forces a rethinking of object-centered  
epistemologies (Moñivas Mayor, Raw Flows, pp. 112--114). The studio/lab  
becomes the place where this crisis is lived as a research condition:  
learning to look and listen from the blurry margins of rationality  
rather than resolving it through abstraction. Crucially, she treats  
accidents as epistemic events: a spill produces an unanticipated form  
that becomes a new priority and redirects the inquiry. Rather than  
eliminating such noise, she argues for methodological distance from  
exactness to admit surprise, play, and reflection, insisting that  
analysis must combine documentation with tool and material knowledge and  
imagination (Moñivas Mayor, Raw Flows, p. 122). Her culminating  
formulation names the whole laboratory as the epistemic medium itself: a  
"condenser of flows and mutations" and a "catalyst of imagination,  
experimentation and knowledge." The studio/lab is not where research  
happens and is then written up. It is the apparatus through which  
research happens at all.

### What is Art Practice as Research?

Kirkkopelto (2015) provides the most precise account of what distinguishes artistic research from art practice. The distinguishing feature is not medium, not institutional location, and not the presence of a written reflection: it is the conversion of the artistic medium into a medium of research. When that conversion happens, the artist becomes "the primary expert of the medium that she herself has created" (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49), responsible not only for the work but for making its consequences traceable and communicable. A medium is not only a path or method but also the material and technical ground on which that path is traced: a place for placing and a happening. As an artistic medium changes into a medium of research, it moves toward the unknown while remaining unable to fully accomplish the transition it communicates: "it remains suspended between two stages; it can only suggest the transition by repeating it endlessly but always anew" (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49). This conditionality is not a failure. It is what makes research in this mode distinct: always oriented toward something it cannot yet fully articulate.

Transposition is the name Kirkkopelto gives to the logic of this shift, drawing on two sources. From music, transposition indicates variation and displacement that produces new harmonics without abandoning the original structure: nomadic yet accountable. From genetics, it draws on Barbara McClintock's work on transposable elements: the organism is the host environment of the genetic sequence and plays an interactive, determining role in what gets transmitted. Together these sources explain why transposition carries both nomadic drift and material embodiment. It is not merely conceptual movement but a materially situated operation in which the host environment: the organism, the medium, the artist: shapes the very information being transposed. As Braidotti formulates it, transposition is "a qualitative leap that produces a materially embedded in-between space" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 26). Haraway makes the genetic logic explicit in a passage Braidotti cites directly: "A gene is not a thing, much less a master molecule, or a self-contained code. Instead, the term 'gene' signifies a mode of durable action where many actors, human and non-human, meet" (Haraway, 1997, p. 142, cited in Braidotti, 2006, p. 27). Neither medium nor artist pre-exists the transposition as a stable entity. Both emerge transformed. Both act as apparatus.

Transposition makes research necessary because consequences are not guaranteed in advance. When a procedure, device, or concept shifts fields, it enters what Kirkkopelto calls an "afterlife" whose outcomes are unpredictable and must be investigated rather than assumed (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49). This is what distinguishes artistic research from artistic practice: not the institutional address, but the commitment to making the transposition's consequences traceable. The result must establish itself discursively in relation to existing practices and account for how it changes the state of affairs: specifically, whether it achieves invention rather than mere innovation. Kirkkopelto's criterion for evaluation is: to what extent can the outcome take into account and reclaim its own instituting aspects and responsibility for them?

The artist-researcher, on this account, is herself a transponent: a hybrid who does not surrender to either side of the transposition and does not fuse them. She makes latent/virtual affinities appear in the surrounding field that were previously unactualized, through the structure of the transposition itself rather than through special authority. The medium and the artist are both apparatus. Neither is sovereign. What they produce together: the diffraction pattern of their encounter: is the research. Schwab names the risk: if the transposition works, new understanding will be gained; if it fails, the outcome is forced acting, incoherent scripts, and pointless monologues. In the absence of deeper historical grounding, there is no other choice but to take this risk (Schwab, in *Transpositions*, 2018). The artist-researcher does not apply an established method to a question. She stages the conditions under which the question and its possible answers become thinkable.

Kirkkopelto's criterion names a standard that practitioner-researchers were meeting before the institutional framework for artistic research existed. Trinh T. Minh-ha is the clearest example. Working as a filmmaker, composer, writer, and theorist from the 1980s onward, Trinh produced work that refuses every available disciplinary container while being rigorously accountable to the conditions of its own production. Anthropologists, film studies scholars, postcolonial theorists, and feminist theorists all claimed her; she refused all of them as complete containers without abandoning any as partial resources. "Anthropology is just one site of discussion among others in my work," she says (Trinh, 1992, p. 83): what matters is not the objects but the relationships drawn between them.

In "Documentary Is/Not a Name" (1990), Trinh names the governing fantasy of documentary filmmaking: that the camera gives direct access to reality, that truth can be caught without the mediating activity of the apparatus: as the Cartesian division between subject and object that perpetuates "a dualistic inside-versus-outside, mind-against-matter view of the world" (Trinh, 1990, p. 83). Against this she insists on the interval: "what persists between the meaning of something and its truth is the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). The methodology she develops from this commitment is speaking nearby rather than about: a speaking that can come very close to a subject "without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, p. 87). It is a practice of partial, situated, non-exhaustive knowledge, developed in film and writing prior to and independent of the NM/PH vocabulary that would later name it. Speaking nearby is not technique. It is an attitude: a way of positioning oneself in relation to the world that must be materialized in every element of the work: verbal, musical, visual.

Trinh's position in this document is specific. She is not a theorist who discusses art practice as research. She is an artist-researcher who was practicing the refusal of exhaustive capture, the interval, the accountability of the apparatus, and the responsibility of the instituting gesture: all before those terms entered academic circulation. The citational chain is now traceable: Trinh articulates the "critical difference within" and the practice of speaking nearby in the late 1980s and early 1990s; Haraway (1992) quotes Trinh directly and develops the diffraction metaphor from that encounter; Barad (2003) quotes Haraway (1992) and grounds the metaphor in quantum field theory as an ontological claim. What began as a filmmaker's methodology becomes a feminist theoretical metaphor and then a physical ontology. The chain runs backward from theory to practice. Restoring Trinh to the genealogy of APAR changes what the question is: from how artistic activity meets academic standards of knowledge-production, to how practices that were already producing knowledge get recognized as doing so by the institutions that initially excluded them.

### What is New Materialist Art Practice As Research?

A new materialist art practice as research is a generative process of  
co-creating with material a diffraction grating that produces patterns  
that would not appear through other methods/frames. The boundaries are  
the consequences of the grating's design, providing a tool for research  
accountability for the "constitutive exclusions."(Barad,  
2003_Posthumanist) Each decision is a cut that together produces a  
locally determinate diffractive pattern.

The insistence on "co-creating with material" is not metaphor. It is an  
ontological commitment grounded in what Barad calls the mutual  
entailment of the material and the discursive: "The relationship between  
the material and the discursive is one of mutual entailment. Neither is  
articulated/articulable in the absence of the other; matter and meaning  
are mutually articulated. Neither discursive practices nor material  
phenomena are ontologically or epistemologically prior. Neither can be  
explained in terms of the other. Neither has privileged status in  
determining the other" (Barad, 2003, p. 822). For new materialist art  
practice as research, this means that the material the artist works with  
is not a passive medium awaiting the inscription of the artist's idea.  
It is a co-constitutive force whose specific resistances, affordances,  
textures, and behaviors shape what becomes thinkable and sayable through  
the encounter. The diffraction grating is not designed and then  
executed. It emerges through the intra-action.

The Enchantment of Textiles gives a full-scale case study of this  
apparatus logic in an interdisciplinary lab setting. It begins with  
museum research into historic embroidered metalwork: materials,  
techniques, cultural meanings: and then translates those histories  
into responsive textile systems using sensors, microcontrollers, textile  
antennas, and embedded circuits. The catalog explicitly frames the lab  
as a collaborative assemblage of artists, engineers, and researchers,  
with textiles as performative tools rather than passive media. Agency is  
distributed explicitly: viewers can "play the sorcerer," but quickly  
discover that power is in fact distributed and accumulates only through  
the specific arrangement of surrounding objects, citing Bennett's  
heterogeneous assemblage claim directly. The textiles are not merely  
objects to be looked at; they "solicit human perception" while also  
"perceiv\\[ing\\] and interact\\[ing\\] with one another," broadcasting and  
receiving via antennas and responsive components. Research-creation of  
this kind, the catalog insists, "cannot be deemed successful in simple  
or finite terms" and does not yield a singular discovery but instead  
"opens up new fields of understanding": a research outcome to be  
assessed through unfolding interactions and capacities rather than  
through closure. The "Critical Methodologies" section of the same  
catalog makes the research method explicit: sensory ethnography as an  
experimental iterative-inductive process for studio/lab environments,  
producing knowledge through embodied sensory engagement and explicitly  
refusing universality. Neither human nor material agency is privileged;  
what matters is the temporally emergent influence of ongoing practice.  
"Tuning" becomes the descriptor for shared adjustment across the  
research team and the researcher's active role in learning: goals  
redefined, decisions shared, embodiments of practice emerging in real  
time through sensory engagement with material systems.

SymbioticA provides a different inflection of the same apparatus logic,  
one in which the institutional entanglements of lab-based art practice  
are not incidental to the work but constitute it. SymbioticA is  
described as an artistic research laboratory inside a university, where  
artists work in scientific laboratories with scientific techniques and  
staff. What counts as the "work" in a SymbioticA project is not only the  
material outcome but the processes that make it possible: securing  
permissions from ethics committees, negotiating agreements with lab  
managers, arranging ongoing care responsibilities. The work consists,  
then, of these processes: and it produces a difference to science  
precisely by following scientific methods while staging that difference  
as the research contribution. This is a strong formulation of new  
materialist ethics as infrastructure: responsibility is not a moral  
sentiment attached to the research after the fact, but a network of  
material practices: protocols, caretaking, institutional permissions,  
risk exposure: that condition what research can occur and what lives  
are involved. The political context is not bracketed: the vulnerability  
of biology-based art practice to state intervention (the Kurtz case) and  
the framing of contemporary biotechnology institutions as a "society of  
control" are explicit stakes of the work.

Transposition names the recomposing side of this: not merely reading or seeing patterns, but building the conditions that make new patterns possible. Transposition is the logic of a shift itself: what is shifted becomes a transponent, a hybrid agent of change whose communication is transformative, actualizing latent/virtual affinities and transforming the field it enters. Diffraction and transposition together constitute the full research movement: diffraction traces the interference patterns the apparatus produces; transposition stages the conditions that make new patterns possible. Neither can do the other's work.

Diffraction as a research method creates rigor through the ongoing  
acknowledgement of the agential cuts. Diffractive rigor means: I name  
the apparatus, name the cut, and describe what the pattern makes visible  
and what it necessarily renders quiet.

Art practice becomes research through the creation of an apparatus that  
materially conditions what becomes perceptible as a  
product/artifact/data. Apparatuses are "exclusionary practices of  
mattering through which intelligibility and materiality are  
constituted."

(Barad,2003,Post)The apparatus does not provide representational  
objectivity, but a boundary space whose cuts can be specified, tuned,  
compared, and held accountable.

Artistic research develops through operations, not representation: practice-as-research is not illustration but epistemic movement generated by staged operations and their shifts. Transposition, in the precise definition that runs through Braidotti and Kirkkopelto, is a creative leap that opens an in-between space: zigzagging, crossing: where difference is not reduced to identity. It operates through a transponent whose communication is transformative: actualizing latent/virtual affinities, transforming the field it enters. The apparatus does not provide representational objectivity but a boundary space whose cuts can be specified, tuned, compared, and held accountable.

### The Knowing Is in the Doing: Artist-Researchers Who Bridge the Questions

The second and third comps questions ask, separately, how posthumanist theorists have conceptualized diffractive practice and how new materialist theorists have conceptualized art practice as research. The practitioners gathered here refuse that separation. Their work does not apply posthumanist or new materialist theory to art practice from the outside. It enacts the ontological commitments of those frameworks in the doing itself: and then names what the doing produces as knowledge. The knowing and the doing are one movement, differentiated only in the telling.

This is the onto-epistemological inseparability Barad names as ethico-onto-epistemology: responsibility for the practices of research (ethics), that which is observed (ontology), and the research apparatus (epistemology) are not three separate concerns that a researcher balances. They are entangled from the start, intra-actively constituted through the same event of making. When Andersen builds a machine from a "dim concern" that has not yet achieved conceptual clarity, when Bolt's paint and canvas act back on the painter's intentions, when Hillary returns to a site until the site reorganizes what she knows, when Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz stage conditions for collective interference: in each case the knowing is not prior to the doing, derived from the doing after the fact, or separable from the doer. Knowing, knower, and known co-emerge through the intra-action of apparatus, body, material, and situation.

This is also what Q2 is actually asking. "How have posthumanist theorists conceptualized diffractive practice?" could be answered entirely from the theoretical literature: Barad, Haraway, Braidotti, Bennett each have explicit accounts. But those accounts are most legible when grounded in what it looks like to practice diffractively. The practitioners here provide that grounding. They confirm from the inside what the theory describes from outside: that there is no external standpoint from which the researcher surveys the field, that the apparatus is always already constitutive of the phenomenon it studies, that marks are left on bodies including the body of the researcher. They do not illustrate the theory. They demonstrate the claim.

Trinh T. Minh-ha is treated in full in the previous section, as the practitioner whose film work is the origin point of the diffraction concept: the figure Haraway was reading in 1992 when she introduced diffraction as a feminist critical method. Her account there addresses all three questions simultaneously, confirming that Q1, Q2, and Q3 name not three separate domains but three angles of vision on a single practice. The practitioners gathered here extend that demonstration in studio, site, workshop, and collective registers.

Barbara Bolt's account of painting practice demonstrates the same inseparability from within studio work. Working hot: the state of "act of actual concurrent production": is not a metaphor for the research process. It is the research process: a dynamic relation among cultural and discursive knowledges, bodies, paint, canvas, weather, and gravity in which none of these elements is inert and none occupies the position of sovereign author (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], pp. 275–276). Bolt draws on methexis: developed through engagement with Indigenous Australian practice and Paul Carter's "concurrent actual production": to name what happens when the making of the work and the meaning of the work are constituted together in the same event, not sequentially (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 274). There is no gap to be crossed between sign and referent because the work is not a sign for a pre-existing reality. It is the site where reality and meaning are produced together. The causal connection runs between the matter of bodies, the matter of the object, and the matter of the materials of making: and this production "has effects rather than only meanings" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 274). The knowing is in the doing because only in the doing does the phenomenon come to exist.

What makes Bolt's account additionally significant is the lineage she draws on. Methexis is not a NM concept derived from European philosophy. It enters through Indigenous Australian practice and through Carter's theorization of "concurrent actual production": which is to say, through traditions that have been practicing what NM theorizes for far longer than the theoretical vocabulary has existed to name it. Bolt's working hot is another instance of the pattern Trinh demonstrates: practice arrives at the ground that theory then names.

Diffractive practice as a research method differs from critique in a way that matters for how art practice as research is evaluated. Barad explicitly distinguishes critique as disclosure and demystification from diffractive reading as affirmative engagement. Critique operates in "a mode of disclosure, exposure and demystification," whereas diffractive reading is "a form of affirmative engagement," "an iterative practice of intra-actively reworking and being reworked by patterns of mattering," aimed at making "new patterns of understanding-becoming" (Barad, "Cutting Together-Apart"). Art practice as research shifts the aim of analysis away from unmasking hidden meanings toward noticing how meaning and materiality are co-constituted through arrangements, practices, and constraints. Diffraction is not a synonym for juxtaposition, collage, or eclectic text-mixing; it demands explicit attention to what is produced through particular engagements, not merely the display of multiple perspectives.

Hillary's public art practice demonstrates what the onto-epistemological inseparability of knowing/knower/known looks like when the research site is not the studio but the city. Her explicit methodology list: attuning, collaboration, relational fieldwork, iterative deep listening, site-specific writing, photography, recording and filming, making, returning, thinking-with, journey: reads as a protocol for what Barad calls making the apparatus explicit: each item names a specific mode of intra-action with the site, a specific way of being constituted by it while constituting it (Hillary, 2021, p. 29). Theory develops after "handling": a word she explicitly expands to mean full multisensory engagement: touch, sounds, smells, texture, visions, tastes (Hillary, 2021, p. 31). Works emerge through repeated everyday interactions with the site, through participation in and observation of intra-actions between human and non-human elements. The repetition is not methodological redundancy; it is the condition under which the site's own logic begins to surface. Research findings are named explicitly as performative utterances: events that prompt a rethinking of relationship to site: not stable interpretations but changes in relational perception, interventions in how futures are rehearsed and how publics are composed. Hillary draws on Barad's definition directly: diffraction is a way of reading insights through one another that shifts away from representationalism toward accounting for "how practices matter." What the knowing is in the doing here is this: the site does not yield its knowledge to a researcher who stands outside it. It yields a changed researcher, and the research is the account of that change.

Franinović's workshop practice carries the same inseparability into the pedagogical register. The question that drives her practice-research is: "Can materials be seeds for people... and people seeds for materials?" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129). This is not a rhetorical question. It is a research hypothesis whose answer can only be produced by making: by mapping concepts, materials, and techniques onto each other, choreographing the timing of processes so that material and conceptual co-emergence becomes possible. A seed-space is "a set of constraints or conditions permanently open to change and new iterations": not a predetermined outcome but a set of generative pressures (Franinović, 2016, p. 129). What the knowing is in the doing here is explicit in the research design: knowledge arises after setting the seeds and allowing them time to evolve, through direct and participatory observation that takes often-surprising discoveries as legitimate epistemic outputs. The most telling moment in her account is the one where a participant insists on verbal explanation and is instead pulled into contact with the material process: and "his questions vanished for a moment" (Raw Flows, p. 136). The material answered the question that language was going to ask. Fluid affordance is the name she gives this: with dynamic materials, affordances transform as the material's state changes, destabilizing the habitual instrumental response and opening the encounter. The knowing is in the doing because the material is a co-investigator.

The three case studies that follow: Andersen, Torosyan et al., and Eno & Byrne (read through Wolfe): each extend the argument in a different direction. Andersen demonstrates the individual studio as research apparatus; Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz demonstrate collective political practice as research; Eno & Byrne (as read by Wolfe) demonstrate posthumanist theory applied retrospectively to practice that never claimed to be research, revealing the ontological commitments that were already operating. Together, they map the full range of new materialist APAR: from the studio practitioner working hot with paint and matter (Bolt), to the site-researcher whose knowing accumulates through return and attunement (Hillary), to the workshop designer who seeds conditions for material and human co-emergence (Franinović), to the collective that grounds diffractive practice in explicitly feminist political methodology (Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz), to the outlier case where the research claim belongs to the theorist and the artists are the phenomenon (Eno and Byrne, read through Wolfe).

#### Case Study 1: Kristina Andersen

Kristina Andersen describes her practice with unusual directness: "I  
oscillate between reading and making. Often the objects I make can be  
seen as diagrams of ideas, of manifestations of the relationships  
between one notion and another" (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). This opening  
statement contains, in compressed form, the onto-epistemological stance  
that new materialism theorizes more elaborately: knowledge is not  
separable from the material forms through which it is worked out, and  
the relationship between one notion and another becomes thinkable only  
by being given body. The making and the reading are parallel, mutually  
disturbing processes: not sequential stages of research.

The objects she makes are "magic machines": hypothetical, non-functional  
props made in short concentrated bursts, guided by desire and fear  
rather than skill and technological possibility, built "to give body to  
dim concerns and questions, to consider the potential reality of a world  
in which such a machine might exist, and in a sense to rehearse how to  
live in such a world" (Andersen, 2016, p. 44). The phrase "dim concerns"  
is the key. The machine is not built to illustrate a concept already  
formed. It is built because the concern has not yet achieved conceptual  
clarity: because the only way to find out what it is, is to make  
something from it. As Valente puts it in a passage Andersen cites:  
"First, you build the machine, then it tells you what it's for"  
(Valente, 2013, cited in Andersen, 2016, p. 44). The machine speaks  
back. It has its own rules and possibilities, not fully determined by  
the maker's intention. This is Bennett's thing-power enacted at bench  
scale: the object is not inert matter awaiting inscription but a  
participant in producing the knowledge the encounter generates.

In the thesis, Andersen theorizes this dynamic through "leaky objects."  
The building process is "a dialogue between the intentions of the maker  
and the limitations of the material, and the object itself. Each small  
modification opens up new possibilities for the ongoing building  
process" (Andersen, 2012, p. 87). Workshop outcomes are "leaky" because  
they absorb and leak knowledge and complexity as they appear. In  
presentation and discussion, "a clear multi-directional connection  
appears between the words, the material, the model and the maker": a  
bringing-forth that Andersen calls "an almost poiesis-like process of  
calling something into existence that was not there before" (Andersen,  
2012, p. 87). The object does not receive the maker's meaning; it  
participates in producing it. The machines built in the workshops must  
express themselves through their makers because they do not actually  
work. As a result they "leak knowledge, power, context": they are  
handled roughly by the process, and what they become is not what was  
planned but what emerged through the encounter.

Andersen borrows the term "marking" from dance rehearsals to describe  
what building these non-functional machines does. In dance, marking is  
when a phrase takes a simplified, schematic form: body-in-motion  
representing an aspect of the full-out phrase without performing it  
completely. Building a speculative instrument is marking the physical  
space a future machine will take up, "sketching the gestures and  
movements required to operate and play this particular device." When it  
comes to describing ideas later, "we have the object itself to guide us.  
The tactile and structural possibilities of the non-functional object  
lets us investigate it in a physical manner; it is suddenly possible to  
speak confidently as to how this device will operate, because it allows  
us to anchor any conceptual or metaphorical notions in an object that  
pushes back into our hands: It is already right there" (Andersen, 2016,  
p. 46). The knowledge is in the handling. The object is not illustrating  
the argument; it is making the argument available.

When conducted as workshops: at STEIM and elsewhere: the process  
is designed so participants draw a sound in charcoal, then build the  
instrument capable of making that sound from available materials,  
without discussion, working fast, with the constraint that the object  
must be non-functional. The workshop "temporarily frees us from the  
practical and technical limitations of our work" and the performative  
moment where participants play and explain their instruments is "a live  
volatile process" in the sense of Dewey's art as experience and Cage's  
commitment to creating opportunities to listen (Andersen, 2016, p. 45).  
In that moment, "the experience between the performer, the new  
instrument, and the audience can be perceived as an almost physical  
thing which can be examined, turned, changed, and at some point is  
'done' as the performance moment lifts" (Andersen, 2016, p. 45). This is  
Barad's fog chamber made workshop-sized: a performative apparatus  
through which boundaries are drawn, phenomena come into being, and the  
encounter produces knowledge that belongs to the situation rather than  
to any individual knower.

Andersen's account also illuminates what this mode of knowing costs  
within traditional academic frameworks. She taught herself to write  
academically when she needed to. "Now, when asked to write subjectively,  
I find that I write like a child" (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). This is not  
self-deprecation. It is a structural account of what happens when a mode  
of knowledge production that operates through material immersion and  
leaky objects is required to re-enter itself through the protocols of  
academic prose. The academic writing does not translate the practice; it  
overwrites the voice the practice developed. What Andersen describes as  
"writing like a child" names the cost of forcing poiesis through the  
grammar of representational research: which is precisely why the  
Violence of Traditional Methods section uses her account directly. The  
violence is not abstract. It lands on specific practitioners in specific  
ways.

#### Case Study 2: Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz: Interferences of the Multitude

Where Andersen's case study traces what new materialist art practice as  
research looks like at the level of individual studio process, Torosyan,  
Reis, and Wuschitz's "Interferences of the Multitude" (Politics of the  
Machines, 2021) demonstrates what it looks like as collective,  
institutionally situated, politically explicit research practice. The  
paper introduces a track of work presented at the Rogue Research edition  
of the 3rd Politics of the Machines Conference, Berlin 2021, and is  
itself grounded in the authors' collaborative work at Mz\\* Baltazar's  
Laboratory, a feminist hackerspace in Vienna where they practice  
trans-feminist hacking as artistic methodology (Torosyan et al., 2021,  
p. 57).

The paper's theoretical framing is explicitly posthumanist and new  
materialist, and it names its methods with unusual precision. The two  
key operators are interference and diffraction. Interference, in the  
paper's usage, is "a critical disruptive process that destabilises the  
status quo and plays an important role in relational and emancipatory  
thinking and action." Diffraction, they argue, "came to replace the  
reflexive methodologies of thinking and doing" and together these two  
notions "are foundational for the new materialist turn and feminist  
ethics of care" (Torosyan et al., 2021, pp. 55--56). The wave physics  
here is not decorative: interference patterns form when different points  
of a wavefront reach a surface at different intensities. The most  
complex knowledge patterns, the paper implies, emerge from exactly this  
kind of collision: not from a single unified perspective but from the  
intra-action of multiple, differently positioned, differently charged  
perspectives meeting in the same space.

Alongside diffraction, the authors explicitly operationalize Barad's  
intra-action as a response to the inadequacy of "interaction," which  
presupposes the pre-givenness of bodies that then encounter each other.  
Intra-action, by contrast, "suggests that agency is not an inherent  
property of an individual body to be exercised but a mutual influence  
and co-emergence of forces" (Barad 2007, p. 141, cited in Torosyan et  
al., 2021, pp. 55--56). This is also framed as a response to what  
Haraway calls "the God's trick": the objectivist framing that  
presents outcomes or measurements as purely neutral. Feminist new  
materialist ethics, as Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz articulate it, is  
rooted "in the critical post-human rethinking of the Eurocentric notions  
of 'Human'" and extends the cyberfeminist critique of constructivism to  
address "the material, gendered, and embodied challenges of existence in  
the augmented world" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 56).

What makes this a useful case study for new materialist APAR is the  
range of practices gathered under its frame. The track included: a  
multiplayer game in which the avatar can only be navigated by multiple  
players who coordinate collectively (Renfro); a computational  
performance in which a performer embodies a tomato to defamiliarize  
colonial and anthropocentric scientific thinking about plant life  
(Pereira and Bruggave); an artist residency on a floating barge in  
Poland structured around feminist hydro-commons and degrowth  
(Walentynowicz and Majewska-Güde); a trans-feminist dance performance  
using a vaginal muscle reader to control sound (Varela); and a paper on  
open-source feminist sextech that tracks menstruation without binary or  
patronizing categories of sex and gender (Dietze and Stewart) (Torosyan  
et al., 2021, pp. 57--60). Across this range, the common thread is not  
medium, not discipline, and not institutional form. It is the  
onto-epistemological stance: matter is active, agency is distributed,  
knowledge emerges from intra-action rather than from observation of a  
pre-given world, and the research apparatus includes the researcher's  
own positionality as a constitutive element rather than a bias to be  
minimized.

The paper's conclusion is explicit about what constitutes knowledge in  
this mode: "Most of the panellists are both artists and scholars. Their  
methodology of knowledge production is a deep conversation between  
theory and practice, and productive oscillation between the two." The  
intervention: whether a game, a coding manual, an installation, or a  
mining community workshop: "becomes fertile ground for new empathetic  
technologies: technologies that facilitate a conversation on a  
specific research question." The technology is generated according to  
the critical investigation, then applied in an experimental setting, and  
"in conversation with this technology a process of co-becoming,  
'becoming with' (Haraway 2013), was initiated, grounded in collaboration  
with concerned communities" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 60). This is  
Franinović's seeding logic applied at political and ecological scale:  
designing conditions in which matter, bodies, and communities can  
activate each other across the boundary between art, science, and  
collective action. The contextualization and theorizing of the  
intervention's impact is accomplished through writing: but writing  
that follows practice, not one that precedes it.

The political framing of the track adds a dimension not yet named. Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz ground their work in a neo-Spinozist notion of multitude: drawn from Negri and Hardt (2000, 2005): as the agent of potentia: the collective, distributed power-to-act as distinct from potestas, the power-over that characterizes sovereign and institutional forms of authority. The return to Spinoza's monism and the idea of multitude as the agent(s) of potentia, they note, is "resonant with the indigenous political thinking" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 55), connecting the philosophical lineage to non-Western traditions of collective agency that the document has elsewhere traced through its own genealogy. Trans-feminist hacking is the methodological form this takes in practice: a research-based artistic framework that applies diffractive practice to "bridge this gap in thinking of and doing with difference," imagining "post-human conviviality through mutual care, co-dependence, and solidarity" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 57). This is not diffractive practice as an individual researcher's epistemological stance. It is diffractive practice as collective political methodology: the interference pattern produced when multiple differently positioned, differently charged perspectives meet in the same space, generating the most complex knowledge patterns precisely through that collision. The case study confirms that new materialist art practice as research can operate at the scale of community, institution, and political ecology, not only at the scale of the individual studio.

#### Case Study 3: Brian Eno and David Byrne: My Life in the Bush of Ghosts

Cary Wolfe reads Brian Eno and David Byrne's 1981 record *My Life in the  
Bush of Ghosts* as a case study in posthumanist practice, and the  
reading illuminates something that the theoretical literature tends to  
state abstractly: that posthumanist ontology is not a set of  
propositions one holds but a mode of working that enacts different  
assumptions about agency, authorship, materiality, and knowledge. The  
record is worth dwelling on at some length precisely because it makes  
legible: in the form of artistic decisions, technical accidents, and  
explicit reflections from the artists themselves: the same structure  
that new materialism theorizes (Wolfe, 2010, pp. 283--299).

The record's central method is the found vocal: recordings drawn from  
radio sermons, Lebanese mountain singers, evangelical preachers,  
exorcism sessions: source materials that Byrne and Eno layered over  
rhythm tracks in a process they describe as close to performance, with  
no samplers available and no digital correction. "Throwing the vocals  
against the tracks was in our case almost a performance," Byrne writes;  
serendipity determined which takes held together, and the analog  
interplay of two tape machines running simultaneously: not  
algorithmic matching: was the operative apparatus (Wolfe, 2010, p.  
289). This is not a marginal technical detail. It is the ontological  
condition of the work. Eno's account of his video work from the same  
period makes the same point from a different angle: working with a  
camera whose tube had been burned out by overexposure to sky, he found  
that the damaged instrument "responded to light and colour in a way no  
other camera did." The malfunction was the method. The broken equipment  
produced what the functioning equipment could not. What Gregory Bateson  
calls "real magnitudes": the specific, embodied, situational  
interplay of material forces including weather, proximity, decay, and  
contingency: were not noise to be eliminated but the generative  
condition of the work (Wolfe, 2010, p. 292). Shannon's suppressed  
insight: that noise carries the most information: is here enacted  
rather than theorized.

Agency in this project is distributed across found voices, broken  
cameras, serendipitous tape alignments, the weather, and the bodies of  
singers recorded decades earlier in contexts entirely removed from the  
record's production. Byrne and Eno are not the sovereign authors of this  
work in any conventional sense. They are, in Kirkkopelto's precise  
formulation, transponents: hybrid agents of change whose communication  
is not informative but transformative, who do not bring messages between  
fields but make latent virtual affinities appear that were previously  
unactualized. The vocal source material is not raw material awaiting the  
artists' meaning: it is already fully formed, already exceeding any  
framework either artist brings to it. What the artists do is stage  
conditions: a specific rhythm track, a specific room, a specific tape  
machine, a specific moment of alignment. The knowledge produced is the  
diffraction pattern of that encounter, not the expression of a prior  
intention.

Wolfe frames the record's resistance to digital rendering through  
Derrida and Stiegler. For Stiegler, digital media depend on a  
"systematic discretization" and "grammaticalization" of content: the  
same extractive logic that stripped Leibniz's binary notation from its  
relational context, that suppressed Shannon's noise, that domesticated  
Bohr's entanglement into the Copenhagen interpretation (Wolfe, 2010, p.  
286). The analog is spectral in relation to this regime precisely  
because it resists complete rendering: it depends on the interplay of  
real magnitudes in space-time, and that interplay is "in principle  
bottomless": a reservoir of complexity and contingency that no  
program or schema can fully subsume (Wolfe, 2010, p. 292). The voice on  
*Bush of Ghosts* is spectral in exactly this sense: not because it is  
mysterious or exotic, but because its continuity, its grain, its  
materiality as analog sound exceeds the diacritical elements a  
discretizing analysis can extract from it. As Eno put it: "When people  
speak passionately, they speak in melodies." The grammar is present but  
what the grammar cannot capture: what makes the voice the voice:  
is the remainder that escapes discretization.

This connects directly to Massumi's claim that "nothing is more  
destructive for the thinking and imaging of the virtual than equating it  
with the digital": a claim Wolfe reads through *Bush of Ghosts* to  
argue that the logic of the virtual is the logic of sampling itself:  
"always leaving its source by the time it reaches us, always in the  
process of vanishing" (Wolfe, 2010, p. 295). The record does not  
thematize this. It enacts it. The appearance of the virtual is in the  
twists and folds of formed content, in the movement from one sample to  
another. What makes the record feel like it comes from "everywhere and  
nowhere, past and future": Wolfe's description of its temporal  
uncanniness: is precisely this: each vocal is already a ghost of its  
original context, and the analog interplay that makes the record work is  
generating new ghosts in real time. The digital is a convention imposed  
on this analog reality; the analog was always there underneath,  
constituting the very substrate that discretization reads and misreads.

What this case study demonstrates for new materialist art practice as  
research is several things simultaneously. First, that the artist as  
apparatus: as transponent, as condition-setter rather than sovereign  
author: is not a theoretical position imposed on artistic practice  
from outside but a description of what certain artistic practices have  
always been doing. Byrne and Eno did not know Barad or Bennett; they  
were working from musical and artistic intuitions that enacted the same  
distributed-agency, material-contingency ontology. Second, that the  
posthumanist refusal of representationalism: the refusal to treat the  
work as the expression of a prior meaning: is itself a productive  
research stance. Byrne's explicit discomfort with the idea that music  
should be "an expression of emotion" or that the artist is "someone with  
something to say" is not nihilism; it is an opening toward the kind of  
knowledge that emerges from the intra-action of materials, chance, and  
situated attention. Third, the record's explicit engagement with the  
analog/digital distinction: its resistance to the grammaticalization  
and discretization of the voice: makes the Leibniz/Shannon/Bohr  
suppression argument concrete and audible. The human is, as Wolfe  
summarizes Derrida and Stiegler, "a prosthetic being, who from day one  
is constituted as human by its coevolution with and co-constitution by  
external archival technologies of various kinds: including language  
itself as the first archive and prosthesis" (Wolfe, 2010, p. 294). The  
analog voice, the found recording, the broken camera: these are not  
tools the artist uses. They are the apparatus through which the artist,  
the work, and the knowledge the work produces all co-emerge.

### What Counts as Knowledge

Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch offer a formulation that makes the  
epistemic claims of new materialist art practice as research legible  
across disciplines: artistic research is distinctive in the combination  
of experimentation, participation, and interpretation, and "reasoning"  
is not only in the reflective text but also located in material  
practices themselves when cognition is embodied, embedded, and enacted  
(Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 4). Unlike laboratory testing oriented  
toward commensuration and standardization, the objective of the artistic  
experiment is "not so much to test... but to tell something, to convey  
content" (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 4). Crucially, they reject the  
assumption that the artwork is the research and the written reflection  
is merely supplementary; if cognition is embodied and material practices  
have epistemic force, then "reasoning is also located in those material  
practices" (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 5). This means the studio/lab  
process is not pre-research that gets translated into knowledge later.  
It is where research operations happen. What counts as evidence,  
accordingly, is not confined to propositional statements. The Textile  
Enchantment catalog makes this explicit at the level of evaluation:  
research-creation "cannot be deemed successful in simple or finite  
terms" and does not yield a singular discovery but "opens up new fields  
of understanding": a research outcome to be assessed through  
unfolding interactions and capacities rather than through closure. The  
articulation required: documentation, dissemination, publication:  
is not separate from the research but is "additional work" that makes  
the material-discursive labor publicly assessable, interwoven with text  
rather than transcribed into it (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 5).

Bolt makes the same argument from within painting practice. Her account of working hot reframes artistic making as "act of actual concurrent production": a dynamic relation among cultural and discursive knowledges, bodies, and materials: where the material practice itself has effects, not just meanings. Creativity is emergent and distributed: the "acting ensemble" includes human and nonhuman actants (paint, canvas, weather, gravity), shifting authorship away from the individual and onto the relations and conditions of making (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], pp. 275–276). Research in this mode produces material effects, traces, capacities, and transformations through situated practice rather than representing a pre-existing reality. It stages intra-actions where knowledge emerges through relations and material constraints: situations, apparatuses, worldings, not just objects.

Cull Ó Maoilearca names what this means for how performance-based APAR researchers understand knowledge: "performance is a form of knowledge that shares its insights through what it does rather than what it means, or better perhaps, that the best way to understand what performance means and why it matters is through attending closely to what it does affectively" (Cull Ó Maoilearca, 2019, p. 156). This formulation holds across all new materialist APAR, not only performance: the work of Andersen's magic machines, Hinterwaldner's marker materials, Franinović's seeding workshops, Hillary's public art practice: each produces knowledge through what it does, not through what it represents. What it represents is secondary and derivative. What it does is constitutive of the phenomenon it studies.

Britton, Klumbyte, and Draude develop three hybrid methodologies in *Doing Thinking* (2019) that give the most operationally precise account of what it means to reposition artistic research as worlding practice rather than stable output. Creating situations for intra-action means beginning from concrete practical issues and reframing them collaboratively by staging engagements that allow intra-action across different knowledges and material constraints: the situation is not incidental to the research but what makes specific relations and therefore specific knowledge possible. Diffractive reading/making is not a formalized recipe; its potential lies in taking methodological openness seriously as a precondition for transformative results: "doing thinking" must be enacted rather than merely described (Britton et al., 2019, p. 316). Material speculation extends this into the third movement: working with materials as active participants rather than passive content, maintaining the availability to what they produce rather than confirming what was assumed in advance. Agency in their account is "not just located in the 'I' but distributed across human and non-human elements": relations precede the relata, and the elements do not pre-exist the situation but emerge within it.

Pirrò's precision is useful here: transpositions resist stabilization into propositional formulation. Relations and differences remain networks of interacting positions: "incompressible," not reducible to a single stable claim. Transposition produces knowledge as sequence effects and relational shifts, not as propositional snapshots (Schwab's framing: operations, not results). Lomax adds that transposition supplies a method-form for how artistic research can generalize through movement "from the particular to the particular," rather than extracting a rule first. Transpositions cannot be fully seen from outside in representational snapshots; they are grasped from within sequences, operations, and relations. This is why practice-research knowledge is operational and processual: not because it is less rigorous than propositional knowledge, but because the kind of knowing it produces lives in the doing rather than in its residue.

### The Object

For artist and researcher Kristina Andersen, the objects she makes can often "be seen as diagrams of ideas, of manifestations of the relationships between one notion and another" (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). Objects are frequently hypothetical, or magic machines, created to "give body to dim concerns," "to consider the potentiality of a world in which such a machine might exist," and "to rehearse how to live in such a world" (Andersen, 2016, p. 44). They are props: material-discursive arrangements, agential swarms of vibrant matter: objects to think through and with for further investigation, not endpoints of research but apparatuses through which inquiry continues.

This account of the object refuses the dominant Western visual theory position. The prevailing account positions the art object as a sign: a substitute for its referent, circulating in the social realm of meanings, its function primarily communicative. To claim anything more: that a material practice might have real material effects, that there could be a mutual exchange between the matter of bodies and the representation of bodies: is, as Bolt puts it, to have "extremely limited currency in Western art history and theory" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 269). It requires casting into doubt the identity of the sign, since the dominant view holds that there is a gap between sign and referent that the sign can never cross. To argue that the object does more than represent is to commit, as Vicki Kirby frames it, to asking whether matter might be "more articulate than imagined" (Kirby, 1997, p. 114, cited in Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 274).

New materialist art practice refuses this domestication of the object. Mäkikoskela defines "artistic thinking" as the often unnoticed movement between materiality and conceptuality in visual art practice, grounded in feminist new materialism alongside dialogical thinking with materials. Her key move is methodological: artistic research allows examination of art-making from a participant's point of view, tracking the thinking of the artist through the process: thinking that is not separate from matter but happens through material engagement. This is what distinguishes art practice as research from art practice as illustration: in illustration, the concept precedes and governs the material; in research, the material pushes back on the concept, revising what was thinkable before the encounter. Kirkkopelto names the same condition: the artistic researcher can "jump right in" by appropriating and inventing concepts and staging them as epistemic things, with real risk: because failure is possible when knowledge is genuinely at stake. The object, in new materialist APAR, is never simply what it looks like. It is what it does, what it enters into, what it makes possible. It is, to use Andersen's phrase, a machine for rehearsing how to live in a world not yet fully here.

### Queering the Reading of Text/Foregoing Language Altogether

Andersen shares that she doesn't \"read texts properly\", and isn't  
sure she knows how to. For her, the texts \"are like images, like scraps  
of colour and texture.\" Text has a sort of thing-power, as a thing that  
can be carried around for years as if in an always wearing pocket, ready  
to be taken out, re-examined and reconsidered. Over time/space/matter  
the texts come back like curses, the meaning changes as she \"gropes  
toward the preponderance of the object\", lingering, as the *there,  
there* of the text recedes again into the agential swarm, into the void,  
the other, the absolute. (Andersen, 2016, p.42)

Franinović queers the reading of text and makes space for material through interweaving texts and materials by "preparing seeds for material events," mapping concepts, materials, and techniques, then planning experiments with specific materials and choreographing timing (Franinović, 2016). She aimed to transfer "seed-space techniques" to material research to avoid overly predefining processes and allow emergence, asking directly what conditions and constraints help "activate materials" and "activate people's thoughts and actions with and through materials." Participatory workshops become a "third space" where diverse parties produce shared knowledge: a shift away from purely verbal techniques toward non-linguistic collaborative experience with physical artifacts (Franinović, 2016).

What the object is, and what it does, are questions that Western visual  
theory has answered badly: and the bad answer has consequences for  
how research through making is evaluated. The dominant account positions  
the art object as a sign: a substitute for its referent, circulating in  
the social realm of meanings, its function primarily communicative. To  
claim anything more: that a material practice might have real  
material effects, that there could be a mutual exchange between the  
matter of bodies and the representation of bodies: is, as Bolt puts  
it, to have "extremely limited currency in Western art history and  
theory" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 269). It requires casting into doubt the  
identity of the sign, since the dominant view holds that there is a gap  
between sign and referent that the sign can never cross. To argue that  
the object does more than represent is to commit, as Vicki Kirby frames  
it, to asking whether matter might be "more articulate than imagined"  
(Kirby, 1997, p. 114, cited in Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 274).

Bolt's concept of methexis: drawn from Indigenous Australian practice  
and Paul Carter's theorization of "concurrent actual production":  
proposes exactly the alternative. In methexis, the making of the work is  
not the production of a sign that subsequently represents a reality. It  
is a production through which meaning and reality are constituted  
together, in the same event. There is no gap to be crossed. The causal  
connection runs between the matter of bodies, the matter of the object,  
and the matter of the materials of making: and this production "has  
effects" rather than only meanings (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 274). Bolt's term for  
practicing within this understanding is "working hot": a state in which  
"the process of painting is a response to what happens in the  
interaction between paint, oil, turps, canvas, gravity, sun, heat, the  
occasional live beast and my body": processes that "have actual  
causal relations, not merely significatory relations" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p.  
276). The work is co-emergent rather than mastered. Matter is not  
impressed upon; matter insists.

The implication for the research artifact is precise. If artistic  
practice is methectic: if the sign is not separate from its  
production but emerges in the interactive labour of making: then what  
the finished work carries is not a representation of the process but an  
index of it. Peirce's indexical sign has a direct causal relation  
between thing and sign: it produces effects, not just meanings, and it  
"allows us to 'witness' in a very different way the process of  
materialisation that occurs in the interaction between the matter of  
bodies, the matter of materials, technologies and the cultural" (Bolt,  
2024, p. 277). The work is a trace of the bodies in labour: human and  
nonhuman: that produced it. This is why the research artifact in new  
materialist APAR is not evidence in the conventional sense of  
documentation of findings that were separately reached. It is the  
finding, carried as index: materialisation as an effect that points  
directly back to the distributed encounter through which it came into  
being. Bolt's conclusion names this directly: the matter of the work  
"implicates the materiality of the paint and the environment, processes  
that have actual causal relations, not merely significatory relations"  
(Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 276). The work does not represent the research. It is  
the research, materially inscribed.

### Performance

What Bolt's account of working hot demonstrates is not only a claim about painting. It names the ontological condition of practice-based research as such: the matter of bodies, materials, and making co-produce each other through intra-action, and what emerges carries real causal effects rather than merely significatory ones. The philosophical grounding for this claim runs through a specific line of development in feminist theory: one that begins with Butler's account of performativity, is extended by Barad's posthumanist revision of it, and finds independent confirmation in both Bolt's studio and Andersen's workshop.

Butler's account of performativity, developed in *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Bodies That Matter* (1993), is the foundation. Bodies do not pre-exist the norms that produce them: they materialize through the repeated, citational performance of those norms over time. The world is not pre-given but produced through iterative, discursive processes. Matter, in Butler's account, is the effect of those processes: shaped by discourse, brought into being through cultural repetition. The insight is significant: it breaks the assumption that bodies arrive in the world already constituted and awaiting cultural inscription. They are constituted in and through the inscription itself.

Barad takes this insight seriously and builds from it rather than against it. What she disputes is not the iteration but where Butler locates the agency in that process. For Butler, iteration is discursive: language and social repetition produce materiality. Matter is downstream of discourse. Barad's posthumanist performativity, developed in "Posthumanist Performativity" (*Signs*, 2003) and *Meeting the Universe Halfway* (2007), expands the ontological ground: the iterative processes that produce the world are material-discursive, not merely discursive. Matter itself participates in its own production. It is not the passive product of language acting on it: it intra-acts, co-constitutes, pushes back. Butler asks how discourse materializes bodies. Barad asks how matter and discourse co-produce each other through intra-action. The revision is not a rejection. It is an extension that restores matter to the status of co-agent rather than downstream effect.

This distinction is not only philosophical. It determines what kind of claim can be made when the machine speaks back. Andersen makes machines as a way of "arriving at an understanding," because she can only process something "if she re-make[s] it [herself] first." The object comes before the idea or concept of the object, through the repetition of time in "an almost scientific notion of repeatability" (Andersen, 2016, p. 44). This is Butler's iteration: but the iteration is not citational repetition of discursive norms. It is material-discursive intra-action in Barad's sense: the apparatus of Andersen's body, tools, and workshop conditions co-produces what becomes knowable. Siffrinn and Coda (2025) confirm the pattern across a different medium: "through processual reflections shared by the language learners involved in the animation making, they came to see how this monotonous tactile work created an affective flow wherein more-than-human conversations spurred learning in creative ways such as through encounters with objects in the immediate environment" (p. 196). The monotonous, tactile, repeated making is the research apparatus. What it produces: the affective flow, the more-than-human conversation: is the phenomenon. Repetition in Barad's extended sense is not mere rehearsal. It is how material-discursive arrangements enact the cuts through which phenomena become knowable.

Through this process, over time, Andersen develops intuition about the capacities of the thing. The machine speaks back, telling her what it can do and what it is for. The machine is "an entity with its own rules and possibilities" (Andersen, 2016, p. 44). This is not metaphor. In Barad's ontology, the machine's speaking back is real: matter is agential, and the intra-action between Andersen and the machine is the site where both are constituted differently than they were before. Andersen does not cite Spinoza, but she is practicing him. The claim she arrives at through making: that you cannot know what a body can do until it does it: is Spinoza's "nobody as yet has determined the limits of the body's capabilities" enacted in the studio. Bolt names the same condition from painting: working hot is the state in which matter insists rather than submits, in which the practitioner's prior concept is exceeded by the encounter. Two artist-researchers, working in different media and different institutional contexts, independently confirm what Barad formalizes: posthumanist performativity is not a theoretical claim about practice. It is a description of what practice, when followed with sufficient attention, actually is.

This is the conflict Cull Ó Maoilearca identifies at the institutional heart of artistic research in performance. Researchers in ARP must negotiate "the broadly representationalist versus performative paradigms: wherein the former construes knowledge as a more or less accurate representation of an objective reality by a transcendent subject, and the latter posits the performative production of things by their ways of knowing and being known and therefore construes knowledge-production as an immanent material part of the same reality which it might seek to represent" (Cull Ó Maoilearca, 2019, p. 154). The very notion of a "contribution to knowledge": which posits knowledge as a cumulative stock or sanctioned canon: is intrinsically problematic for research projects working from performative rather than representationalist ontologies. This is not a methodological preference. It is a conflict between incompatible accounts of what the world is, and whether practice can be a site of knowledge or only an object to be analyzed by it.

### Interactivity/Assemblage/Co-constitutive

The Enchantment of Textiles catalog provides a concrete laboratory model for what new materialist interactivity looks like as research apparatus. The catalog describes the project as an interdisciplinary investigation of electronic cloth: studying historic textiles and transforming that knowledge into interactive objects that demonstrate what becomes possible when material languages meet digital culture (Jefferies, 2019, pp. 14–29). The lab is explicitly framed as a collaborative assemblage: artists, engineers, and researchers: with textiles as performative tools rather than passive substrates.

The research apparatus generates an explicit distributed agency claim: users become collaborators; interaction creates "connections without hierarchy," where the spectator becomes a contributor imprinting subjectivity on the work (Ledesma Guadarrama, 2019, p. 6). Bergen grounds this directly in Bennett: "bodies enhance their power in or as a heterogeneous assemblage" (Bennett, 2010, p. 23, cited in Bergen, 2019, p. 10). The Enchantment of Textiles is NM conceptualization enacted rather than described: a research apparatus in which phenomena are generated through material-technological arrangements of signal, interference, body orientation, and craft technique, and in which the boundaries between researcher, maker, object, and participant are constitutively underdetermined. The co-constitutive is the condition, not the outcome: the assemblage does not produce co-constitution as a result; it begins from it.

### Beyond Representation: Engaging with the Material Itself

Kirschner's foreword to *Raw Flows* (2016) frames fluidity not as a metaphor but as methodological pressure on research. He positions the book as investigating how fluidity and flow "carve their specific paths into experimental practices and thinking patterns" across arts, humanities, and sciences: a direct "practice-as-research" hinge: material properties shape method and thought, not the other way around. He insists on dealing with fluidity "beyond its metaphorical use," grounding inquiry in "actual substances or stuff" rather than abstract notions of matter. Fluids undermine formalization: they resist capture in still images, which fail to communicate the "core feature of processuality." The project's approach is reframed not as containment: the vessel model: but as an "irregular and cross-disciplinary grid" immersed in material flows to catch, extract, and evaluate ways of dealing with fluidity, producing insights into practice across epistemic, aesthetic, and experiential registers simultaneously.

This is the NM move in its most concentrated form: matter not as illustrative content but as the condition and driver of research. Hinterwaldner's chapter in the same volume demonstrates what this means procedurally. In turbulence experiments, marker materials are not neutral helpers that reveal a pre-given flow. They are materially agentic participants that shape what can be seen, stabilized, recorded, and turned into knowledge. The marker has multiple simultaneous roles: "being, embodying, representing and preparing an object of research… stabilizing an interface… being photogenic… base for further visualizations" (*Raw Flows*, p. 49). The object of research is not prior to the experimental apparatus; it is prepared by it. This is Bennett's thing-power applied to methodology: the "capacity of things… to act as quasi agents or forces with trajectories, tendencies of their own" (*Raw Flows*, p. 49), and Barad's intra-action applied to experimental design: markers "make flows visible… by intra-acting… in an experimental setup-as-assemblage" (*Raw Flows*, p. 49). The apparatus is not neutral with respect to what it knows. It is constitutive of it: which is precisely what Barad theorizes as the agential cut and what Borgdorff et al. name as method-as-intervention. The same principle that runs through quantum physics runs through the turbulence tank: what you use to look at the thing is part of what makes the thing what it is.

### The Body in Time/Space/Matter: Marking and the Fog Chamber

Andersen introduces the practice of marking from the medium of dance. Marking makes a record of the movement of the dancer's body through time/space/matter so that the movement may be studied, collected, shared, and recreated later. The collection of gestures, movement, and time can act as an anchor for conceptual and metaphorical notions performed through the ongoing always-already becoming of an object. In dance, marking is what allows a practice to accumulate without being frozen: it preserves trace without collapsing the living movement into an archive. Andersen borrows the concept because it describes exactly what new materialist APAR requires: a way of holding the research event long enough to work with it, without reducing it to the static representation that would end its generativity.

The studio and workshop space that houses this kind of research is what Moñivas Mayor (2016) calls a flux condenser: a site that gathers drifting phenomena, sounds, material behaviors, and atmospheric conditions, and converts them into creative-research movements. It is not documentation of practice but practice itself as a condensing apparatus for knowing-with materials. The condenser does not contain the flow; it intensifies it until something becomes perceptible that was not perceptible before.

Andersen names the performative moment of making as a fog chamber: the material-discursive arrangement where agential cuts are made through the intra-action of apparatus, subject, object, and time. The fog chamber creates what she calls a possibility space: the conditions under which something new might become thinkable. Making these conditions requires a mentally and emotionally fluid environment, where the researcher risks coming undone through the process of making strange (Adorno's courage of the clown), and where a "chance of becoming human" is created rather than assumed (Andersen, citing Butler, 2005). The possibility space is the momentary product of the research experiment. It is not a stable location. It is what happens in the interval: Trinh T. Minh-ha's break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed (Trinh, 1990, p. 77): between the concept and the material, between what was known before the encounter and what becomes thinkable through it.

The boundaries of the experiment are set not by the researcher's prior plan but through the agency and liveness of the material pushing back on the limitations of skill, imagination, and time. The boundaries are redrawn through the presentation of the object to others and through subsequent play, where the participants define the play space through improvisation, listening, and the creation of the rules of the game as the game is played. Britton, Klumbyte, and Draude name this directly in *Doing thinking*: artistic research and new materialism together reposition research away from object-production toward making situations, sites, and worldings: the elements do not pre-exist the situation but emerge within it (explicitly Baradian). Research here is not the production of a stable object that then gets analyzed. It is the creation of a situation in which phenomena can appear that could not have been predicted in advance.

### New Materialist APAR as Pedagogical Methodology

When the studio is understood as a flux condenser and the workshop as a fog chamber, new materialist art practice as research becomes transferable as a pedagogical methodology: not a curriculum but a set of conditions. Franinović (2016) provides the most explicit articulation of what this looks like in practice. Her chapter in *Raw Flows* is a methodological manual for new materialist practice-research: the central research-design problem is how to activate materials and activate people's thoughts and actions "with and through materials," via conditions and constraints in a workshop setting. Her guiding question is: "Can materials be seeds for people... and people seeds for materials?" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129).

The method she develops is seeding: mapping concepts, materials, and techniques onto each other, then choreographing the timing of processes so that material and conceptual processes co-emerge rather than one governing the other. A seed-space is "a set of constraints or conditions permanently open to change and new iterations" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129): not a predetermined outcome but a set of generative pressures. The goal is not to predict participants' desires or needs but to "flirt with existing behaviors to produce new ones" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129). Knowledge arises after setting the seeds and allowing them time to evolve, through direct and participatory observation that explicitly values often-surprising discoveries as legitimate epistemic outputs.

The core concept emerging from this practice is fluid affordance: with dynamic materials, affordances are unstable and transform as the material's state changes. This instability invites exploration rather than use. Where the affordances of everyday objects tend to constrain imagination through habitual instrumental responses: brainstorming pressure yields "the most obvious and habitual affordance" (Franinović, 2016, p. 140): active materials destabilize that model in an embodied way, making affordances emergent and open-ended. The question shifts from what is active to how is it active: the research contribution is defined as designing conditions where material processes and conceptual processes co-emerge, generating surprising insights, and producing shareable methods: seeding, choreographing, constraining: rather than only artworks.

This is why new materialist APAR is genuinely pedagogical and not merely expressive. It teaches by creating conditions. It shares by transmitting the structure of a situation rather than the content of a finding. What is learned is not a conclusion but a capacity: for attunement, for noticing, for staying with the encounter long enough to let the material's own logic begin to surface. Andersen describes what this produces in the practitioner: the process of making is a process of changing ourselves, "becoming the person who built that thing" (Andersen, 2016). New thoughts are acted out and created "in a complex collaboration between the object, the situation and a performative moment." The knowledge is not deposited in the object. It is held in the changed relation between the practitioner and the world that the making produced.

### The Violence of Traditional Methods of Research 

The words/representations/abstractions of the objects are a method of  
necessary communication for traditional academic research. Andersen had  
to learn to write academically to communicate her practice, but academic  
writing overshadowed/overtook/made her subjective writing seem like that  
of a child, and she had to struggle to regain her own voice. (Andersen,  
2016, p.42)

The violence named in this section title has a precise theoretical  
genealogy that needs to be made explicit. Calvo-Pascual, tracing  
"connections: or, rather, intra-actions —" among critical  
posthumanism, environmental/animal studies, and new materialism,  
foregrounds an "ethics of inclusion" as the ethical core of this  
theoretical convergence (Calvo-Pascual, 2024). She draws on Alaimo's  
trans-corporeality to argue that humans are "always intermeshed with the  
more-than-human world," and that ethics must be accountable to a  
material world that is "the very substance of our selves and others."  
She then draws on Barad to link ethics to "responsibility and  
accountability for the lively relationalities of becoming" in which  
bodies and environments are intra-actively co-constituted. The violence  
of traditional research methods is, on this account, not metaphorical.  
When research forces material and experiential knowledge into  
representational protocols that were designed to exclude it: when  
Andersen must learn to write academically in a way that overwrites her  
own voice, when Schwab's "forced acting, incoherent scripts, and  
pointless monologues" become the mark of failure rather than evidence of  
genuine epistemic risk: what is being enacted is an exclusion with  
material consequences for bodies, practices, and the forms of knowledge  
that can circulate. The violence is the systematic refusal of the  
intra-active, the trans-corporeal, the entangled: which is to say,  
the refusal of the world as it actually is.

Schwab frames the field's confusion as coming from unsuitable  
adaptations from non-artistic fields (humanities/sciences), which reduce  
the epistemic implications of aesthetic labour to sensory/experiential  
"input."

The institutional question becomes unavoidable: what is a suitable  
peer-reviewed format for "the exposition of practice as research"?

He treats prescribed "reflection" (especially in art education) as a  
haunting constraint: a demand for verbalization that may miss "more  
fundamental operations of knowledge creation," and that keeps  
hierarchies from being "flat enough" under post-media conditions.

Instead of locating "reflection" and "creation" in predetermined sites,  
expositionality presses toward forms where practice articulates research  
outside conventional orders of production and reflection.

### Speaking Nearby and the Refusal of Closure

The refusal of closure is not a methodological weakness of new materialist art practice as research. It is its most rigorous commitment: and the one most difficult to defend within institutional frameworks built to evaluate research by its conclusions.

Trinh T. Minh-ha articulates the principle before it has institutional context to push against. In "Documentary Is/Not a Name" (1990), she names what the refusal of closure protects: "meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown," and what persists between the meaning of something and its truth is "the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). The interval is not empty space between findings. It is the condition of possibility for knowledge that does not replicate the epistemic violence of totalization. To close the interval: to reach the definitive conclusion, to produce the result that travels cleanly from the studio to the publication: is to suppress what the encounter was producing before the demand for closure arrived. The closure is itself the agential cut that forecloses what might have continued to emerge. Trinh puts this as the work's own condition: "A work that reflects back on itself offers itself infinitely as nothing else but work... and void. Its gaze is at once an impulse that causes the work to fall apart (to return to the initial no-work-ness) and an ultimate gift to its constitution. A gift, by which the work is freed from the tyranny of meaning as well as from the omnipresence of a subject of meaning. To let go of the hold at the very moment when it is at its most effective is to allow the work to live, and to live on independently of the intended links" (Trinh, 1990, p. 87). The work that refuses exhaustive capture does not fail to communicate. It communicates itself in itself: and the research claim is located in that communicative event, not in the propositional account a researcher subsequently constructs of it.

Speaking nearby enacts this refusal as a relational practice. Trinh describes it as "a speaking that does not objectify, does not point to an object as if it is distant from the speaking subject or absent from the speaking place. A speaking that reflects on itself and can come very close to a subject without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, p. 87). The closures in this mode "are only moments of transition opening up to other possible moments of transition": not endpoints but inflection points in an ongoing process. The interval is not resolved. It continues to work. Trinh is explicit that speaking nearby is not a technique but an ontological stance: "It is not just a technique or a statement to be made verbally. It is an attitude in life, a way of positioning oneself in relation to the world. Thus, the challenge is to materialize it in all aspects of the film: verbally, musically, visually" (Trinh, 1992, p. 87). This is the APAR claim in its most concentrated form: the research is not something added to the work after its making. It is materialized in every dimension of the making itself. The Void that Trinh invokes throughout *Shoot for the Contents*: the spiritual Void "thanks to which possibilities keep on renewing, hence nothing can be simply classified, arrested and reified": is the same condition the document has been tracing from different directions: the primordial swerve, the clinamen, Barad's indeterminacy, Andersen's fog chamber. It is the ontological ground for the refusal of closure. An economy of suture: filling every empty space, blocking, occupying, talking about: is not rigorous. It is the suppression of what the encounter was producing.

Braidotti's cartography enacts the same principle at the level of method. Being grounded, any cartographic account is "necessarily selective, partial and never exhaustive" (Braidotti, 2018). The refusal of exhaustiveness is not modesty: it is the methodological consequence of an ontology in which phenomena are constituted through intra-action and any account is always already partial, always cutting in a specific way that simultaneously forecloses other possible cuts. The nomadic subject does not produce a map that someone else can follow. She produces an account of her movement through a territory that was itself in motion, attending to what became visible from her specific location at her specific moment: and offering that trace as evidence rather than conclusion.

Schwab formulates this for artistic research practice: the event "may have opened more questions than it answered" is not an admission of failure: it is the signature of work that remained available to what the encounter produced rather than closing around a predetermined outcome. The research contribution of new materialist art practice as research is "generativity over closure": assessed not by what it concluded but by what it made thinkable, what conditions it built, what relational capacities it extended (Schwab, 2018). Pirrò's facta argument runs in the same direction: transpositions resist stabilization into propositional formulation. Relations and differences remain networks of interacting positions: incompressible, not reducible to a single stable claim. The transposition produces knowledge as sequence effects and relational shifts, not as propositional snapshots. This knowledge is grasped from within operations, not from a representational vantage point outside them.

The institutional stakes of this position are not abstract. The traditional research evaluation framework demands a specific form of accountability: the research question is posed in advance, the methods are specified, the findings are reported, the conclusions are stated. This sequence assumes that knowledge is extractable from the encounter: that the researcher enters the field, gathers data, and produces conclusions that travel out of the context of their production. For new materialist art practice as research, every one of these assumptions enacts the suppression the framework is built to resist. The question that drives the research often cannot be fully articulated before the making begins. The methods are inseparable from the encounter. The findings are not propositional statements separable from the practice that generated them. The conclusion, in the traditional sense, is precisely what the practice withholds: because the interval that makes meaning possible cannot be closed without destroying what it was producing.

Andersen is explicit about this cost from the practitioner's position. When required to write academically about her practice, she finds that she "writes like a child": the academic writing does not translate the practice, it overwrites the voice the practice developed (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). The violence is not metaphorical. The demand for propositional conclusions forces the practitioner to perform a kind of knowledge she does not produce, in a register that suppresses the kind she does. What she produces through building her magic machines: the leaky objects that absorb and leak knowledge as they appear, the fog chamber that creates conditions for new thinking, the possibility space held open through the making: is not representable in a findings section. It is indexed in the object, held in the changed relation between practitioner and world that the making produced, traceable in the marks left on bodies. Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch name this at the structural level: artistic research "cannot be deemed successful in simple or finite terms" and does not yield a singular discovery but "opens up new fields of understanding": a research outcome to be assessed through unfolding interactions and capacities rather than through closure (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 5).

The refusal of closure is therefore not a retreat from accountability but a different form of it. What is demanded in its place is not the conclusion but the specification of the apparatus: what was the material-discursive arrangement through which the encounter was staged? What agential cuts did it enact? What was excluded? What marks were left on bodies? These questions are answerable. They are more precisely answerable than the demand for a generalizable finding, because they name what the specific encounter actually produced rather than what a decontextualized claim extracted from it might assert. Accountability without universalism: Braidotti's phrase for the nomadic ethics: is the form of rigor that new materialist APAR demands and, in the strongest of these accounts, delivers.

### Push Back 

Fox & Alldred (2021, applied research) warn that diffractive methodology  
can become "researcher-centric and context-dependent" because the  
analytical cuts are driven by the analyst's decisions, raising issues of  
bias and transferability. They also note that ethico-onto-epistemology  
alone may not supply insight into "how or to what extent" specific  
methods influenced findings.

(Fox & Alldred, 2021)

This push back elucidates the central problem of art practice as  
research within traditional institutions. Critique of APAR methods  
misunderstand that the essential nature and foundational tenants do not  
comport with the structure and desires of traditional research and  
reporting of research findings.

Critique of diffractive practice because of the potential for bias and  
researcher centeredness, missed the necessity of the researcher  
experience investigated horizontally alongside the other participants in  
the assemblage/apparatus. The goal is not to erase the human, but to  
flatten the field and make space for things/matter/material.

The charge of researcher-centrism is itself a representationalist  
criticism. It presupposes that a properly objective method would be one  
from which the researcher has been removed: a view from outside the  
phenomenon, a neutral apparatus that doesn't contaminate what it  
measures. But as Barad makes clear, representationalism is "a prisoner  
of the problematic metaphysics it postulates. Like the frustrated  
would-be runner in Zeno's paradox, it never seems to be able to get any  
closer to solving the problem it poses because it is caught in the  
impossibility of stepping outward from its metaphysical starting place"  
(Barad, 2003, p. 803). The demand that diffractive methodology  
demonstrate its objectivity by removing the researcher from the  
apparatus is not a stricter standard of rigor: it is a return to the  
very representationalist fantasy that diffractive practice is designed  
to refuse. Accountability in diffractive practice does not come from  
removing the researcher. It comes from naming the apparatus, specifying  
the cuts, and tracing the marks left on bodies. That is what objectivity  
means when the observer is understood to be always already part of the  
phenomenon.

Draude and Klumbytė add a further dimension to this pushback by warning  
against idealizing collaborative or hybrid research spaces as neutral  
zones. "Third spaces" and hybrid methodologies must retain their  
postcolonial, decolonial, and feminist stakes, including sustained  
attention to power relations and positionality (Draude & Klumbytė,  
2019). Their insistence that genuinely hybrid methodologies may need to  
be created: rather than simply importing methods from one domain into  
another: is both methodological and political: it rejects extractive  
method transfer and demands the accountable co-constitution of methods  
inside hybrid research spaces. This applies directly to the critique of  
diffractive methodology as researcher-centric: the researcher's  
situatedness, positionality, and power are not contaminants to be  
removed from the apparatus, but constitutive conditions to be named,  
examined, and held accountable. Removing the researcher does not produce  
objectivity; it produces the illusion of objectivity while concealing  
the specific human-nonhuman assemblage that generated the findings.

The necessity of explicitly tying specific methods to research insights  
is ignoring the emergent causality of the field and that the  
entanglement of becoming/knowing/doing is a feature, not a bug.

## Conclusion

The three questions that organize this document are not three separate inquiries. They are three levels of resolution of the same theoretical and methodological project: from the broad ontological commitments shared across posthumanism and new materialism, to their operationalization as diffractive practice, to their instantiation in art practice as research. Because new materialism is nested within the posthumanist theoretical scenario rather than parallel to it (Ferrando, 2013, p. 30), the three questions fold into each other: every account of diffractive practice is simultaneously an account of the theoretical distinctions that make it possible, and every account of new materialist APAR is simultaneously an account of diffractive practice enacted.

What the document has traced, across its three parts, is the argument that practice arrives at the ground that theory then names. Trinh T. Minh-ha was holding the interval open in film form before Barad formalized indeterminacy philosophically. Bolt was working hot with paint before NM had a vocabulary for methexis. The Mosaic Approach was staging co-constitutive listening before Meeting the Universe Halfway was published. The counter-tradition that runs from Lucretius through Spinoza through Bergson through the Macy Conferences through Haraway to Barad was not building toward a predetermined destination. It was a fold: the same ontological commitment kept resurfacing in different registers, kept being covered over, and kept breaking through.

New materialism and posthumanism did not discover that the world is relational, agentive, and generative. They named it: precisely enough, finally, for the naming to travel across disciplines simultaneously and produce the conditions for practice-as-research to be legible as research. The institutional problem remains: evaluation frameworks built to assess knowledge as extractable from its context of production will continue to misread what diffractive APAR produces until those frameworks are themselves transposed. What the practitioners gathered in this document demonstrate is that the transposition is already underway: in studios, workshops, film practice, hackerspace, public site, and turbulence tank. The knowledge is in the doing. The doing has been producing knowledge for longer than the theory has been naming it.

The generativity of diffractive practice is not a methodological weakness to be tolerated. It is its most rigorous commitment: the refusal of exhaustive capture that Trinh named as the condition of non-totalizing knowledge, that Pirrò named as incompressibility, that the fog chamber holds open as possibility space. The document performs this commitment in its own structure: multiple descriptions of the same thing vibrating together, each carrying the specific weight of the theoretical tradition it comes from, none exhausting what the others name. This is posthumanist method, not imprecision. It is what it looks like to take the ontology seriously.
