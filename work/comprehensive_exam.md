# Part One: What are the theoretical and methodological distinctions between posthumanism and new materialism?

---

## § 1. How the Field Answers the Question

Posthumanism and new materialism are two distinct critical traditions that developed in opposition to the same historical, cultural, and economic conditions: the Cartesian-Newtonian settlement that rendered matter inert, Enlightenment humanism that installed the sovereign rational subject at the center of knowledge and value, the industrial and postindustrial orders that depended on both. They share a non-dualistic, non-representationalist, entangled understanding of nature and culture, a vitalist understanding of matter, and an empirical field that no longer takes human exceptionalism as a point of departure (Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 191, citing Coole & Frost, 2010; Bennett, 2010; Braidotti, 2013). Posthumanism focuses on the figure of "Man" — the human universal, the species hierarchy that placed one version of the human above all other forms of life and matter. New materialism focuses on the reduction of the material world to passive substance, available for inscription, extraction, and instrumentalization by an external force. Over time, their unique identities have become more entangled. According to Ferrando (2014, p. 169), new materialism has its own origin story unique from posthumanism, but by the end of the 1990s it had emerged as a specific branch of the posthuman field, and the two now function as necessarily twinned frames through which to navigate the world (Militsi, 2021, p. 11, citing Ferrando, 2013, p. 30). They are emerging continuums of difference, co-constituted, and their combined momentum pushes toward new ethics of what, who, where, when, and why counts as the real, counts as agency, and counts as knowledge.

The commitments they share are not recent. Ferrando (2019) traces them back to Aristotle, Plato, and the Old Testament, where the world was separated into hierarchies of the ideal and the profane, and where thinkers who remained with complexity, flux, and porous boundaries were repeatedly absorbed into frameworks that preserved binary order (p. 170). Spinoza's monism, Whitehead's process philosophy, and phenomenology all resisted that reduction in different ways. Indigenous knowledge systems long predate the Western academic traditions that later marginalized or omitted them from the standard genealogy (Anderson, 2020; Kontturi & Tiainen, 2021; Pennycook, 2024; Torosyan et al., 2021). What changed in the 1990s was not the first appearance of these commitments but the beginning of their consolidation into a shared vocabulary (Ferrando, 2013). Haraway marks the threshold clearly: "bounded individualism in its many flavors in science, politics, and philosophy has finally become unavailable to think with, truly no longer thinkable, technically or any other way" (Haraway, 2016, p. 4).

Philosophically, posthumanism and the new materialisms presuppose that knowing and being are inseparable: "epistemological practices are necessarily ontological practices; to know is to be part of the world's continual construction" (Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 192, citing Barad, 2007, p. 379). As Wolfe insists, to become posthuman is to find a mode of thought adequate to "processes which can never be entirely reduced to patterns or standards, codes or information" (Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii, citing Rutsky). This is the logic Clarke names when quoting Bateson's suggestion that "the whole of logic would have to be reconstructed for recursiveness" (Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii, citing Clarke, citing Bateson, 1972): if the world is recursive, relational, and non-deterministic, then method cannot remain representational, as if it were a neutral mirror standing outside what it studies.

## § 2. The Two Critiques

### § 2.1 The Critique of Man

Posthumanism is not an announcement that the human is finished. It is a critique of a specific figure — the autonomous, sovereign, rational subject installed by Enlightenment humanism — and of the exclusions that produced that figure. Foucault could already see, from within the humanist revolution, that it was contingent: "man is an invention of recent date. And one perhaps nearing its end" (Wolfe, 2010, p. xii, citing Foucault). The "man" at stake is not all humans. It is a particular configuration that van der Tuin and Dolphijn trace to Kant: "Kant started his philosophy with the human being, or even better, with human thought and its relation to nature. In other words, Kant turned (theological) metaphysics into transcendental anthropology" (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 165). After Kant, the human subject becomes the condition of possibility for knowledge itself, the measure against which everything else is placed.

Ferrando (2014) specifies what was installed in the figure of that universal human: a particular type of embodiment — male, white, Western, heterosexual, physically able, propertied — treated as neutral and fit-for-all. The critique of Man is therefore inseparable from the feminist, critical race, queer, disability, and postcolonial traditions that refused to accept the universal as universal. Braidotti (2024) proposes the framework of two discrete but intersecting critiques: the critique of the humanistic "'man of reason'" and the critique of exceptionalist anthropocentrism, or "'anthropolatry'" (p. xii). Together, these critiques expose how Eurocentric universalism and belligerent "'species-ism'" produced exclusionary norms of the human, casting sexualized, racialized, and naturalized others as dehumanized or outside the category of the human (p. xii). Braidotti argues that the notions currently circulating around the posthuman are affirmative because they "break free from the sedimented habits of thought that confirm institutionalised power relations" (p. xii).

Posthumanism is therefore not antihumanism. Transhumanism is antihumanism's mirror: it claims to transcend the human while intensifying the very figure posthumanism rejects. Transhumanism, associated with figures such as Nick Bostrom and Hans Moravec, seeks the enhancement of human intellectual, physical, and emotional capacities, the elimination of disease and suffering, and the extension of human life span, all in service of a figure of the human whose "basic capacities so radically exceed those of present humans as to no longer be unambiguously human by our current standards" (Bostrom, cited in Wolfe, 2010, p. xiii). Wolfe therefore describes transhumanism as "an intensification of humanism" because it extends the prerogatives of the autonomous liberal subject rather than questioning that subject as the basic unit of analysis (Wolfe, 2010, p. xiv). Moravec's fantasy of downloading consciousness into a computer in pursuit of technological immortality continues the humanist investment in disembodied rationality. Posthumanism instead opposes "the fantasies of disembodiment and autonomy, inherited from humanism itself" and insists on embodiment, embeddedness, and materiality (Wolfe, 2010, p. xiv).

What the critique of Man requires is not the end of humans but the end of a particular way of being one: the view from nowhere, the unmarked universal, the subject whose body is not supposed to be part of what he knows.

### § 2.2 The Critique of Inert Matter

The second critique targets a settlement older than humanism and deeper in its effects. Western metaphysics inherited from the atomists — Leucippus, Democritus, Epicurus — a model of matter as inert: tiny, indivisible, passive particles moving through the void, their shapes fixed, their combinations determined by collision. Matter moved when struck. It produced only what its given characteristics permitted. Lucretius extended this in De Rerum Natura in a direction later overshadowed — Gamble, Hanan, and Nail (2019) argue that he anticipates performative new materialism by treating matter as something that ceaselessly constitutes novel entities and relations (pp. 112–113) — but the line that endured was the one that served mechanism. Bacon synthesized theology, naturalism, and mechanism into a single framework in which, as Gamble, Hanan, and Nail summarize him, "the force implanted by God in these first particles makes up all the variety of things" (2019, p. 115). God imparts force to matter, sets the laws of nature in motion, and rests. Matter is what is moved. Force is what moves it. The observer stands apart from both.

Descartes sharpens this into dualism: mind and matter are categorically different substances, and the mind examines the material world from a position of separation. Kant, as already noted, secures this epistemologically. What this settlement produces is a world in which matter can be inscribed, extracted, and instrumentalized without resistance — because matter, by definition, offers none. Coole and Frost (2010) name the consequence: a framework that "yields a conceptual and practical domination of nature as well as a specifically modern attitude or ethos of subjectivist potency" (p. 9). The figure of Man and the figure of inert matter are therefore the same figure, seen from opposite sides. One can only be sovereign over what cannot answer back.

The critique of inert matter refuses this. Barad's formulation is the sharpest: matter is "not a fixed substance; rather, matter is substance in its intra-active becoming — not a thing but a doing, a congealing of agency" and "always already an ongoing historicity" (Barad, 2003, pp. 821, 822). Bennett opposes the parsing of the world into "dull matter (it, things)" and "vibrant life (us, beings)" and asks how political responses would change if we took seriously "the vitality of (nonhuman) bodies" (Bennett, 2010, pp. vii–viii). Braidotti describes philosophical neo-materialism as a line of immanence concerned with "the vital immanence of non-anthropomorphic life-systems," where differences are not constituted oppositionally or dialectically but as "internal modulations within a common matter that is intelligent and self-organised" (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii). Across these accounts, matter is not inert substrate but active, generative, and implicated in the production of worlds.

Coole and Frost (2010) also stress that the new in new materialisms does not deny a "rich materialist heritage" (p. 7). Matter as active, generative, and self-organizing has a longer philosophical history than the recent turn. Spinoza had already made the counter-claim in 1677: the mind is the idea of the body. Mind and body are the same thing. What Spinoza calls conatus, the striving of singular things to persevere in their being, is not something added to matter from outside but immanent to it (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, pp. 95–96). Bergson extended this into a philosophy of duration in which life works to "insert some indetermination into matter," and the forms it creates are therefore "indeterminate, i.e. unforeseeable" (Bennett, 2010, p. 79). Merleau-Ponty extended the emphasis on immanence, process, and embodiment through a phenomenology of perception in which the body is itself a fold, a reversibility that "always miscarries at the last moment" (Coole, 2010, pp. 110–111). This line was not lost. It was kept being marginalized. New materialism's return to matter is, among other things, a recovery.

The recovery also required a reckoning with what postmodernism had done. Ferrando (2013) argues that postmodernism's dismantling of the nature/culture dualism often privileged its nurtural side, producing a constructivist and representationalist radicalization that "somehow lost track of the material" (p. 114). Barad states the consequence directly: "language matters, discourse matters, culture matters," but "the only thing that does not seem to matter anymore is matter" (Barad, 2003, p. 801, cited in Ferrando, 2013, p. 114). New materialism responded not by restoring nature against culture, but by refusing the dualism altogether and treating matter as an ongoing process of materialization that is "dynamic, shifting, inherently entangled, diffractional and performative" (Ferrando, 2013, p. 114). Posthumanism inherits postmodernism's critique of the sovereign subject; new materialism reasserts the material dimension that constructivist accounts subordinated (Ferrando, 2013, pp. 25, 114).

### § 2.3 What the Literature Contests

Not all strands grouped under new materialism are equivalent. Gamble, Hanan, and Rossdale (2019) distinguish among vital materialism, negative materialism, and performative materialism. The present project draws on vital and performative materialisms. Barad's account of posthumanist performativity is especially important because it joins an anti-anthropocentric critique of the human subject to an account of matter as active and co-constitutive. As Militsi (2021) summarizes, Barad presents posthumanist performativity as a "diffractive elaboration" of Butler and Haraway (Barad, 2003, p. 808).

The relation between the two traditions is also contested. Srnicek (2017) groups posthumanism, speculative realism, and object-oriented philosophy as variants within a broader new materialist field, while noting that posthumanism contributes concepts of agency, structure, and epistemology to that grouping. Smelik (2018), by contrast, situates new materialism within a posthuman context, arguing that posthumanism's decentering of the human opened conceptual space for agency to be attributed to things and nature. Wolfe (2010) cautions that some posthumanist scholarship continues to preserve a residual human exceptionalism, even after rejecting the classical figure of Man. Srnicek (2017) notes that some posthumanist approaches preserve distinctions between human and nonhuman systems, especially around self-reflection and intentional action. De Liaño and Fernández-Götz (2021) argue that some elements of New Humanism remain valuable for contemporary social problems. Blok (2024) rejects a symmetrical distribution of conativity and responsivity across all material entities.

Both frameworks, by design, travel across fields. Posthumanism can apply to "any field of enquiry, starting from our location as a species, to the individual gaze" (Ferrando, 2014, p. 168); Wolfe demonstrates this reach across animal studies, disability studies, bioethics, literary and cultural studies, architecture, systems theory, and poetry (Wolfe, 2010). Van der Tuin and Dolphijn (2010) identify new materialism's strength as "this nomadic traversing of the territories of science and the humanities, performing the agential or non-innocent nature of all matter" (pp. 158–159). Alaimo and Hekman find material feminism operating across "science studies, environmental feminisms, corporeal feminisms, queer theory, disability studies, theories of race and ethnicity, environmental justice, (post-)Marxist feminism, globalization studies, and cultural studies" (Alaimo & Hekman, 2008, pp. 9–10, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 103). Siffrinn and Coda's (2025) survey of posthumanist and new materialist research in applied linguistics finds that "these theories are being operationalized by scholars in the field on a larger scale than is being returned in research databases" (p. 194).

## § 3. What the Two Critiques Combined Give Us

### § 3.1 Create the world

Once the figure of Man and the figure of inert matter are both refused, what is left is not nothing. It is a world that has been there all along, now reportable because the frames that obscured it have been set down.

The Wild. The void. The absolute. Non-identity. A life. The primordial swerve. Zoe. The virtual. The plane of immanence. These are not synonyms in the strict sense; each carries the weight of the theoretical tradition from which it comes. But they point toward the same thing. What they name is a ground that exists before and after any specific intra-action, outside any particular agential cut, beyond the reach of any apparatus that would make it measurable. It is the field of virtual potential from which phenomena emerge when conditions for their emergence are met, and into which they dissolve when the apparatus that held them in place is released.

This is a description of how reality actually works. In quantum physics, before measurement, before the agential cut that produces a phenomenon, there is superposition: not multiple things coexisting but a state of ontological indeterminacy in which the boundaries and properties of the "thing" have not yet been enacted. The apparatus does not reveal what was already there. It brings forth what was not yet determined. Between measurements, between intra-actions, what exists is this indeterminate field, the real-but-virtual, the condition from which specific configurations of time/space/matter emerge and into which they return. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle was often read as a limit on knowledge produced by measurement. Bohr's intervention was to relocate the problem from uncertainty, understood as a limit on knowledge, to indeterminacy, understood as a feature of the phenomenon itself (Barad, in Dolphijn & van der Tuin, 2012, p. 63). Barad states the point directly: quantum entities "do not have an inherent ontological identity," and determinate properties do not preexist the phenomenon (Barad, 2011, p. 127).

The difference between uncertainty and indeterminacy is not technical. It is the difference between a world that is already there and waiting to be discovered, and a world that becomes determinate only through specific material arrangements. The second world is what quantum physics demonstrates. Barad reads Bohr as having advanced an ontic interpretation against the epistemic interpretations of Schrödinger and Heisenberg: entities "do not determinately exist apart from the particular, physical measuring apparatuses that constitute them one way to the exclusion of others" (Gamble, Hanan, & Nail, 2019, p. 122). Phenomena, in Barad's account, "do not merely mark the epistemological inseparability of 'observer' and 'observed'; rather phenomena are the ontological inseparability of intra-acting 'agencies.' That is, phenomena are ontological entanglements" (Barad, 2007, p. 333). There is no prior, independently determinate reality waiting to be measured. "Space, time, and matter do not exist prior to the intra-actions that reconstitute the entanglements" (Barad, 2007, p. 74).

Other traditions name this ground in their own registers. Deleuze, as Cheah (2010) reports, calls it the transcendental field and the plane of immanence — "the real 'condition of possibility' of the actual," "made up of pre-individual and impersonal singularities" (pp. 84–85). Matter on this account is "the unformed, unorganized, nonstratified, or destratified body and all its flows: subatomic and submolecular particles, pure intensities, prevital and prephysical free singularities" (p. 87). The virtual is real: "every real object has a virtual content . . . The virtual must be defined as strictly a part of the real object" (p. 85). Actualization does not unfold a preformed possibility. It determines a virtual field that is already real.

Haudenosaunee and Anishinaabe scholar Vanessa Watts names the same ground through Place-Thought: "the non-distinctive space where place and thought were never separated because they never could or can be separated. Place-Thought is based upon the premise that land is alive and thinking and that humans and non-humans derive agency through the extensions of these thoughts" (Watts, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 211). Zoe Todd argues that Indigenous thinkers deserve recognition for "their millennia of engagement with sentient environments, with cosmologies that enmesh people into complex relationships between themselves and all relations, and with climates and atmospheres as important points of organization and action" (Todd, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 210). Anderson, following Braidotti, argues that placing different orders of knowledge in dialogue "does not mean creating false equivalences" but instead "aims at amplifying possible resonances between them" (Braidotti, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 205). The point is not equivalence but resonance. Indigenous frameworks and new materialist ontology converge at important points, and the omission of Indigenous scholarship from standard origin stories of new materialism is a structural feature of how those stories are told.

Braidotti calls the force that moves through this ground zoe: life as a cosmic force that precedes and exceeds any individual subject, any bounded organism, any particular moment of consciousness. The subject is a temporary intensification of this force, not its owner. "This nonanthropocentric view expresses both a profound love for Life as a cosmic force and the desire to depersonalize subjective life-and-death. This is just one life, not my life. The life in 'me' does not answer to my name: 'I' is just passing" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 210). Zoe is distinct from bios, the politically qualified, institutionally recognized, humanist life. Bios is the life the institution counts. Zoe is the life that was always already happening before the institution arrived to count it. Posthumanism's decentering of the human subject is not nihilism; it is the recognition that bios has been mistaken for the whole of life, and zoe for mere background.

### § 3.2 Inhabit the world

If this is what the world is, then we are already in it, and always have been.

Alaimo names what this looks like at the level of a body. "Trans-corporeality means that all creatures, as embodied beings, are intermeshed with the dynamic, material world, which crosses through them, transforms them, and is transformed by them" (Alaimo, forthcoming, p. 1). The figure-ground relation between organism and environment dissolves as the outline of the human is traversed by substantial material interchanges. The trans-corporeal subject is generated through and entangled with biological, technological, economic, social, and political systems at vastly different scales (Alaimo, forthcoming, pp. 1–4). The ontological ground is therefore not elsewhere than embodiment. It is in the traffic through bodies.

Bennett makes the same claim from another direction. Human power is also a kind of thing-power. From the perspective of evolutionary time, mineral efficacy becomes visible in the emergence of bone, and Vernadsky's formulation follows: "We are walking, talking minerals" (Bennett, 2010, pp. 10–12). The human is not an exception to matter but one of its temporary organizations. Bennett extends this further in her discussion of microbial life: "the its outnumber the mes," so that it is no longer adequate to imagine the body as a single bounded being. "We are, rather, an array of bodies, many different kinds of them in a nested set of microbiomes" (Bennett, 2010, p. 113). What appears as an individual subject is already a heterogeneous compound, a swarm, an assemblage of lively materials whose coordination is real but never absolute.

The unit of reality adequate to this inhabitation is not the thing but the phenomenon. For Bennett, it is the thing or actant within a distributed agency of assemblages (Bennett, 2010, pp. viii–ix, 37–38). For Barad, it is the phenomenon produced through specific material-discursive practices and agential cuts (Barad, 2007, pp. 128–129, 146, 175–176). For Braidotti, it is the nomadic subject traced through cartographies and transpositions (Braidotti, 2018, pp. 26–29). What these accounts share is a refusal of the singular as basic. The body is an array. The researcher is an assemblage. The subject is nomadic. None of these are metaphors; they are reports of what becomes visible once the bounded individual is no longer the unit of analysis.

Leibniz, in 1714, had already sat with this. The Monadology is not a treatise proposing that matter is folded. It is what someone writes when they have actually stayed with the folding, in the body, and found no voids, no discrete units, only the continuous pressure of external forces holding provisional form against dissolution. Leibnizean matter has no voids: "folds replace holes," and matter offers, as Deleuze glosses him, "an infinitely porous, spongy, or cavernous texture without emptiness," each body elastic, "pierced with irregular passages," dependent for its coherence on the pressure of external forces (Coole, 2010, p. 111). The world here is not made of discrete units standing in external relation, but of folds — matter endlessly folding and unfolding, generating provisional forms as styles of existing rather than fixed substances. It is not the flat geography of Cartesian extension, laid out before the lumen naturale "without dark recesses, crevices, or hollows" (Coole, 2010, p. 94). It is a topography of curves, labyrinths, and hollows, coiled and creased and rippled, with clarity "endlessly plunging into obscurity" (Coole, 2010, p. 111). The exemplary science of this ontology is not Euclidean geometry but origami, and its afterlives run through the pre-Socratics, Whitehead, postclassical physics, and the folded proteins of microbiology (Coole, 2010, p. 110). Leibniz himself is explicit that the machine cannot account for what this ontology describes. Supposing a machine "so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception," he writes, one could enlarge it and walk inside "as into a mill," and examining its interior would find "only parts which work one upon another, and never anything by which to explain a perception" (Monadology, §17). He is reporting bodily knowledge. He has felt perception from inside his own folding body and is telling us that mechanism cannot account for what he has actually experienced.

Merleau-Ponty reads the same ontology through the body. The body is itself a fold: a reversibility that "always miscarries at the last moment," producing the dehiscence through which emergence remains possible rather than closing into inert repetition (Coole, 2010, p. 107). This is a teeming, turbulent, convoluted world of infinite folded matter, continuously folding and unfolding, its modulations yielding "provisional forms as styles of existing" rather than fixed substances (Coole, 2010, p. 111). The body does not perceive the world from a detached point of view. It does not inhabit a flat, geometrical, fully determinate plane but a milieu, an Umwelt that remains ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant with expressive significance. Cézanne tried to paint this: the object "in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes" (Coole, 2010, p. 104). Cézanne's practice is lingering rendered in paint. The eye stays with the emergence long enough to let it emerge. He is not representing an object that was already there. He is staying in the interval where the object is becoming determinate, and painting from inside that staying.

Bennett names this staying as the practice adequate to inhabiting this world. She calls for methods that cultivate a "patient, sensory attentiveness to nonhuman forces operating outside and inside the human body" (Bennett, 2010, p. xiv). Without this "countercultural kind of perceiving," the world appears as if it consists only of "active human subjects who confront passive objects and their law-governed mechanisms" (Bennett, 2010, p. xiv). The corrective is to linger. To stay long enough in the encounter that the shimmer of the thing's participation in the larger field becomes briefly perceptible. Bennett reads Adorno's Negative Dialectics as a source of practical comportments — intellectual and aesthetic exercises that support this perceptual retraining: attending to the qualitative singularity of the object, exercising the utopian imagination to recreate what conceptualization has flattened, admitting a playful element into inquiry, and making conceptualization itself an object of thought. Together, these constitute "the self-criticism of conceptualization, a sensory attentiveness to the qualitative singularities of the object, the exercise of an unrealistic imagination, and the courage of a clown" (Bennett, 2010, p. 15).

Bennett renders the Deleuzian virtual in her own key as "a life": "a pure event, freed from the subjectivity and objectivity of what happens," "a subjective current visible only fleetingly" that "tears the fabric of the actual without ever fully coming out in a person placed or thing"; it is "matter of movement or matter energy, a matter in variation that enters assemblages and leaves them" (Bennett, 2010, p. 53). A life is not the life of a subject. It is what runs through the temporary organizations of matter that the inhabited body turns out to be.

### § 3.3 Navigate the world

A knower who is part of what she knows cannot know from above. She has to know from where she is. This is not a limitation on knowledge. It is a condition of knowledge, and naming it is what makes it answerable.

Adrienne Rich names the body as the starting point. In Of Woman Born, she writes:

> Patriarchal thought has limited female biology to its own narrow specifications. The feminist vision has recoiled from female biology for these reasons; it will, I believe, come to view our physicality as a resource, rather than a destiny. In order to live a fully human life we require not only control of our bodies (though control is a prerequisite); we must touch the unity and resonance of our physicality, our bond with the natural order, the corporeal ground of our intelligence. (Rich, 1976, p. 40)

In Blood, Bread, and Poetry, Rich states the methodological principle directly: "Begin not with a continent or a country or a house, but with the geography closest in: the body" (Rich, 1984, p. 212). Braidotti identifies this politics of location as "the cornerstone of the neo-materialist feminist project" (Braidotti, 2022, p. 22), tracing a line from Rich through situated knowledges and standpoint debates. Bennett identifies "feminist studies of the body" as part of the larger trend that put "desire and bodily practices back on the ethical radar screen," a key precondition for her own move toward nonhuman vitality (Bennett, 2010, p. xi).

Trinh T. Minh-ha brings the politics of location and the refusal of closure into film practice. In "Documentary Is/Not a Name," she argues that "meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown," and identifies "the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" as the condition that prevents closure (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). Haraway later takes up Trinh's formulation of "critical difference within" in "The Promises of Monsters" (1992, pp. 299–300), linking this refusal of closure to feminist accounts of situated knowledge and difference. Haraway pushes the epistemological argument further in "Situated Knowledges." The "god trick," the claim to disembodied universal vision, is not a neutral description of scientific method but an effect of power. Against it, Haraway argues for "politics and epistemologies of location, positioning, and situating, where partiality and not universality is the condition of being heard to make rational knowledge claims" (Haraway, 1988, p. 589).

Barad's Meeting the Universe Halfway (2007) is where these threads are most systematically brought together, but Timeto (2011) shows that the linkage was already underway: constrained constructivism, situated knowledges, and agential realism can be read as three connected stages in a single argument.

The first stage is Hayles's constrained constructivism, which Timeto reads as an attempt to escape the deadlock between realism and anti-realism without abandoning representation entirely. Hayles's key move is to replace congruence with consistency as the criterion for adequate representation. Congruence implies a one-to-one correspondence between signs and things — the classical representationalist demand that a representation mirror its object. Consistency asks something different: not whether a representation accurately reflects reality, but whether it is viable, whether it holds at the cusp between the constructed concepts through which we know the world and the flux those concepts can never fully capture. The flux is "inherently unknowable and unreachable by any sentient being" (Hayles [1991] 1997, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 157); what we can know are the constraints that make certain representations consistent with it rather than others. Representations, on this account, are "species-specific, culturally determined and context-dependent processes of dynamic interaction" (ibid., p. 157) rather than neutral transcriptions. Constrained constructivism establishes that the observer is always already in a feedback loop with what she observes and that the zone of interaction between observer and flux is the site where knowledge occurs. It does not yet account for how the observer's specific position shapes that knowledge, or why positionality is a condition of knowing rather than contamination.

The second stage is Haraway's situated knowledges, which extends Hayles's cusp into an explicitly political and epistemological account of location. Where Hayles establishes that the observer is always embedded in the interaction, Haraway insists that the specific coordinates of that embedding are not incidental variables but constitutive of what becomes knowable. The partiality of the situated perspective is not a concession to limitation; it is, as Timeto reads Haraway, "the whole that we see as the result of contextual and specific interactions with the flux" (Timeto, 2011, p. 159). Haraway's figurations do the work that mere positional disclosure cannot: they are "performative images that can be inhabited" (Haraway, 1997, p. 11, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 160), not static pictures of the world but representations that remain adequate insofar as they keep their performativity alive. Figurations do not stand outside what they describe; they are living maps always generated from somewhere, from below and within the networks of technobiopower, and they work by putting boundaries in "constructive and transformative tension rather than using them as dividing lines" (Timeto, 2011, p. 161). Diffraction enters here as Haraway's methodological name for this different optics: rather than reflection, which "only displaces the same elsewhere" (Haraway, 1997, p. 16, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 163), diffraction maps where the effects of differences appear, registering interference and interaction rather than mirroring a pre-given original. Situated knowledges argues that partiality names where knowledge sees from, and that naming is what makes it answerable. It does not yet state that the phenomenon itself is constituted through the intra-action of apparatus and matter, or that the cut producing the phenomenon is simultaneously an ethical act.

The third stage is Barad's agential realism, which extends the movement prepared by Hayles and Haraway. Barad, as Timeto reads her, accepts Haraway's diffractive methodology and grounds it in a thoroughgoing ontological claim: representation is not a sign that mirrors a separate external referent but "a diffractive practice that reveals the co-emergence and the co-implication of both meaning and matter" (Timeto, 2011, p. 164). The apparatus does not reveal what was already there; it is constitutive of the phenomenon it produces. Timeto identifies the continuity between all three stages at precisely this point: Barad's "constraining intra-actions" return directly to Hayles's consistency, since constraints are what enable the selection among viable representations and shift the question from what we could see in isolation to "the interaction with reality we see when we see" (Hayles [1991] 1997, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 164). The observer does not stand in a separate domain but participates in the "world's differential becoming" (Barad, 2007, p. 91, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 164), engaging in "specific worldly configurations from the inside" (ibid.). Agential realism adds that the cut producing a determinate phenomenon from an indeterminate field is not a free interpretive choice but a material event with ethical weight: "particular possibilities for acting exist at every moment, and these changing possibilities entail a responsibility to intervene in the world's becoming, to contest and rework what matters and what is excluded from mattering" (Barad, 2003, pp. 826–827, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 164). Representation is not removed from an originary source. It is the cut that produces the things it appears merely to describe.

Posthumanist and new materialist frameworks do not stop at the critique of unlocated knowledge. They actively redefine the researcher's relation to the research. If there is no phenomenon prior to the apparatus that produces it, and if matter and meaning emerge through specific material-discursive arrangements, then there is no outside position from which the researcher can observe without implication. Barad's account of agential realism, as summarized by Timeto, rejects separation as foundational and defines representation as an intra-active practice embedded in sociotechnical networks. "Entangled agencies" do not precede their relations; they emerge through them (Timeto, 2011, pp. 153, 164). The researcher is part of the apparatus.

Braidotti traces feminist neo-materialism to Rich's politics of location. She identifies "feminist philosophies of neo-materialism and diversity" as a fundamental source for navigating the posthuman convergence and defines the politics of location as "a method based on the notions of embodiment and lived experience" (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii). Feminist theory, she writes, emphasizes the "situated and accountable nature of knowledge," introduces "the embodied and embedded," and holds that "Being embodied — sexed and gendered subjects embedded in the world — is the starting point for critical reflection" (Braidotti, 2024, pp. xvii–xviii). Bodily materialism produces "a new empirical-discursive methodological approach that combines critique with the creation of alternatives" (Braidotti, 2024, p. xviii). Braidotti's nomadic subject and cartography give this orientation its methodological form: nomadic because the knower moves rather than settles, cartographic because the account is a map drawn from somewhere rather than a picture taken from above.

Somerville names the condition in research practice as the post-human I. Accompanying children on weekly creek walks, she writes: "I feel as if I am changed through these videos, learning to be human differently in the company of the creek and children" (Somerville, 2016, p. 1165), and later: "I become other-to-myself, as if I migrate outwards into these scenes that form and reform around me" (Somerville, 2016, p. 1167). There is, she writes, no sense of following a research question and finding an answer, only "complete immersion in the event of place" (Somerville, 2016, p. 1167, citing Massey, 2005). The account remains hers, but the "I" is no longer outside the field it describes.

What this navigation requires of method, the literature names in a family of oriented commitments. Methods are cartographic rather than classificatory: they trace forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold rather than assigning phenomena to pre-existing categories. They are accountable to the apparatus: the instrument of knowing is not neutral with respect to what it knows but constitutive of it, and rigor lies in naming and specifying the apparatus rather than treating it as absent (Barad, 2007, pp. 29–30, 140–141). They are affirmative rather than only critical: they attend not only to what is wrong but also to what is being produced and what is becoming possible (Braidotti, 2024, pp. xiii, xviii). They refuse exhaustive capture: generativity is treated as a condition of accountable knowledge in a world of ongoing becoming. They are situated: every account is grounded in specific political and material conditions rather than in a claim to universality that suppresses the apparatus that produced it (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii). Ferrando names one failure mode directly: "hegemonic essentialism," the habit of producing a posthumanist text in content while framing it within the hegemonic traditions posthumanism sets out to unsettle. As she writes, "A text written by such standards might claim a posthuman content, but does not appear posthuman in its praxis. Its inner contradiction is obvious: while attempting to produce a discourse critical of humanism, it uncritically frames itself within those same traditions from which humanism developed" (Ferrando, 2012, p. 12). Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch generalize the commitment: "research methods do not only observe and represent materials, issues, and events but in fact act upon and intervene in these materials, issues, and events" (2020, p. 7).

Kontturi and Tiainen extend this historically, arguing that art practice was not simply a later application of new materialist thought but "one of the very milieus in which the call to rethink the involvement of matter in human practices and in the more-than-human world arose" (Kontturi & Tiainen, 2021, p. 259). Art practice as research is one of the sites in which posthuman and new materialist methodological commitments were enacted, and Part Three returns to the specific art-theoretical lineages — Bolt's account of working hot, the recovery of matter in visual theory — that make this visible from inside practice.

### § 3.4 Affirmative ethics guides us through the world

If the world is as § 3.1 reports it, and we inhabit it as § 3.2 reports, and we know it from inside as § 3.3 reports, then the question of what to do follows from the question of where we are. Affirmative ethics is the name the field gives to the answer.

For Braidotti, the raison d'être of the whole project is affirmative ethics, and its ontological ground is zoe. Because the individual "I" is not the center of life but a temporary passing of it, the logic of retribution, rights-claims, and compensation — all premised on a stable individual subject — dissolves. What replaces it is an "ethics of affirmation" that "suspends the quest for both claims and compensation and resists the logic of retribution or rights" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 214). In Transpositions, she writes that "an ethics of sustainability" structures her argument and that "this transformative ethics includes a critical or reactive and an affirmative or active phase" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). She asks how to cultivate "the political desire for change or transformation" and describes this as a change in both "our schemes of thought" and "our ways of inhabiting the world" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). In later work she states that "our politics must be guided by an affirmative ethics" and defines ethics as "a discourse about forces and relations, and not as a set of normative moral injunctions" (Braidotti, 2024, p. xiii). Bodily materialism, in this account, produces "a new empirical-discursive methodological approach that combines critique with the creation of alternatives" (Braidotti, 2024, p. xiii). Ethics is not a set of rules the subject applies. It is what happens when the subject recognizes she is a temporary intensification of zoe and orients herself toward what that recognition makes possible.

Haraway frames the same orientation through response and relation. In Staying with the Trouble, she describes the task as "learning to stay with the trouble of living and dying in response-ability on a damaged earth" (Haraway, 2016, p. 2). On the opening page, she writes that the task is "to make kin in lines of inventive connection as a practice of learning to live and die well with each other in a thick present" (Haraway, 2016, p. 1). Response-ability is not responsibility in the sense of blame or obligation. It is the cultivated capacity to respond — to be, as the word contains, able to respond — to what is actually happening in the thick present where one is already located.

Bennett does not use the phrase affirmative ethics in Vibrant Matter, but she explicitly presents her project in ethical and political terms. The political project, she writes, is "to encourage more intelligent and sustainable engagements with vibrant matter and lively things." She asks how political responses might change if we took seriously "the vitality of (nonhuman) bodies," defining vitality as the capacity of things not only to impede human designs but also to act "as quasi agents or forces with trajectories, propensities, or tendencies of their own" (Bennett, 2010, p. viii). She also writes that "alongside and inside singular human agents there exists a heterogeneous series of actants with partial, overlapping, and conflicting degrees of power and effectivity" (Bennett, 2010, p. 23). Later in the preface, she describes the ethical task as cultivating "the ability to discern nonhuman vitality" and becoming "perceptually open to it" (Bennett, 2010, p. xiv). Ethics, for Bennett, begins with a perceptual discipline — the same lingering and attunement that § 3.2 named as the practice of inhabiting the world. You cannot treat what you cannot perceive as mattering. The practice of perceiving is therefore already ethics.

Barad makes ethics a matter of accountability for the cuts that enact inclusion and exclusion. This is where the architecture of § 3.1, § 3.2, and § 3.3 resolves into a single operation. The agential cut is the site where the indeterminate field becomes a determinate phenomenon. It is where ontology happens. It is also where the researcher's position materializes as a specific engagement rather than a neutral observation. It is therefore, simultaneously, the site of objective knowledge and the site of ethical responsibility. "Objectivity," Barad writes, means "being accountable for marks on bodies, that is, specific materializations in their differential mattering." "We are responsible for the cuts that we help enact," and "the cuts that we participate in enacting matter" (Barad, 2007, p. 179). Because "intra-actions cut 'things' together and apart," ethics is not about a radically exterior other, since "'they' and 'we' are co-constituted and entangled through the very cuts 'we' help to enact" (Barad, 2007, p. 179). Agency, in this framework, includes "the possibilities and accountability entailed in reconfiguring material-discursive apparatuses of bodily production, including the boundary articulations and exclusions that are marked by those practices" (Barad, 2007, p. 214). Acknowledgment of "nonhuman agency" does not reduce human responsibility. It requires "that much more attentiveness to existing power asymmetries" (Barad, 2007, p. 219).

What this means, taken together, is that the cut is not something that happens to the researcher from outside and then raises the question of what she should do about it. The cut is the researcher's enacted relation to the phenomenon. What she knows is produced by the cut. What she is accountable for is the cut. The question of ethics and the question of objectivity are the same question, answered by the same act. This is the sense in which the agential cut is, as Barad puts it, the site at which responsibility and possibility meet: "particular possibilities for acting exist at every moment, and these changing possibilities entail a responsibility to intervene in the world's becoming, to contest and rework what matters and what is excluded from mattering" (Barad, 2003, pp. 826–827).

Affirmative ethics is therefore not an add-on to the theoretical framework of posthumanism and new materialism. It is what the framework yields once its ontological, epistemological, and methodological commitments are taken as a single motion. The subject who has recognized zoe, who has inhabited her body as trans-corporeal array, who has located herself as knower rather than claimed the god trick, has by that recognition already entered the frame of response and accountability. Braidotti names this as the raison d'être of the whole. Haraway names it as response-ability. Bennett names it as perceptual discipline. Barad names it as the agential cut as the site of objectivity and ethics at once. They are not four positions. They are four naming of what it means to live and work inside the world that § 3.1 reported.

## § 4. Closing of Part One

The distinction between posthumanism and new materialism across Part One maps different emphases, genealogies, and methodological centers of gravity. Posthumanism critiques the figure of Man; new materialism critiques the reduction of matter to passive substance. Both frames refuse dualism, representationalism, and the view from nowhere. What they yield together is a world that is relational, processual, and virtual before it is actual, inhabited by bodies that are arrays rather than singulars, navigated by situated knowers who are part of the apparatus that produces what they know, and lived within as the ongoing work of accountable response to what each cut makes possible.

The literature reported here converges on a particular account of what rigor looks like under these commitments. Trinh names it through the interval, "a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). Braidotti names it through cartography, whose accounts are "necessarily sequential, partial and unfinished" because they are grounded (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii). Barad names it through the agential cut, always one possibility among others, never a neutral disclosure of what was already there (Barad, 2007, p. 179). Bennett names it through lingering, the patient attentiveness that makes the shimmer of the thing briefly perceptible (Bennett, 2010, pp. xiv, 15). What these formulations share is a treatment of generativity rather than exhaustive capture as the condition of accountable knowledge in a world of ongoing becoming.

Part Two extends Part One with the question: if posthumanism and new materialism together establish that the world is relational, processual, and virtual before it is actual, then the question becomes how to inhabit and work within that world as a researcher and practitioner. Part Two reports diffractive practice, transpositions, making strange, and the practitioner accounts of method as the named operations through which this work is conducted.


# Part Two: Relational Methods

## § 1. Why This Question Follows from Part One

Part One established that posthumanism and new materialism together describe a world that is relational before it is individual, processual before it is stable, and virtual before it is actual — a world in which the researcher is part of the apparatus that produces what she knows, and in which accountability for the cuts she helps enact is simultaneously the site of objective knowledge and ethical responsibility. Part Two asks what practice looks like for someone who has taken this seriously.

The original framing of the second question named "diffractive practice" as its target. That framing remains useful but requires widening. The literature does not yet name diffraction and transposition as a joint category. What it does show is a family of named operations — diffractive practice (Barad, Haraway), transpositions (Braidotti), becoming-diffractive (Hill), diffractive watching and watching diffractively (Militsi), mosaic listening (Clark and Moss, extended by Dudley), making strange (Shklovsky, Brecht, extended by Andersen and Schwab) — that share the same ontological ground without sharing a common name. The umbrella that holds them together is not a label but a set of commitments: refusal of representational distance, decentering of the human, distributed agency, accountability to the apparatus rather than to an external canon, ethics as ongoing negotiation rather than fixed rule. Protocols named as diffractive practice, transposition, or making strange are what happens when these commitments are put to work simultaneously.

Part Two organizes the literature into three named method families — diffractive practice, transpositions, and making strange — and then reports a group of named practitioner accounts of method in the terms their authors themselves use. The three families are named by the theorists and practitioners who adopt them. The practitioner accounts are reported under their own names because filing them under a family they do not claim would be interpretation rather than report. Each section names its terms from inside the literature. The organization is a way in, not a classification.

If the world is constituted through relation and intra-action — if phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, if agency is distributed across assemblages of human and nonhuman actants, if matter is vibratory and generative rather than inert — then methods that treat phenomena as discrete, stable, and pre-given do not merely produce incomplete findings. They produce a systematically distorted account of what the world is. The mismatch between a relational ontology and a representationalist method is not a technical problem to be corrected through rigor. It is an ethical problem: to impose a subject-object framework on a relational world is to enact the very suppression of vitality, agency, and entanglement that posthumanism and new materialism were built to refuse.

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn (2010) name the methodological consequence directly. A relational world requires cartography rather than classification as its basic posture. Classification is a territorializing gesture that assigns new phenomena to pre-existing categories and forecloses what the encounter might otherwise disclose. Cartography is generative: it maps forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold, producing a "living map" — Braidotti's phrase — rather than a fixed taxonomy. The shift from classification to cartography is the methodological consequence of the ontological shift from substance to relation. Relational worlds cannot be classified into stability. They can only be mapped in motion. Haraway had already named the choice at the level of research: reflexivity, she argues, "like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere, setting up the worries about copy and original and the search for the authentic and really real." What is needed instead is to "diffract the rays of technoscience so that we get more promising interference patterns on the recording films of our lives and bodies" (Haraway, 1997, p. 16). Diffraction, in Barad's development of the same distinction, produces a pattern of the effects of difference, not a map of where differences are located in things (Barad, 2007, citing Haraway).

The phenomenon, Barad's basic ontological unit, is what makes participation concrete. Phenomena are not objects observed by a detached researcher. They are material-discursive arrangements produced through specific intra-actions of apparatus, matter, and meaning. Relata do not pre-exist these arrangements; they emerge within them. The diffraction pattern is what the phenomenon looks like from inside the apparatus. There is no view from outside. The interference pattern — the research finding — is always a pattern produced by a specific encounter between specific forces at a specific moment. It is not less rigorous for that. It is more precisely accountable: accountable to the apparatus that produced it, to the cuts it enacted, to the marks it left.

## § 2. Diffractive Practice

Diffractive practice names a family of methods that reject representational distance and treat inquiry as participation in the production of the phenomenon under study. Across the texts gathered here, method proceeds through situated partiality, non-objectifying relation, and accountability to the apparatus rather than through neutral observation from outside. What matters methodologically is not comparison between fixed terms but the tracing of interference patterns, the specification of cuts, and the maintenance of the intervals through which something new becomes perceptible.

### § 2.1 Trinh

Trinh T. Minh-ha develops diffractive practice in filmmaking before the name. As a filmmaker, artist, and anthropologist working in Senegal, Vietnam, the United States, and across the postcolonial world, she inhabits multiple positions without resolving them into a stable identity. Her contribution to diffractive practice begins from location and relation rather than from detached observation. In the 1992 conversation "Speaking Nearby," she opposes the tendency to treat situations as if they were only outside the subject and describes another orientation: learning to "know the world inwardly," so that "the deeper we go into ourselves, the wider we go into society." She describes each work as an attempt to inscribe "this constant flow from the inside out and outside in" (Trinh, 1992, p. 82). Knowledge here is located, relational, and inseparable from the place from which one speaks.

That orientation takes methodological form in "speaking nearby." Trinh defines it as "a speaking that does not objectify, does not point to an object as if it is distant from the speaking subject or absent from the speaking place," and as "a speaking that reflects on itself and can come very close to a subject without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, pp. 86–87). She is clear that this is not merely verbal. It is "an attitude in life, a way of positioning oneself in relation to the world," and the challenge is "to materialize it in all aspects of the film: verbally, musically, visually" (p. 87).

In "Documentary Is/Not a Name," Trinh names the anti-closure principle that follows from this position. "Meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown," and what persists between meaning and truth is "the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). Two years later, in the "Speaking Nearby" conversation, she describes such closures as "only moments of transition opening up to other possible moments of transition" (Trinh, 1992, p. 87). The point is not indeterminacy for its own sake. It is to resist the seizure of the object by a form of knowledge that completes too quickly.

In the footnote to her 2003 essay, Barad cites Haraway's account of Trinh as looking for a way to figure difference as a "critical difference within," not as "special taxonomic marks grounding difference as apartheid," and quotes Haraway's formulation of diffraction as "a mapping of interference, not of replication, reflection, or reproduction" (Barad, 2003, p. 804 n.3, citing Haraway, 1992, p. 300). Trinh's account of relation, interval, and non-objectifying proximity is part of the genealogy through which diffraction becomes a feminist method before Barad gives it a more ontological and material-discursive formulation.

### § 2.2 Haraway

Haraway introduces diffraction as a feminist optical metaphor before Barad formalizes it philosophically, and she does so in dialogue with Trinh. In "The Promises of Monsters" (1992), Haraway takes up Trinh's formulation of difference as a "critical difference within" and extends it: "Diffraction does not produce 'the same' displaced, as reflection and refraction do. Diffraction is a mapping of interference, not of replication, reflection, or reproduction. A diffraction pattern does not map where differences appear, but rather maps where the effects of differences appear" (Haraway, 1992, pp. 299–300). This is the origin of the diffraction metaphor in feminist theory: generated from a reading of Trinh, not from quantum physics alone. By 1997, in Modest_Witness, Haraway develops diffraction into what Barad later calls a "fourth semiotic category," connecting it to her broader critique of reflexivity: "reflexivity, like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere," whereas diffraction produces genuinely new interference patterns (Haraway, 1997, p. 16). Haraway's version of diffractive practice is inseparable from her commitment to figuration: representations that are "performative images that can be inhabited," partial maps accountable to their situatedness (Haraway, 1997, p. 11).

### § 2.3 Barad

For Barad, diffractive practice is inseparable from the apparatus. The apparatus is not a tool the researcher picks up and puts down. It is the material-discursive arrangement — the configuration of instruments, concepts, bodies, institutions, and practices — through which phenomena are produced. The researcher is always already part of the apparatus; there is no view from outside. Diffractive practice, in Barad's version, means making the apparatus clear: naming it, specifying the agential cuts it makes, and tracing the marks it leaves on bodies. The agential cut is not a free interpretive choice. It is the determinate event through which boundaries are enacted, relata emerge, and accountability is located. Diffractive practice is rigorous precisely because it specifies these cuts rather than naturalizing them. The question it asks is not "what does this mean" but "what does this configuration of differences produce, and what does it foreclose." Its ethical force comes from the insistence that exclusions are constitutive: what the apparatus does not produce is as significant as what it does, and the researcher is response-able for those constitutive exclusions (Barad, 2003; 2007).

Barad then takes Haraway's diffraction metaphor and grounds it in quantum field theory, making the metaphor into an ontological claim: phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, and diffraction is not only a way of reading but a description of how the world is constituted (Barad, 2003, citing Haraway, 1992, p. 300).

### § 2.4 Diffractive Practice in Education

The five educators gathered here work across levels and contexts — from early years practice to teacher education to undergraduate curriculum — and each one attaches diffractive practice to specific educational acts. Taken together they show diffractive practice not as a fixed procedure but as a commitment to materially situated inquiry, multiple forms of attunement, and knowledge-making through entangled practice.

Hill, writing in teacher education at Simon Fraser University, gives the clearest methodological distinction. Her aim is not to replace reflective practice with diffractive practice, but to move "beyond reflective practice" by distinguishing "diffractive reading" from "experimenting with a diffractive apparatus" (Hill, 2017, pp. 5–6). Diffractive reading usually happens after the event, while experimenting with a diffractive apparatus happens within the event as an "onto-epistemological material practice" that helps "configure the world in particular ways" (p. 6). Hill names three interrelated ways of becoming diffractive: "becoming-with the world," "displacing and diffracting the selves who teach," and "embracing difference and interference" (pp. 6–11). She does not leave those terms abstract. She illustrates becoming-with through a beach assignment in which teachers collecting materials for a weaving project encounter a wounded bird. The event is an assemblage composed of "the assignment, the wounded bird, the hot sunny day, the cell phone, and the instructor's lack of intervention," which together produced "enacting care in the face of hopelessness." She illustrates displacing and diffracting the selves who teach through the "interview to the double" method: a teacher's account of meeting with a struggling student materializes several teacher-identities at once — "the compassionate teacher," "the professional teacher," "teacher as gatekeeper," "the litigious teacher," and "teacher as empath." She illustrates embracing difference and interference through a family quarrel read through two different theories — self-regulation theory and new materiality theory — to show that diffractive practice produces "new phenomena and new subjectivities" through interference rather than fit.

Lambert, working in initial teacher education at Birmingham City University, defines diffraction as an "otherwise" practice. She writes against forms of reflection that have become "a technical task orientated towards summative assessments of professional competency" and "a predominantly cognitive and linear practice" (Lambert, n.d., p. 2). Against that model, she proposes "diffraction as an 'otherwise' means" for exploring teaching as "non-linear and materially and affectively entangled through time and space" (p. 1), and states that diffraction is "not an 'either/or' alternative but an 'otherwise', expanded way of thinking" (p. 3). The named concepts through which she develops that practice are affect, intra-action, assemblage, becoming-with, and spacetimemattering. Her fullest example is the participant "M," whose collage centers on her attempt to run a Black Lives Matter club and her later move to running a choir. Lambert shows assemblage by naming the heterogeneous elements in the scene: mentor, policy, curriculum silences, local racial history, music, administration, children, affects, and M's own body. She shows spacetimemattering when historical and contemporary racial discourses "come to matter" in the present encounter, including the local history of Enoch Powell's "rivers of blood" speech and the wider resonance of Black Lives Matter. The collage itself materializes affect and overload through images Lambert describes as a "disintegrating body," "eyeballs rolling across the craft paper," and an "octopus whose legs perform multiple functions of administration and teaching tasks."

Dudley, writing from early years practice, presents diffractive practice through Mosaic listening. She describes the Mosaic Approach as "multi-contextual listening" and names "photo-elicitation, digital cameras, and observations of play" as the techniques through which children are given "diverse avenues to express themselves naturally" (Dudley, n.d., p. 2). Dudley names this approach "Mosaic listening (Clark & Moss, 2001)" and states that it "centres children's voices, ensuring they are heard and respected" (p. 2). She grounds the study in "ethico-onto-epistemology" — the intertwining of ethics, knowing, and being in every intra-action — and turns immediately to "nomadic ethics," which she defines as "an ongoing responsibility, requiring flexibility and responsiveness in practice" attentive to "a constant state of becoming" (p. 3). She specifies what this looks like in practice through examples from the study: a child using painted sticks while talking about "complex friendships and family," children "making a home fit for a King," and a "chatterbox session" where children bring a favorite toy. Her summary formulation is that "diffractive practice — a way of seeing through multiple lenses — is a powerful tool" for practitioners working with children's gender expression and identity (p. 4).

Taylor, writing from an undergraduate BA Education Studies module in a UK university, places diffraction in curriculum design. Her chapter uses Barad's notion of diffraction to show how "curriculum-making can be done via a patterned activity of creative interference" and grounds that claim in the module Educational Spaces: Theories and Perspectives (Taylor, 2019, pp. 37–38). She illustrates diffraction first through the design of the module itself. The course runs for twelve weeks, but the order of the themed teaching weeks is not fixed in advance. Students are given the themes and readings, and participate in sequencing the module. They "participate in materializing the (their) curriculum through an act of collaborative co-creation" and must "live with" the curriculum they have helped make, which means they experience their own "cuts" as productive of matter and meaning and as entailing "responsibility and accountability" to themselves and each other. Taylor illustrates innovative teaching and learning practices by having students inventory the classroom matter itself: "desks, carpet, lectern, rubbish, dust, lights, pens, iPads, whiteboard etc." They then ask questions such as "why is that there?" and "who decided that should go there?" In another task, the class sits in silence for two minutes and attends to "the lights hum, the feet pass outside, the traffic in the distance, and to our own breathing," along with "heat, atmosphere, aromas and light."

Sauzet, writing from Danish University College work with students in a professional bachelor's programme in social education, names her method "thinking through picturing." She wanted to move students from "learning about practices" to "producing situated knowledges in and through practices" (Sauzet, 2015, p. 42). She names "a diffraction apparatus" and describes the specific method she chose: "snaplogs," defined as "a visual, ethnographic method that involves taking pictures (snap) in response to specific questions, and writing small corresponding texts (logs)" (pp. 41–42). The snaplog assignment required students to conduct ethnographic fieldwork in practicum settings, take at least five pictures of something that went "across and beyond" their existing conception of professionalism, and answer for each image what is shown, why they took the picture, and how it relates to interprofessional practices. In Carla's snaplog, the image shows "M" working at a local DIY center. The interview expands the scene to include sweeping the parking lot, wearing yellow safety vests, moving shopping carts, using the coin mechanism, and M's pride in saying, "Hi I work at the DIY center!" Sauzet reads the photograph, student, activity center, DIY center, parking lot, vests, shopping carts, and feeling of usefulness as entangled in the production of interprofessionalism. In Johanne's snaplog, the image is a bathroom with a hoist at the youth club where she is interning. Johanne's concern is not only what the hoist is, but what professional knowledge is needed to use it safely over time and who takes responsibility for that expertise. Sauzet says this case does not only show what interprofessionalism is, but also what it "might be." The snaplog slows practice down and makes different cuts possible.

### § 2.5 Differences that make a difference

Diffraction is the mapping of effects of difference rather than the locating of difference in things (Barad, 2003). This distinction — between difference as a property of objects and difference as the pattern produced by their intra-action — is what separates diffractive practice from comparative analysis. Comparison asks: how do these things differ? Diffraction asks: what interference pattern is produced when these things are read through one another, and what does that pattern make visible that neither could produce alone? Bateson's formulation is the methodological anchor: information is "a difference that makes a difference" (1972, p. 459). Information is not content extracted from a source; it is the relational event of one difference registering as significant within another system. For research practice this means the unit of analysis is never a single text, dataset, or object in isolation. It is always the encounter: what happens when this theoretical framework is read through this practice, when this body of work is read through that ontological commitment, when this site is held against that history. The pattern that emerges from the encounter is where the knowledge lives.

### § 2.6 Refusal of exhaustive capture as method

Diffractive practice does not aim to produce a total account of its object. It aims to produce a situated, accountable account that remains open to further intra-action. The refusal of exhaustive capture is not an evasion of rigor. It is a specific methodological commitment to generativity as a condition of accountable knowledge in a world of ongoing becoming. Braidotti states this directly in her account of cartography: being grounded, any cartographic account is "necessarily sequential, partial and unfinished" (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii). Trinh's interval and speaking nearby give the principle its clearest practical form. The interval is the space the method leaves open so that meaning does not collapse into truth; speaking nearby is the practical orientation of coming close without seizing. Barad sharpens this further by showing that every method is boundary-making. Agential cuts are not neutral observations but material events that enact what becomes determinate and what is foreclosed. Because cuts matter, the researcher is responsible for them; because the cut is always one possibility among others, the method that enacts it cannot claim exhaustive capture without misrepresenting what it has done.

Seen this way, refusal of exhaustive capture is a practical orientation for doing research. It asks the practitioner to make the apparatus explicit, specify the cuts, register what the encounter produced and what it foreclosed, and leave the account open to the next intra-action. The commitment is simultaneously epistemological and ethical: to claim exhaustive capture is to deny the participation that made the account possible in the first place.

## § 3. Transpositions

Transposition is the named method that does what diffraction cannot do alone. Diffraction reads interference patterns produced by specific cuts; transposition names the creative leap across registers, materials, or fields that produces new material to diffract. The two terms name related but distinct operations. The literature gathers a family of accounts under the same term, with important variations among them.

### § 3.1 Braidotti

Braidotti does not name her method "diffractive practice," but her transpositions enact the same ontological commitments and extend them into new territory. Transposition is "a scientific term used in biology and musicology" that she adopts as "a metaphor for this specific way of working" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). It "indicates an intertextual, cross-boundary or transversal transfer, in the sense of a leap from one code, field or axis into another, not merely in the quantitative mode of plural multiplications, but rather in the qualitative sense of complex multiplicities" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). The move from genetic code to music is not analogical; it is the method itself: a "creative leap" that produces new relations by carrying content across registers that were not previously connected. Transposition "is not just a matter of mere translation, but a creative act" that produces "something new" through the passage (Braidotti, 2006, p. 6). Braidotti's cartography extends this: the researcher is a cartographer whose accounts are necessarily sequential, partial, and unfinished because they are grounded in specific locations and histories (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii).

### § 3.2 Schwab

Michael Schwab introduces transposition in artistic research as operating "outside registers of representation, resemblance, or reference" (Schwab, 2018, p. 11). A transposition does not reproduce its source; it performs a displacement through which new knowledge becomes possible. Schwab develops this through what he calls the multistable-image logic: making strange becomes an epistemic operator, not a rhetorical figure. Representation can be treated as a breakdown that collapses multiple potentials; artistic research operates in the intertwinedness of knowledge and non-knowledge, emphasizing the "active state," operations, rather than result. A transpositional shift produces an encounter that cannot be reduced back into stable representation: no shared coordinates, no common basis.

### § 3.3 Pirrò

David Pirrò writes, "I regard this text as a diffraction. The object being diffracted is the concept of transposition and the obstacle, the slit that it encounters, is the need to formulate this very text" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 136). He describes transpositions as "relational positions: they are objects whose primary characteristic is to indicate distances to their origin or towards other transpositions. That is, they construct a space from a network of differences" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 140). He names complexification as the movement opposed to analysis: "a process that tries to keep the phenomenon intact without breaking it apart and therefore neutralising the interactions between its parts" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 141), and states directly, "A transposition is not an analysis. A transposition is a complexification" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 141). He writes that transpositions are "differential," "resist integration," and are "incomparable" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 144). There is "no defined common basis" linking one transposition to another or to its original material, and "relations and differences towards one another and with the object they are applied to remain incompressible" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 144). On data, he writes that "data is not simply 'given'" but "might as well have a double nature of 'facta,' created, produced, and emerging from the interactions between all the technological and theoretical tools that scientists employ" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 145). "Transposing a data set means recognising and acknowledging its complexity. It means recovering its context and the net of interactions in it" (Pirrò, 2018, p. 145).

### § 3.4 Other theorists of transposition

Other contributors to Schwab's edited volume extend transposition in related directions. Esa Kirkkopelto develops transposition as a "methodological hypothesis" for artistic research (Schwab, 2018, p. 12), specifying the conditions under which artistic procedures can be transposed into other fields without losing their artistic force. Annette Arlander asks whether transposition is "a useful term to describe artistic practices that involve repetition with a difference," and works through the question by examining her own durational performance practice. Lucia D'Errico uses transposition to describe "the process of divergence" from the starting point of a Western notated musical score, where each performance produces something that is not a reproduction of the score but a transposition from it. The variations among these accounts matter because each specifies a different register in which transposition operates: Kirkkopelto on procedures, Arlander on repetition-with-difference in performance, D'Errico on musical divergence.

### § 3.5 Martin, Sayers, and Carroll: transpositions as six practices

Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025), working in organizational studies, operationalize Braidotti's transpositions as six practices in the context of organizational studies and instructional learning, drawing on feminist posthumanist methodology for research into workplace culture, leadership formations, and organizational change. Grounded in Braidotti's relational ontology of becoming, her critique of imaginary formations, her nomadic subject position, and her affirmative ethical orientation, they provide a full systematic derivation of transpositions as research protocol.

The six practices are: embracing alternative ways of seeing and understanding the world, assessing and critiquing imaginary formations, preparing to make the creative leap, accounting for locations and positioning, storying the in-between space, and developing new frames of resonance.

*Embracing alternative ways of seeing and understanding the world* is a reorientation before inquiry begins. An ontology of becoming implies that it is the nature of all life to become and to keep doing so through the embedded and embodied relations that produce affects. Affect is "the change, or variation, that occurs when bodies collide, or come into contact": an "experiential force" that propels processes of becoming and that is not limited to human bodies (Colman, 2010, pp. 11–12, cited in Martin et al., 2025). The challenge is that humanist dualisms and a unitary vision of the autonomous subject continue to "act with force in the context of everyday organizational relations" even when they are not consciously endorsed.

*Assessing and critiquing imaginary formations* identifies "the choreography of constraints and entitlements" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 86) that imaginary forms — constellations of images, ideas, values, representations, and narratives — materialize for differently historied bodies. The assessment is not of the imaginary form directly but of its effects: what ways of relating it endorses or precludes, what identities it confers or regulates, how it increases or restricts the relational capacities of subjects within their specific locations. Martin et al.'s exemplar is "heroic leadership" as an imaginary formation sustained by masculinist assumptions even in contexts where men are physically absent.

*Preparing to make the creative leap* cultivates "generative cross-pollinations" across multiple and often seemingly unconnected theoretical domains, genres, modes of apprehension, and discursive communities (Braidotti, 2017, p. 83). The researcher embodies the identity of the nomad: a traveler who suspends attachment to established discourses and follows "a map of her own making" (Braidotti, 2011, p. 165). This is the "consumption of the old in order to engender the new" (Braidotti, 2011, p. 68).

*Accounting for locations and positioning* names the multi-faceted embeddedness the researcher brings to any inquiry. Braidotti's phrase "history tattooed on your body" (2003, p. 54) names the different forms and experiences of oppression, empowerment, and difference that constitute any specific location. A feminist politics of location insists on the multiplicity of intersecting differences within and between subjects.

*Storying the in-between space* is the work of experimentation and creation. The researcher brings her "hybrid mixture of codes, genres, or modes of apprehension" (Braidotti, 2011, p. 225) to story different, more empowering rhythms for existing cultural formations. Martin et al. use feminist speculative fiction (Le Guin, Griffith) as the site where transpositions practices are embedded in narrative form, because speculative fiction "defamiliarizes our normative conceptions of existence, nature and the human" (Grebowicz & Merrick, 2013, p. 113).

*Developing new frames of resonance* proposes more adequate frameworks for imaginary formations. New words, terms, and frames "unsettle the 'old' or accepted meanings and practices" associated with imaginary forms and "signal the possibilities for other ways of thinking, acting and responding" (Martin et al., 2025, p. 513, citing Truman, 2019). Transposition practices produce "generative cracks" in hegemonic systems and in dominant social imaginaries, bringing forth affirmative alternatives for thought and practice.

### § 3.6 Militsi: diffractive watching as seven propositions

Anna Militsi proposes "the methodology of diffractive watching and watching diffractively as another tool for film analysis" (Militsi, 2021, p. 2), extending van der Tuin's diffractive reading into the act of viewing. She distinguishes between the two terms: diffractive watching as "a set intention and methodology" and watching diffractively as "an active process," and is interested in "the different implications that will result if diffractive watching is applied as a lens and/or employed as a tool" (Militsi, 2021, p. 2).

Militsi names cartography as structurally integral to the method: "I consider the mapping of the films' cartographical account to be a constituent part of diffractive watching which in this case functioned as the starting point for the analysis" (Militsi, 2021, p. 2). "One could go as far as suggesting that the cartographical account is what constitutes the backbone of diffractive watching" (p. 21).

She derives seven propositions. Diffractive watching aims at allowing for a smooth space for thought and "rejects linearity and proposes an analytical entanglement of spacetimemattering" (p. 22). "The subject, concept, object representation is not fixed but contingent upon volatile junctures" (p. 22). "Diffractive watching is an affirmative act in accordance with posthumanist thought as affirmative" (p. 22). It "does not repose on identity; it rides difference" and "is characterized by sudden rememberings" (pp. 22–23). It "does not aim at a reflection of the world, but at the immersion in a dynamic ever modifying state of things" (p. 23). It "should lead to a reconstitution of intensity and vitality that has existed in other mediums at other times" (p. 23). And it should be considered, "apart from a methodological apparatus, as a challenge of searching for dynamic intensities to be reinfused in other spaces, times, matters" (p. 23).

Militsi specifies the apparatus directly: "The diffractive apparatus, the tool through which the films will then be diffractively watched are the time, space and medium, the when, the where and the how the films will be watched" (p. 26). She names the viewer's position as intra-active and respons-able: "utter immersion; an intra-active response-able methodology for analyzing cinematographic assemblages through the conceptual tools of materiality, emotionality and performativity" (p. 23). She illustrates the method through a diffractive watching of Blade Runner (1982) and Blade Runner 2049 (2017), where the outcome was a poem composed only of words from the films' cartographical Wordclouds.

## § 4. Making Strange

Making strange has a specific theoretical genealogy that runs through literary theory, theater, and into new materialist art practice. Viktor Shklovsky (1917) introduced ostranenie — defamiliarization — as the defining operation of art: art exists to restore the experience of life, to make us feel things rather than merely recognize them. The technique of art is to make objects unfamiliar, to make forms difficult, to increase the length and difficulty of perception. Bertolt Brecht translated this into theatrical practice as the Verfremdungseffekt, the estrangement effect, a set of techniques for preventing the audience from losing itself in the illusion of the performance and forcing critical distance rather than identification. For Brecht, estrangement is not a failure of art but its most powerful political tool: to make the familiar strange is to reveal that it could be otherwise, that what appears natural and necessary is in fact historical and contingent.

New materialist art practice inherits and extends this tradition but shifts its target. Where Shklovsky and Brecht use making strange primarily to defamiliarize human social relations and cultural forms, new materialist making strange extends the operation to the nonhuman. The goal is not critical distance from a cultural illusion but perceptual access to matter's own agency. Making strange clears the trained perceptual habits — Bennett's "already-always-perceiving of objects" — that suppress the vitality, the thing-power, the ongoing becoming that was always there. Schwab's multistable-image logic extends this further. Making strange becomes an epistemic operator. Representation can be treated as a breakdown that collapses multiple potentials; artistic research operates in the intertwinedness of knowledge and non-knowledge, emphasizing the "active state," operations, rather than result.

Kristina Andersen writes that "Making strange or defamiliarisation is the artistic technique of forcing the audience to see common things in an unfamiliar or strange way in order to enhance perception of the familiar (Shklovsky 1917)," and identifies the technique as "a central concept to both Surrealism and Dada, centered on the idea that the act of experiencing something occurs in the moment of perception and that the further you confuse or otherwise prolong that moment of arriving at an understanding of an object of art, the deeper or more detailed that understanding will be" (Andersen, 2012, p. 3). In the 2016 chapter, she restates the principle as the operational core of her own practice: "Hypothetical making facilitates the creation of different kinds of objects and interpretations. The actual process of investigating these magic machines leans directly on the technique of making strange (or othering)" (Andersen, 2016, p. 46). She adds, "In a world that already feels overwhelmingly unfamiliar, making things stranger somehow allows me to inhabit that strangeness, as if it was mine" (Andersen, 2016, p. 47).

## § 5. Named Practitioner Accounts of Method

This section gathers theorists and practitioners who articulate method in their own terms and are not filed under diffractive practice, transposition, or making strange — though resonances with those families are often present. Each account is reported in the vocabulary the author names as her own. Where an operation resonates with one of the named method families, that resonance is noted but not used to reassign the practice.

### § 5.1 Bennett: comportments and the four techniques

Jane Bennett's account of what a vital materialist methodology requires is the clearest statement in the literature of what relational practice demands as a personal and political orientation. For Bennett, the central obstacle is perceptual: the trained habit of seeing the world as consisting only of active human subjects and passive objects. Correcting this requires what she calls foundational comportments — an open-minded, open-ended willingness to suspend the preconceived; an anticipatory readiness to encounter thing-power before it has been named or categorized; "a perceptual style open to the appearance of thing-power" (Bennett, 2010, p. 5). She traces this orientation to Thoreau's instruction to practice "the discipline of looking always at what is to be seen" (p. 5).

Bennett calls for vital materialists to "try to linger in those moments during which they find themselves fascinated by objects, taking them as clues to the material vitality that they share with them" (p. 18). The lingering is the method. Bennett reads Adorno's Negative Dialectics as the source of four practical comportments that support this perceptual retraining. The first is to make conceptualization itself an object of thought: to become aware that the process of concept-formation automatically obscures the inadequacy of its own products. The second is to attend to the qualitative singularities of the object: to resist flattening the thing into what the concept needs it to be, and instead stay with the specific, irreducible ways in which it pushes back. The third is to exercise the utopian imagination: to imaginatively recreate what conceptualization has suppressed, to recover "the possibility of which reality has cheated the objects and which is nonetheless visible in each one" (Bennett, 2010, p. 16). The fourth is to admit a playful element into inquiry, to adhere to one's "clownish traits" — the courage to present reality in configurations others might read as nonsense. Together, Bennett summarizes, the four techniques constitute "the self-criticism of conceptualization, a sensory attentiveness to the qualitative singularities of the object, the exercise of an unrealistic imagination, and the courage of a clown" (Bennett, 2010, p. 16).

### § 5.2 Guattari: transversality

Félix Guattari widens this from a practice of individual perceptual retraining to a structural claim about the conditions of thought itself. Transversality, he argues, is the operation by which thought refuses to stay within the boundaries of any single discipline, institution, or paradigm. New materialism, van der Tuin and Dolphijn (2010) argue, cannot be added to an existing field as a method; it is transversal by design, and its passage across disciplines is not a secondary application but the foundational ground. Guattari's transversality names the structural condition that makes this passage possible. Where disciplinary thought holds boundaries in place, transversal thought puts them into "constructive and transformative tension" (Timeto, 2011, p. 161, describing Haraway's figurations in the same terms).

### § 5.3 Somerville: worlding and data that glow

Margaret Somerville names the practitioner-level experience that Bennett's comportments and Guattari's transversal thinking describe. In her account of research with children walking to Redbank Creek, she writes: "I feel as if I am changed through these videos, learning to be human differently in the company of the creek and children" (Somerville, 2016, p. 1165), and later, "I become other-to-myself, as if I migrate outwards into these scenes that form and reform around me" (p. 1167). She names the operative term: *worlding*. Worlding is bodily-material attunement that precedes language, the pre-conceptual registering of place and relation that comes before any finding can be articulated.

Data that glow names the methodological consequence of worlding. In the same research project, certain data stood out not because they confirmed what the researcher expected but because they commanded attention by resisting analysis. Somerville borrows the phrase from MacLure (via Deleuze): data that glow are the moments in fieldwork where something pulls at the attention and will not be neatly assimilated into the existing analytic frame. They are the signal that worlding is happening — that the body is registering something the concept has not yet caught up with. Somerville traces this phenomenon into the children's mark-making. When the students who had been walking to Redbank Creek were given paper and materials, their marks showed the creek's presence in their bodies: the way water moves, the way the bank drops, the way light falls on wet stones. The data that glowed, for Somerville, were the marks in which the place itself was materializing.

### § 5.4 Mäkikoskela: chiasm, material resistance, and changing perspective

Riikka Mäkikoskela states both her object of study and her methodological frame: "I study the experience of three-dimensional visual art practice through the practice itself" (Mäkikoskela, 2016, p. 71). Her method is artistic research in which the practice is itself the research instrument. The bodily and spatial dimensions of the practice are central: "When I work, I experience as a three-dimensional form" (p. 71).

Mäkikoskela conceptualizes her practice through chiasm and material resistance. "Material, space, and body are intertwined in three-dimensional visual art practice," she writes, and names this entanglement as chiasm — a reciprocal crossing in which the practitioner and the materials constitute one another as the work takes shape (p. 72). Material resistance is the operative term: the active push-back of materials against the practitioner's intentions, the way matter "sets the pace of the work" rather than following the maker's plan. Her diary entry is blunt: "My working with laces is not going as I wanted. The material resists" (p. 73). The active material and the resistance it induces set the pace of the work, and the practitioner who refuses to register that resistance produces work that is empty of the encounter.

Localization extends this methodological account. "Three-dimensional visual art practice can be located in the body, its movement, and its relationship to space" (p. 74). The title phrase of her study, "from far to close (and back)," names an important procedural movement: "I move between far to close and back far again" as a deliberate shift between distant conceptual engagement and close material encounter. Part Three returns to this procedure as the organizing movement of her candy-laces research case.

### § 5.5 Rheinberger: the experimental system and the epistemic thing

For Hans-Jörg Rheinberger, the object of inquiry in experimental systems is not fully known in advance (Rheinberger, 2010). What distinguishes an experimental system from a method applied to a pre-given object is that the system itself produces the object of inquiry through its own operation. Rheinberger names this produced object the epistemic thing: the thing that is being investigated but whose identity is itself emerging from the investigation. The experimental system functions here as method. The epistemic thing names the condition under which inquiry becomes possible in a field where the object cannot be specified before the apparatus has done its work. This is structurally parallel to Barad's account of the phenomenon as material-discursive arrangement: in both cases, what becomes determinate is produced by the apparatus rather than revealed by it.

### § 5.6 Moñivas Mayor: the Liquid Things laboratory

Esther Moñivas Mayor names her method through time, observation, and open results, relocating research into the slower rhythms of watching liquids behave. Her account is procedural and situated. She lists "the testing of different points of observation within the same work, different capture methods (from photographs and videotapes to computer-processed scans), but also the generous use of waiting times" as operative techniques (Moñivas Mayor, 2016, p. 121). The research "was enriched by a series of unexpected events." The liquid "escapes from its containers," "drips," "spills," "bubbles out," and at times "the plastic support structure falls clamorously under the weight of an ever increasing amount of material." She then states what matters: "the most interesting thing might be the way in which any of these accidents modifies the direction of the research." A spill produces "an extremely light, white cloth on the surface of the floor," and "this sort of fabric, the result of an involuntary spill, becomes a priority and opens a new line of research" (pp. 121–122).

Moñivas Mayor names the laboratory itself. "The activity of the Liquid Things laboratory offers nothing that could be considered stable." The "general research methodology reveals a network of complementary actions, only partially fixed in terms of their development over time." She writes that "the absence of white lab coats and precision scales shows a desire for distance from smartness, asepsis and exactness in order to let in surprise, play and reflection" (p. 122). Her closing language is strong: "The more we look into these materials and processes, the more we lose the ability to say something concrete and definite about them." "The ontology of the substances dissolves." "The epistemology of processes becomes increasingly complex." She writes that "reductionism derived from control and from hierarchical exercise of power has been substituted by a discipline of emergent processes" (pp. 122–123).

### § 5.7 Franinović: seeding material events

Karmen Franinović describes Material Aktiv Denken as a "thinking-making event on active materials" with a "twofold" goal (Franinović, 2017). The workshop begins with the problem of preparation without overdetermination: "How can we prepare an encounter with active materials without predetermining what will emerge from it?" Her answer is seeding. "In order to interweave texts and materials, we had to prepare seeds for material events to take place." The seed is not a plan. It is a minimal preparation — a text, a material, a constraint, a space — that creates the conditions for an event without specifying its outcome. The seeding operation resonates with Barad's apparatus and with Braidotti's transposition without being reducible to either. Apparatus names the material-discursive arrangement through which a phenomenon is produced; transposition names the creative leap that carries content across registers. Seeding names the preparatory gesture that makes both possible without claiming in advance what the event will yield.

Franinović specifies how shared knowledge is produced. Participatory workshops "can be understood as a form of research in which participants collaboratively produce situated knowledge through their engagement with materials." The research process remains open to error and redirection: "We embrace the disobedience of materials as part of the research process." The question that drives her practice-research is: "Can material encounters engender new ways of thinking?" Her workshop practice shows what happens when that question is taken as a practical design constraint. Part Three returns to her workshops as full research cases.

### § 5.8 Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz: collective practice as method

Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz report their collective practice at Mz* Baltazar's Laboratory, a feminist hackerspace in Vienna, as "a research-based artistic methodological framework" and "a method of imagining post-human conviviality through mutual care, co-dependence, and solidarity" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 57). They define interference as "a critical disruptive process that destabilises the status quo and plays an important role in relational and emancipatory thinking and action" (p. 55), naming their method as *techno-ecofeminist and trans-feminist hacking*. The framework is explicitly collective: the unit of research is not the individual artist-researcher but the hackerspace as apparatus, the multitude as agent, the trans-feminist collective as the entity that does the knowing. They ask, "which methods can be generative to the process of reinventing liveable futures for all?" and name a range of interventions: "modelling the future, collectively reimagining technology, exploring Do-It-With-Others communities, sensing interdependency, experiencing embeddedness, rethinking artistic productivity, enacting vegetal agency, exploring creative possibilities of decay, learning from collective-based participatory art, cultivating creative-critical-code writing, and exploring the relationality of transcendence and immanence" (p. 56). Part Three treats the track's research outputs as collective research cases.

## § 6. Closing of Part Two

Part Two has named three method families — diffractive practice, transpositions, and making strange — as they are named by their theorists and practitioners, and reported a group of named practitioner accounts of method in the terms their authors themselves use. What the families share is the refusal of representational distance, accountability to the apparatus, and the treatment of generativity rather than exhaustive capture as the condition of accountable knowledge. What distinguishes them is the specific operation each names: diffraction reads interference patterns, transposition makes creative leaps across registers, making strange suspends default perception long enough for the nonhuman to become perceptible.

The named practitioner accounts — Bennett's comportments, Guattari's transversality, Somerville's worlding and data that glow, Mäkikoskela's chiasm and material resistance, Rheinberger's experimental system, Moñivas Mayor's Liquid Things, Franinović's seeding, Torosyan/Reis/Wuschitz's hacking as method — are not filed under the three families because their authors do not file them there. They are reported in their own terms. The resonances between these accounts and the named families are real but are not used as grounds for reclassification.

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn warn that bringing new materialism into a discipline as if both are pre-existing tends to undo transversality, producing "inter- rather than intra-acting" relations and reinstalling the very schism the framework set out to refuse. If the methods described here are to be genuinely relational rather than merely interdisciplinary, their passage across fields has to be the ground rather than something applied after.

Part Three turns to art practice as research: how the methods reported here operate when they are enacted as research practice by artists who claim no stable separation between their artworks and their knowledge production.


# Part Three: New Materialist Art Practice as Research

## § 1. What This Question Asks

Part Three asks a narrower question than the parts that came before it: how is new materialist art practice research? The answer does not lie in adding theory to art practice after the fact, or in treating artworks as illustrations of claims produced elsewhere. It lies in practices that enact the ontological and methodological commitments established in Parts One and Two — material participation, distributed agency, situated apparatuses, non-representational engagement, and inquiry conducted in and through entangled practice rather than detached observation of pre-existing objects.

### § 1.1 What research is, and what art practice as research is

Taken together, several accounts define research as original inquiry directed toward knowledge and understanding, and as a practice that may take different forms across different fields. Henk Borgdorff defines research as "original investigation undertaken in order to gain knowledge and understanding" (Borgdorff, 2012, p. 87), noting the Dublin Descriptors understand research "in an inclusive way," not in a "limited or restricted sense" tied only to a narrow scientific model (p. 87). Graeme Sullivan writes that "an agreed goal of research is the creation of new knowledge" and that this goal "can be achieved by following different, yet complementary pathways" (Sullivan, 2006, pp. 19–20). Christopher Frayling distinguishes research with a small r, "the act of searching closely or carefully," from research with a big R, "work directed towards the innovation, introduction, and improvement of products and processes" (Frayling, 1993, p. 1). He also writes: "Research is a practice, writing is a practice, doing science is a practice, doing design is a practice, making art is a practice" (Frayling, 1993, p. 4).

Within this broader definition, art practice as research names a specific mode of inquiry in which practice is central rather than supplementary. Sullivan writes that "art practice is a creative and critical form of human engagement that can be conceptualized as research" and that "human understanding arises from a process of inquiry that involves creative action and critical reflection" when "art practice is theorized as research" (Sullivan, 2006, pp. 19, 28). Borgdorff writes that, in artistic research, "artistic practice is central to the research itself" and that "the research takes place in and through the artist's creative and performative actions" (Borgdorff, 2012, p. 121). Art practice qualifies as research, he adds, "if its purpose is to expand our knowledge and understanding by conducting an original investigation in and through art objects and creative processes" (Borgdorff, 2012, p. 160). Frayling distinguishes "Research into art and design," "Research through art and design," and "Research for art and design," and describes "Research for" as work "where the end product is an artefact" and where "the thinking is, so to speak, embodied in the artefact" (Frayling, 1993, p. 5). De Assis and D'Errico describe artistic research as "a particular mode of artistic practice and of knowledge production, in which scholarly research and artistic activity become inextricably intertwined," "practice-based, practice-led, and practice-driven," and "primarily conducted by practitioners" (de Assis & D'Errico, 2019, pp. 2–3). Christensen-Scheel, Aure, and Bergaust write that "the field of artistic research is constituted not by one art-based or artistic research mode, but is nourished by several theoretical, scientific, and artistic approaches" and that current work moves toward "methodological crossovers" and "multimethod development that is not based on polarised dichotomies" (Christensen-Scheel et al., 2022, p. 2).

Esa Kirkkopelto provides the most precise account of what distinguishes artistic research from art practice. The distinguishing feature is the conversion of the artistic medium into a medium of research — not institutional location, and not the presence of a written reflection. The conversion signals a transmogrification of the artist into "the primary expert of the medium that she herself has created" (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49), responsible not only for the work but for making its consequences traceable and communicable. Refusing exhaustive capture, as an artist's medium changes into a medium of research, the conversion moves toward the unknown while remaining unable to fully accomplish the transition: "it remains suspended between two stages; it can only suggest the transition by repeating it endlessly but always anew" (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49).

### § 1.2 What new materialist art practice as research is

New materialist art practice as research retains Borgdorff's previous claims about art practice as research but places them under conditions in which the practice is conducted through specific material-discursive apparatuses in which matter is active, agency is distributed, and the researcher is part of the apparatus that produces the phenomenon under study. Apparatus and transposition are related but not identical. Apparatus names the material arrangement through which a phenomenon is produced. Transposition names the creative leap across registers, materials, or fields that produces new material to diffract. Both function as operations through which art practice becomes research in the sense Kirkkopelto specifies.

The artist-researcher is not external to the process. She is a horizontal actant within the apparatus, sharing distributed agency with the materials, instruments, bodies, and institutions that together constitute the research phenomenon. Her unique transposition framework, mediated through her practice, produces the research artifact as evidence for other perceiving entities.

Across the sections that follow, new materialist art practice functions as research through recurring commitments: refusal of representational distance, accountability to apparatus, inquiry that produces its object rather than describing a pre-given one, and generativity rather than exhaustive capture as the condition of accountable knowledge.

## § 2. Trinh: Knowledge Through Practice

Kirkkopelto's criterion — that artistic research occurs when the artistic medium is converted into a medium of research — names a condition that some artists were already meeting before the institutional field of artistic research existed to name it. Trinh T. Minh-ha is one of those artists. Her filmmaking was research in Kirkkopelto's sense before the field had the vocabulary for saying so.

In "Documentary Is/Not a Name," Trinh names the documentary fantasy she is refusing: the claim that the camera gives direct access to an object that was already there. Documentary's long-standing aesthetic of objectivity rests on "the assumption of reality as the ultimate referent, thus promoting the documentary as the most direct means of accurate accounting for actual facts of everyday life, in the form of truth" (Trinh, 1990, p. 76). Against this, she refuses closure: "meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown," and "the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" is what allows something else to happen (Trinh, 1990, p. 77).

That method is given its clearest name in "speaking nearby." Trinh describes this as "a speaking that does not objectify, does not point to an object as if it is distant from the speaking subject or absent from the speaking place," and as "a speaking that reflects on itself and can come very close to a subject without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, pp. 86–87). She states directly that the challenge is to materialize this "in all aspects of the film: verbally, musically, visually" (p. 87). Speaking nearby is not a figure of speech. It is the operative technique of her filmmaking.

Trinh also states what such a practice refuses. "A work that reflects back on itself offers itself infinitely as nothing else but work… and void… which is to say, existence" (Trinh, 1990, p. 90). The film is not a container for information about a subject matter. It is an existence that holds itself open, that does not seize, and that produces knowledge through the holding-open rather than through the completion of the frame.

Refusal of exhaustive capture and generativity over closure are therefore not later theoretical descriptions imposed on Trinh's work. They are commitments named by her directly, in her writing about her own practice. Her filmmaking produces knowledge through operations whose principles she articulates in parallel.

Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch generalize this point at the level of artistic research as a field. Artistic research cannot proceed through the objectifying, representational protocols of the dominant research paradigm without betraying its own commitments. The art object is not a representation of a prior knowledge. It is the site where the knowledge is produced, and any method that treats it as secondary to a textual report misses the research itself (Borgdorff, Peters, & Pinch, 2020).

Placed at the opening of Part Three, Trinh does specific work. She is not included here as a theorist commenting on art practice as research from outside. She is included as a practitioner whose films are already doing the work Kirkkopelto's definition names — and whose writing about her practice articulates, in her own vocabulary, what that work requires.

## § 3. Bolt: Performativity and Materialising Practice

Barbara Bolt identifies a limit in Western visual theory. The suggestion that "a material practice might have actual material effects… rarely gets a mention" in Western scholarship after Plato (Bolt, 2003, p. 269). Representation is treated as second-order data, a substitute that can never be its object, a sign circulating within social meaning but unable to remain in dynamic relation to the materiality of its referent.

Bolt locates the limit even in Butler's account of performativity. Butler can explain how bodies materialize through repetition and discourse, but she cannot account for "the materialisation that occurs in the interplay between the matter of bodies, cultural knowledges or discourse, and the materials of production" (Bolt, 2003, p. 271). In Bolt's account, matter is pulled back into signification.

Bolt turns to Butler through materialisation. In Bodies That Matter, Butler argues that materialisation emerges through iterated performative citation of norms. Bolt extends Butler's account of materialisation but locates the "interplay" not only in discourse but in the interaction between matter, bodies, and the materials of production. Drawing on Paul Carter's phrase "concurrent actual production," Bolt develops her notion of methexis. Bolt draws from Indigenous Australian painting, which is productive rather than representative, where "meaning and reality are constituted in the performance" (Bolt, 2003, p. 273). The process "has effects" rather than only meanings (Bolt, 2003, p. 274).

Bolt names this condition of practice "working hot." She writes: "the process of painting is a response to what happens in the interaction between paint, oil, turps, canvas, gravity, sun, heat, the occasional live beast and my body" (Bolt, 2003, p. 276). These interactions "have actual causal relations, not merely significatory relations" (p. 276). The painter does not impose a plan on inert material; she responds to what the material is doing, and her response is not external to what the painting becomes.

The finished work is not detached from this process. Bolt turns to Peirce's index because the index retains a "direct causal relation" between thing and sign. It allows us to "witness in a very different way the process of materialisation that occurs in the interaction between the matter of bodies, the matter of materials, technologies and the cultural" (Bolt, 2003, p. 277). The painting as index is not a representation that mirrors a prior scene. It is a mark that remains causally connected to the conditions of its making.

In Art Beyond Representation, Bolt states that the argument "emerged in the heat of practice" (Bolt, 2004, p. 1). She describes the point at which "the painting no longer merely represents or illustrates… Instead, it performs" (Bolt, 2004, p. 1). She states that "art is a performative, rather than merely a representational practice" and that "through creative practice, a dynamic material exchange can occur between objects, bodies and images" (Bolt, 2004, pp. 9–10). Imaging "can produce real material effects in the world" (Bolt, 2004, p. 10).

Bolt's account of working hot is not only a claim about painting. It names a broader condition of practice-based research. Butler provides the starting point: bodies materialize through repeated, citational performances, and performativity is the discursive mode through which ontological effects are installed. Barad extends this into material-discursive practice. What she disputes is not iteration itself but the location of agency within the process. In the Baradian account, matter does not sit downstream of discourse as its passive result. Matter and discourse co-produce one another through intra-action. Bolt's working hot names the practitioner-level experience of this co-production in painting: bodies, materials, and making co-produce one another, and what emerges carries real causal effects rather than merely significatory ones.

Cull Ó Maoilearca gives the institutional form of this distinction in performance research. Researchers in artistic research in performance must negotiate "the broadly representationalist versus performative paradigms," where the former treats knowledge as representation by a transcendent subject and the latter treats knowledge-production as an immanent material part of the reality it studies (Cull Ó Maoilearca, 2019, p. 154). Under that distinction, the usual notion of a "contribution to knowledge," understood as addition to a stable stock, no longer holds in the same way. The issue is not style. It is the difference between a representational account of knowledge and a performative one.

Bolt then moves from practice to research. In "A Performative Paradigm for the Creative Arts?" she writes that the creative arts need "to define the terms of a performative model in relation to the existing theories of performativity" and "differentiate it from the established research orthodoxies by refining its protocols and procedures" (Bolt, 2009, p. 1). She asks: "What is performativity and what would be the characteristics of a performative research paradigm?" (Bolt, 2009, p. 2). In "Artistic Research: A Performative Paradigm?" she returns to the same question and answers it through "the performative force of the research," its capacity to effect "movement" in thought, word, and deed in the individual and social sensorium (Bolt, 2016, pp. 129–130). These movements enable "a reconfiguration of conventions from within rather than outside of convention" (Bolt, 2016, p. 130). While scientific validity is tied to repetition of the same, "the performative paradigm operates according to repetition with difference" (Bolt, 2016, p. 132).

Bolt also carries the art-quantum thread from Part One forward into practice-based research. Her return "to the energetic potential of matter" runs through Vicki Kirby's reading of Bohr, and Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) note that this made Bolt one of the first art scholars to address quantum theory in relation to visual practice. The art-historical lineage within which Bolt's intervention sits — Duchamp's ready-mades read as problems of observation (Schwab, 2018), Cage's chance operations as the withdrawal of compositional mastery (Wilson, 2018), Rauschenberg's combines as practice "especially sensitive to agentivity" (Słodkowski, 2023) — locates the performative paradigm within a longer counter-tradition that has been thinking through the same problems from the side of art that Barad later formalizes philosophically. Bolt's contribution names this lineage from inside painting practice: working hot is what the encounter between matter and maker actually feels like when the representational frame has been set down.

## § 4. Mäkikoskela: The Candy Laces as Research

Riikka Mäkikoskela states her research problem directly: "I study the experience of three-dimensional visual art practice through the methodology of artistic research" (Mäkikoskela, 2017, p. 1). From that perspective, "art practice involves the body, materiality and continuous movement," and the "chiasmatic relationship between the artist and the work of art reveals the resistance of material making, which opens the possibility for the artist to change working perspectives" (Mäkikoskela, 2017, p. 1). Her method and her research question are inseparable. She is not studying three-dimensional practice from outside. She is studying it through the practice itself.

Her method is described in equally direct terms. "By employing artistic research methods, I produce the research material and analyze it in the same process of art-making. I report my research by exhibiting the research material and writing about the research process" (Mäkikoskela, 2017, p. 3). Artistic research, she adds, "includes both the practical and theoretical review," and by making art and studying her activity, she seeks "to deepen and expand" her experience (p. 3). The research material is not gathered before the making begins. It is produced in the same process.

The bodily and material terms of that process are specified early. "When I work, I experience as a three-dimensional form in a three-dimensional space – the body" (Mäkikoskela, 2017, p. 2). She "confront[s]" her "working material and an evolving work of art as a sensory body," and her body's motion "gains its significance by perceiving sensory experience from within" her "environment" (p. 2). Three-dimensional visual art practice is defined not only by visuality but by material and spatial making. "The sensory perceptions blend when experiencing a three-dimensional work of art; the visual, material, and spatial combine. The artist observes in motion as she traverses around the work. At the same time, the front, the sides, and the back of the work change places" (pp. 7–8). "As a result, the artist's body and its movement have special characteristics in three-dimensional visual art practice" (p. 8).

The candy-lace narrative is where the methodological account becomes research evidence. "I want to play with the candy laces again, so I bring some of them to my studio. Once there, I have the chance to take my time with them and roll them in my hands to feel my experiences. While doing so again, I am confronted with many important memories of my life. That is what my hands tell me" (Mäkikoskela, 2017, p. 6). A few lines later: "I am able to reach back into my childhood memories only by holding and rolling the actual material, the candy laces, in my hands" (p. 6). The material is not only a support for memory. It is part of the process through which memory becomes available. Mäkikoskela's research finding here is not that candy laces remind her of childhood. It is that certain forms of knowing — including the knowing that gives her access to specific memories — are only available through direct material handling, and that this constitutes a specific claim about what three-dimensional art practice can produce as research.

That process is then named through chiasm and material resistance. "Material, space, and body are intertwined in three-dimensional visual art practice," she writes, naming the entanglement as chiasm (p. 8). Material resistance is the operative term. Her diary entry is blunt: "My working with laces is not going as I wanted. The material resists" (p. 73). The active material and the resistance it induces set the pace of the work. "The active material and the resistance it induces sets the tempo for the work." The refusal of the material to follow the plan is not an obstacle to the research; it is the research producing its finding.

Localization adds a further layer. "Three-dimensional visual art practice can be located in the body, its movement, and cooperation with active material" (p. 12). "The artist's body becomes a part of the materiality of the practice as she collaborates with her working material" (p. 13). When new materialism enters this account, it "generates an additional dimension to the movement and locating of three-dimensional visual art practice by defining an active material" (p. 14). "The movement of both artist and material and their temporary localizations form a series of events" (p. 14). In that series, "the uniqueness and singularity of happening is emphasized" (p. 14).

The title phrase "from far to close (and back)" names the movement that organizes both the practice and the research. "I move between far to close and back far again in both three-dimensional visual art practice and the analytical stage of artistic research" (p. 20). "My practice-led research method brings along a perspective of a participant, from which I cannot break away in the traditional sense" (p. 20). At the same time, "I also have the opportunity to review my activity from a distance perspective, in relation to cultural practice and research" (p. 20). She ends by stating, "When I work on the material form in space, my body is a resistor to the surrounding area. I can also feel the resistance which focuses on my body in chiasm. I employ this activity as part of my research method" (p. 20). "Material resistance is one of the main elements of my research, with which I justify the methodology of artistic research" (p. 20).

Mäkikoskela's methodological point is that artistic research makes it possible to examine art-making from the participant's point of view, tracking thought as it happens through material engagement rather than after the fact in abstraction. This is also what separates research from illustration. In illustration, the concept precedes and governs the material. In research, the material pushes back, revising what was thinkable before the encounter. Kirkkopelto names the same condition from another angle: the artistic researcher can "jump right in" by appropriating and inventing concepts and staging them as epistemic things, with real risk because failure remains possible when knowledge is genuinely at stake.

## § 5. Andersen: Magic Machines as Research

Kristina Andersen opens her account by saying, "Like most of us, I oscillate between reading and making. Often the objects I make can be seen as diagrams of ideas, of manifestations of the relationships between one notion and another" (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). She then places reading under the same material pressure: "I don't read texts properly – in truth I am not sure I know how to – instead the texts are like images, like scraps of colour and texture. I carry them with me, take them out, rearrange them, reconsider them. Most snippets of texts are with me for years, worn and known. Recited quietly like curses, changing meaning over time, coming into focus and disappearing back into incomprehension again" (p. 42). Reading is not extraction. It is another mode of handling.

She returns to that relation: "When asked to write this chapter, I was thrown back into consideration of how I interact with words and things. How I collect and abuse them, assign them power as curses and shields. I taught myself to write academically, I needed to know how to do that. Now, when asked to write subjectively, I find that I write like a child" (p. 42). The statement does not describe a lack of reflection. It names a problem of form. Academic writing does not carry the practice over intact. It changes the voice through which the practice had been working.

The machines themselves are introduced in the same vocabulary of partial knowledge and material encounter. "Over the years my work has emerged around the notion of making machines. They are machines for thinking, living, or expression, and sometimes for making sense with" (p. 42). Earlier they were functional, but "now they seem to be more and more abstract, often haphazard or even hypothetical. I make them as a way of arriving at an understanding, as if I can only process something if I re-make it myself first" (p. 42). The machine is not the endpoint of the thought. It is how the thought becomes available.

That relation is stated again through the recipe passage: "First comes the object, the notion, the timing, then the words" (p. 43). Andersen cites Valente's formulation directly: "First, you build the machine, then it tells you what it's for" (Valente, 2013, cited in Andersen, 2016, p. 43). She restates the point in her own terms: "For me it is through this making, that I begin to comprehend what I am building. Or rather, with Valente, over time they tell me what they are for. Building a machine is the only way for me to comprehend what it might do" (p. 43). Knowing in the doing is stated plainly.

The making process is neither linear nor fully controlled. "It is an ongoing process of discovery and failure" (p. 44). Along the way one finds "the accidental object, the technology that exists merely as a quirk, the successful misconstruction. These are all glimpses of the material speaking back to you. They are the gifts from working with your hands" (p. 44). She adds that "building a machine can be an act of exploration, of starting a journey that might have direction, but does not need to have a certain goal, or rather you may be wrong about what you thought was the goal" (p. 44). Through the process, "the machine is ultimately understood as a whole, a new entity with its own rules and possibilities" (p. 44).

When Andersen turns to hypothetical or "magic" machines, she describes them as "non-functional props" made "in short concentrated bursts and guided by desire and fear, rather than skill and technological possibility" (p. 45). She makes them "to give body to dim concerns and questions, to consider the potential reality of a world in which such a machine might exist, and in a sense to rehearse how to live in such a world" (p. 45). The non-functional machine is not failed technology. It is a prop that allows rehearsal, speculation, and embodied trial.

In Andersen's workshop structure, participants draw a sound in charcoal, build the instrument that could make it, and then gather to "play" the non-functional object and explain "which gestures create which sounds and how the object is handled and manipulated" (p. 45). The workshop facilitates "an embodied conversation about the desire for a new sound and the instrument that makes it" (p. 45). It "temporarily frees us from the practical and technical limitations of our work," and the objects are "created as props, animated and investigated through the live 'playing' at the end" (p. 45). In that performative moment, "the experience between the performer, the new instrument, and the audience can be perceived as an almost physical thing which can be examined, turned, changed, and at some point is 'done' as the performance moment lifts" (p. 45).

Andersen's account of "marking" sharpens the evidentiary role of the object. Borrowing from dance rehearsal, she writes that when non-functional machines or speculative instruments are built, "we are marking the physical space our future machines will take up. This also means that as much as sketching the physical object itself, we are sketching the gestures and movements required to operate and play this particular device" (p. 46). Later: "we have the object itself to guide us. The tactile and structural possibilities of the non-functional object lets us investigate it in a physical manner; it is suddenly possible to speak confidently as to how this device will operate, because it allows us to anchor any conceptual or metaphorical notions in an object that pushes back into our hands: It is already right there" (p. 46). The object does not merely illustrate the claim. It anchors what can be said.

The section on speculative technologies keeps making strange in clear view. "Hypothetical making facilitates the creation of different kinds of objects and interpretations. The actual process of investigating these magic machines leans directly on the technique of making strange (or othering)" (p. 46). A page later: "In a world that already feels overwhelmingly unfamiliar, making things stranger somehow allows me to inhabit that strangeness, as if it was mine" (p. 47). The work inside these "temporary trashy misunderstandings" lets her "listen and express" herself "outside of the forces of my own tiresome internal monologues and of course the devil of silence" (p. 47).

By making "crude and clumsy models of hypothetical machinery," Andersen tries "to glean an understanding of what they might do, to see if I can 'catch the world in its changing and change with it'" (p. 48). "We are not so much 'thinking with our hands' as we are changing ourselves; becoming the person who built that thing" (p. 48). "The thoughts that are acted out are created in a complex collaboration between the object, the situation and a performative moment" (p. 48). She names "the performative moment" as "the fog chamber in which the ideas and devices prove themselves or fail" (p. 48). The hypothetical objects "facilitate a kind of risk taking; they provide a temporary space in which I can be changed and change" (p. 48).

## § 6. Franinović: Seeding Material Events

Karmen Franinović describes Material Aktiv Denken as a "thinking-making event on active materials" with a "twofold" goal: "a thorough reflection about active materials and their unique behaviours and performances" and the advancement of "critical and artistic approaches that conceptualize materials and materiality in a more active and performative way" (Franinović, 2016, pp. 128–129). The workshop created "a collaborative space" in which participants could "playfully, yet systematically, shift between materials and concepts" and "experientially approach activity, process, transformation and flow" (pp. 128–129).

The workshop begins with the problem of preparation without overdetermination. Franinović asks, "How can we prepare an event without overly predetermining it?" and answers through "seed-spaces," which she defines as "a set of constraints or conditions" that are "permanently open to change and new iterations" (p. 129). In transferring seed-space techniques to material research, she says the aim was "to not overly predefine material processes but allow them to emerge, offering us surprising insights" (p. 130). The workshop proceeds through a "double-sided challenge": "activating materials themselves and activating people's thoughts and actions with and through materials" (p. 130).

"In order to interweave texts and materials, we had to prepare seeds for material events to take place" (p. 130). This involved "mapping different concepts, materials and techniques," planning experiments around selected materials, and choreographing "the timing of the material processes and the timing of the workshop activities" (p. 130). The workshop was "loosely structured by different themes," including agency, human/non-human actors and affordances, imagination and material transformation, and reflection on the workshop itself (p. 130).

Franinović specifies how shared knowledge is produced. Participatory workshops "can be understood as a kind of third space in which diverse parties produce shared knowledge" (p. 130). She notes that most techniques for creating such space are "verbal and narrative," but cites work suggesting that "a non-linguistic collaborative experience, especially through the use of physical artifacts, could help bridge the gap between people with different expertise" (pp. 130–131). The opening exercise of the workshop asked participants to introduce themselves through an object and a material. This turned into "an individual or collaborative performance," and "the materials entered the stage as active and activating participants from the start" (p. 131).

The research process remains open to error and redirection. "We embrace the disobedience of materials, and work with errors and qualities that are undesired in engineering contexts." She adds, "We let the unexpectedness of a material process guide our research process" (p. 128). Later: "the event may have opened more questions than it answered" (p. 139).

Franinović's workshop practice enacts the inseparability of practice and research. The question that drives her practice-research is: "Can materials be seeds for people... and people seeds for materials?" (p. 129). This is not a rhetorical question. It is a research hypothesis whose answer can only be produced by making: by mapping concepts, materials, and techniques onto each other, choreographing the timing of processes so that material and conceptual co-emergence becomes possible. The most telling moment in her account is the one where a participant insists on verbal explanation and is instead pulled into contact with the material process: "his questions vanished for a moment" (Raw Flows, p. 136). The material answered the question that language was going to ask.

Fluid affordance is the name she gives to this condition. With dynamic materials, affordances transform as the material's state changes, destabilizing the habitual instrumental response and opening the encounter. The knowing is in the doing because the material is a co-investigator.

Franinović's account makes visible what research looks like when its contribution is generative rather than conclusive. "The event may have opened more questions than it answered" (p. 139). The value of the work lies not in closing the inquiry but in producing conditions under which something newly thinkable, sayable, or perceptible can emerge. Research in this sense is not measured only by the stability of its final claim, but by the quality of the operations it sets in motion and the kinds of understanding those operations make possible.

## § 7. SymbioticA: The Laboratory as Research Apparatus

SymbioticA describes itself as "an artistic laboratory" located within a biological science department and organized around artists working with scientific techniques, equipment, and staff (Jorgensen & Savat, 2019). The laboratory is not background. It is one of the conditions through which the work takes place.

The work includes more than the final bioart object. It includes ethics applications, permissions, negotiations with laboratory managers, and ongoing care obligations. These are not presented as external constraints on the research. They are part of the research process itself. The question of what counts as the work is therefore enlarged from the start.

The object remains important, but it is not isolated from the procedures that sustain it. Where living systems are involved, care, protocol, and institutional permission remain active within the work. The living object functions as evidence because it materializes those relations. It does not stand alone as a finished form detached from its conditions of production.

The account also gives ethics a different status. Ethics is not added after the research as commentary or regulation. It is built into the laboratory's operation through care practices, permissions, and negotiated responsibilities. Ethics therefore functions as infrastructure. It shapes what can be done, what can be maintained, and what kind of research can occur at all.

SymbioticA is useful as a case because it keeps scientific procedure and artistic procedure in direct contact without collapsing one into the other. The laboratory functions as research. The living system functions as evidence. The work is not only the object produced, but the network of material, technical, institutional, and ethical relations through which the object becomes possible.

## § 8. Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz: Collective Research at Mz* Baltazar's Laboratory

Interferences of the Multitude is an introductory analysis of the perspectives of multiple artist-researchers collected under the umbrella of the track of the same name, presented during the Rogue Research edition of the 3rd Politics of the Machines Conference in Berlin in September 2021 (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 55). The paper examines "the implementation of arts-based research into the new modes of techno-ecofeminist imaginaries" and investigates its "generative potential for the enactment of the new materialist and feminist ethics of care, collaboration, and solidarity" (p. 55). The projects it gathers span "the entangled fields of human–computer interaction, computer-supported cooperative work, material sciences, critical design and making, architecture, machine learning, interactive art, and post-human performance" (p. 55).

Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz place interference and diffraction at the center of their account. They define interference as "a critical disruptive process that destabilises the status quo and plays an important role in relational and emancipatory thinking and action," and they write that diffraction "came to replace the reflexive methodologies of thinking and doing" (p. 55). With a posthumanist and new materialist grounding, they cite Barad's account of intra-action in order to reject the assumption that pre-existing entities simply meet one another. Intra-action, they write, "suggests that agency is not an inherent property of an individual body to be exercised but a mutual influence and co-emergence of forces" (Barad 2007, cited in Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 55).

The authors situate this work at Mz* Baltazar's Laboratory, a feminist hackerspace in Vienna where they practice trans-feminist hacking as an artistic methodology (p. 56). They describe their grounding in "the critical post-human rethinking of the Eurocentric notions of 'Human'" and connect that grounding to "the material, gendered, and embodied challenges of existence in the augmented world" (p. 56). The track is unified not by form but by how knowledge is being made.

Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz ground the track in a neo-Spinozist notion of multitude drawn from Negri and Hardt, distinguishing potentia from potestas and identifying collective distributed power-to-act as central to the practices gathered there (p. 56). Trans-feminist hacking is the practical form this takes. It is described as a research-based artistic framework that applies diffractive practice to "bridge this gap in thinking of and doing with difference" and to imagine "post-human conviviality through mutual care, co-dependence, and solidarity" (p. 57).

Knowledge is produced through "deep conversation between theory and practice," through oscillation, collaboration, intervention, technological making, and co-becoming (p. 60). Performance and interactivity are not illustrations of findings reached elsewhere. They are part of the research process itself. The authors write that "arts-based and practice-based research enabled scholars to ask new and bold questions about the agency of co-authored, human-generated assemblages, be it the ruins of a decaying web, traces of shared code, devices within our body, or rules in gaming" (p. 59). "The panellists debated the way that this process has transformed their own subjectivities and methods. Their artworks were presented as a significant outcome of this inquiry process" (p. 59).

In the conclusion, the authors write that the newly emerging techno-ecofeminist and trans-feminist practices of hacking "invite us to rethink techno-artistic practices through the diffractive prism of critical co-dependence," and that these practices "try to generate environments, relations, and situations that enable new subjectivities to unfold" (p. 60). They also write that these subjectivities are "crafted through unlearning of self-censorship, as well as unlearning of colonial praxis" (p. 60).

## § 9. The Violence of Traditional Methods

The violence of traditional methods of research appears in several linked forms: forced verbalization, premature closure, overwritten voice, and the reduction of material or performative inquiry to something acceptable only once translated into a prior academic grammar.

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn identify "dualist thinking that still dominates academia today" (2015, p. 26). Bolt places artistic research within that academic terrain when she writes that "the development of artistic research has proceeded in the shadow of the research 'model' par excellence, that is, science-as-research" (2016, p. 137). Schwab identifies "reflection" as a prescribed demand in art education and artistic research contexts, especially when it is assumed that knowledge must be verbalized after the fact in order to count (Schwab, 2018, p. 9). Luc Döbereiner states: "Epistemologically, artistic research tends to challenge propositional semantics," and it must go "beyond the confines of standardised forms of academic publishing in the humanities" (2019, p. 48). De Assis and D'Errico write that artistic research "makes of the resistance to definitions, closures, and disciplinary constraints one of its strongest points" (2019, p. 2). Bolt adds that artistic research "continues to be seen as lacking credibility because the methods cannot be replicated exactly" (2016, p. 137).

Draude and Klumbytė warn against treating hybrid or "third space" methods as neutral zones. Their argument is that such spaces must retain their feminist, decolonial, and postcolonial commitments, including attention to power, positionality, and the uneven conditions under which methods are produced and received (Draude & Klumbytė, 2019). Their insistence that hybrid methodologies may need to be created rather than imported is methodological and political at once. The researcher's situatedness is not a contaminant to be removed from the apparatus. It is one of the conditions that must be named and held accountable.

The problem is not only that traditional methods do not fit certain practices. It is also that they can reinstall the very hierarchies those practices were built to contest. Fox and Alldred (2021) warn that diffractive methodology can become "researcher-centric and context-dependent," questioning "how or to what extent" particular methods influenced findings. This is not a representationalist objection. Fox and Alldred are working within posthumanist methodology, not against it. Their concern is internal: if findings are produced through a specific apparatus in a specific situation, how do those findings travel beyond that situation? The question of transferability remains a genuine tension within the methodology, one the field has not fully resolved.

A different problem arises when the standards used to assess research presume a representational model that diffractive and new materialist work does not share. The demand that diffractive methodology prove its objectivity by removing the researcher from the apparatus is not a stricter standard of rigor. It is a return to the very fantasy diffractive practice was developed to refuse. Barad rejects that assumption directly. Representationalism, she argues, is "a prisoner of the problematic metaphysics it postulates" (Barad, 2003, p. 803). Accountability here does not come from erasing the researcher. It comes from naming the apparatus, specifying the cuts, and tracing the marks left on bodies.

## § 10. Closing of Part Three

What the artist-researchers gathered here have in common is not a shared method but a shared refusal. Each refuses the separation between the artwork and the knowledge it produces. Trinh refuses the documentary fantasy of direct access. Bolt refuses the representationalist claim that a practice cannot have material effects. Mäkikoskela refuses the protocol that gathers research material before the making begins. Andersen refuses the position that knowing precedes making rather than emerging through it. Franinović refuses the workshop structure that predetermines what participants will learn. SymbioticA refuses the boundary between ethics and research. Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz refuse the individual author as the unit of knowledge production.

What each then affirms is its own positive account. The filmmaker's interval. The painter's working hot. The sculptor's material resistance. The maker's non-functional prop. The workshop leader's seed. The laboratory's care protocol. The hackerspace's multitude. These are not rebrandings of a single method. Each names a specific operation through which a specific art practice functions as research under conditions in which matter is active, agency is distributed, and the researcher is part of the apparatus that produces what she knows.

This is what Kirkkopelto names when he says that the artistic researcher's medium is converted into a medium of research and that the transition "remains suspended between two stages; it can only suggest the transition by repeating it endlessly but always anew" (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49). The suspension is not a failure of completion. It is the form the research takes. It is the ongoing condition in which generativity rather than exhaustive capture is the form accountable knowledge takes in a world of ongoing becoming.
