## Part One: Posthumanism and New Materialism

### All Things Are Co-Constituted

To begin the task of teasing apart the theoretical and methodological
distinctions between posthumanism and new materialism I must begin with
the irony of pulling apart the entanglement in any semblance of forcing
a taxonomy. Both posthumanism and new materialism reject dualism,
Cartesian binaries, and oversimplification into opposing categories. So
to tease them apart is to follow the diffractive practices we will later
discuss, to search for the differences that make a difference. We begin
(now it is we because the reader is also participating. As my thoughts
unfold so does the readers and a thought baby, an entanglement is made)
with recognition that all things (to be explored later) are
co-constituted together through the continuous becomings of the protean
flow, zoe, the primordial swerve, the entanglement, the void, the
virtual, the immanence, the Wild, non-identity, a life, the absolute,
and ever shifting assemblages both cosmic and atomic and everywhere,
everything, all at once. I will use these words/portals interchangeably
throughout the text except where I am explicitly writing the story of
their origin in the context of our theory/methods/practice.

The point of this teasing apart is to find a place and time to stand, to
stake a position (metaphorical or literal-you choose) in the
entanglement from which to explore/ponder/make sense (a gathering of
diffractions coalesced into an accessible whole-not too large, not too
small) and to most importantly connect to other travelers in the protean
flow. Without a position, how would they find you? How would you find
them? See Henri Matisse\'s series of paintings and cut paper
compositions from early 1900\'s Paris, that for many viewers evoke a
feeling of human connection in the protean flow. [[Dance
I]{.underline}](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/79124)

Before moving further, one concept needs grounding, because it surfaces
under many names throughout this text and the accumulation is
intentional but not arbitrary. The Wild. The void. The absolute.
Non-identity. A life. The primordial swerve. Zoe. The virtual. The plane
of immanence. These are not synonyms in the strict sense --- each
carries the specific weight of the theoretical tradition from which it
comes. But they are pointing at the same thing, or rather, at the same
absence of a thing.

What they name is the ontological ground that exists before and after
any specific intra-action, outside any particular agential cut, beyond
the reach of any apparatus that would make it measurable. It is the
field of virtual potential from which phenomena emerge when conditions
for their emergence are met, and into which they dissolve when the
apparatus that held them in place is released. It is not nothing. It is
not chaos. It is, in Bennett's framing through Lucretius, the condition
of possibility for all swerving --- the clinamen, the deviation from the
straight line that generates all actual things. It is what Adorno names
when he insists that concepts are ouroboran --- that all concepts live
somewhere between the absolute and our more traditional human definition
of knowing, because the absolute is real, generating, and permanently
beyond our grasp.

Bennett borrows the phrase "a life" from Deleuze --- a life, not my life
or your life, but the impersonal generative force that moves through all
living things and is not exhausted by any of them. When she admonishes
Lucretius through Gertrude Stein --- "there is no there, there" --- she
is not saying the void is empty. She is saying it has no fixed location,
no stable identity, no address you can return to. It is everywhere the
formed thing is not yet, and everywhere the formed thing has already
dissolved back into becoming. It is always already operating. It cannot
be measured, only approached --- which is why Bennett names lingering as
the most vital step: to stay long enough in the encounter that the
shimmer of the thing's participation in this larger field becomes
briefly perceptible.

Barad reaches the same ground through quantum physics. Before
measurement, before the agential cut that produces a phenomenon, there
is superposition --- not multiple things coexisting but a state of
ontological indeterminacy in which the boundaries and properties of the
"thing" have not yet been enacted. The apparatus does not reveal what
was already there. It brings forth what was not yet determined. Between
measurements, between intra-actions, what exists is this: the
indeterminate field, the real-but-virtual, the condition from which
specific configurations of time/space/matter emerge and into which they
return. Braidotti calls it zoe --- life as a cosmic force that precedes
and exceeds any individual subject, any bounded organism, any particular
moment of consciousness. The subject is a temporary intensification of
this force, not its owner. "The life in 'me' does not answer to my name:
'I' is just passing" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 211).

These words --- the Wild, the void, the absolute, zoe, a life, the
virtual, the plane of immanence --- will be used interchangeably
throughout this text except where their specific theoretical origins
require distinguishing. The accumulation is the point. Each tradition
arrived at the same condition from a different direction. None of them
can fully name it, because naming requires the agential cut and the
agential cut is precisely what this condition precedes. The words are
portals, not definitions. They gesture toward what is always already
there when there is no there, there.

### The Mechanism of Suppression: How Entanglement Keeps Getting Bracketed Out

The entanglement described above is not a new discovery. It was present
in the earliest strata of western thought --- in Heraclitus, in the
pre-Socratics, in thinkers who were willing to sit with the complexity
and flux of the world before a more convenient framework was
institutionalized over them. The pattern that follows is consistent
across centuries: wherever a thinker pointed toward interrelatedness,
indeterminacy, or the porousness of boundaries, the Platonic inheritance
arrived to domesticate the insight --- extracting the useful notation,
stripping the relational ontology, and handing back a binary. This is
not incidental intellectual history. It is the mechanism that explains
why posthumanism and new materialism had to be invented at all: not to
discover entanglement, but to finally name and defend what kept getting
suppressed (Ferrando, 2019, p. 170; see also van der Tuin and Dolphijn, 2010, p. 95; Haraway, 2016 [page TK]).

The retreat did not end with Plato. It became a repeating pattern:
thinkers whose work pointed toward entanglement, relation, and
indeterminacy get misread or domesticated into the dualist framework
that followed them. Leibniz's binary arithmetic --- originally a
cosmological meditation on generativity, creation from nothing, the
infinite combinations arising from two starting conditions --- was
extracted from its relational context and formalized by Boole into a
logic of true/false, yes/no, on/off. The mystical generativity got
stripped; the binary stayed. That stripping became the foundation of
digital computing, as though Leibniz had meant to say: reality is made
of two things. He meant almost the opposite. Shannon's information
theory compounds the irony: his core insight was that noise ---
disorder, uncertainty, the unpredictable --- carries the most
information. A completely predictable signal carries none. But the
computer science built on his foundations embraced boolean either/or and
suppressed the noise. And underneath every boolean bit is an analog
signal --- a continuous voltage --- interpreted as on or off by applying
an arbitrary threshold. The digital is a reading convention imposed on
an analog reality. The entanglement was always there; it kept getting
bracketed out. (The connection is not accidental: Leibniz sent his binary system to the Jesuit missionary Joachim Bouvet in 1703, reading it through the I Ching — the relational logic was always in dialogue with non-Western thought before Boole stripped it to boolean arithmetic.)

What Boole's formalization stripped from Leibniz was not incidental
surplus but the ontological core of his thought. Coole, following
Merleau-Ponty and Deleuze, traces a Leibnizean inheritance that runs
directly counter to the binary logic his notation was misread to
authorize. For Leibniz, the world is constituted not by discrete units
standing in external relations but by folds: matter as endlessly folding
and unfolding, generating provisional forms as styles of existing rather
than fixed substances. There are no voids in Leibnizean matter: "folds
replace holes," and Leibnizean matter offers, as Deleuze glosses him,
"an infinitely porous, spongy, or cavernous texture without emptiness,"
with each body elastic, "pierced with irregular passages," dependent for
its coherence on the pressure of external forces (Coole, 2010, p. 111).
Deleuze, writing on Leibniz in *The Fold*, notes that the exemplary
science of the fold is not Euclidean geometry but origami --- and finds
anticipations of Leibnizean folding among the pre-Socratics, as well as
within postclassical physics: in Merleau-Ponty's Nature lectures, in
Whitehead, and --- most strikingly --- in the folded proteins of
microbiology (Coole, 2010, p. 110). The fold is not a metaphor for
complexity. It is Leibniz's actual ontological claim: that the interior
and the exterior are not separate domains but continuous surfaces, that
a "fold is always between two folds," that "difference endlessly unfolds
and folds over each of its two sides," and that organic and inorganic
matter share this folded constitution without fundamental distinction
(Coole, 2010, p. 111). For Merleau-Ponty, who in his working notes
explicitly planned to substitute being-in-the-world for Leibniz's God
while maintaining "entirely" certain of Leibniz's descriptions --- "only
ridding them of their substantialist and onto-theological elaborations
in order to apply them to wild Being" --- the body is itself a fold: not
a hole in being but "a hollow, a fold, which has been made and which can
be unmade" (Coole, 2010, p. 107). The body's chiasm --- touching and
touched, sensing and sensed, perceiver and perceived, subject and object
--- is the lived experience of precisely this Leibnizean folded
ontology: not binary opposition between two fixed terms, but a
reversibility that "always miscarries at the last moment," producing the
productive dehiscence, the difference between identicals, that makes
emergence possible rather than only inert repetition. What the Baroque
artists understood, and what Boole stripped out, is that this fold is
not a theoretical position but a perceptual and material fact ---
present in how the body inhabits the world, in how matter coils and
hollows and wraps, in how Baroque chiaroscuro renders "clarity endlessly
plunging into obscurity" rather than separating light from dark as in
Cartesian optics (Coole, 2010, p. 111). Deleuze observes that Descartes
tried to move through the labyrinth of matter along rectilinear tracks
and failed to grasp its curvature; the Baroque art of Caravaggio and
Tintoretto --- where contours are effaced, where light vibrates in the
folds of matter, where draperies and tessellated fabrics and complex
harmonics with multiple rhythms make philosophies that "resolve
Cartesian distinctions of mind and matter through physical means" ---
these are not decorative flourishes but ontological propositions enacted
in form (Coole, 2010, pp. 111--112). The counter-tradition does not
begin with the modernist artists. It runs through Leibniz himself,
through the Baroque, through Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of the flesh.
The binary was always a misreading of a thinker who meant something
closer to the opposite.

Quantum physics follows the same pattern. Bohr's core claim --- that
observation and observed are inseparable, that there is no view from
outside the phenomenon --- was both philosophically and politically
threatening. The entanglement that quantum theory places at the
foundation of physical reality was domesticated: the equations were
kept, the ontological implications were bracketed. The Copenhagen
interpretation became the acceptable face of quantum mechanics precisely
because it permitted physicists to use the formalism without confronting
what it said about the nature of reality. As Barad demonstrates at
length in *Meeting the Universe Halfway*, Bohr was pointing toward
something that the dominant framework had no room for: an entangled
world in which the observer is always already part of what is being
observed, in which there is no prior independently determinate reality
waiting to be measured. The retreat from this recognition is the same
retreat Plato made. Posthumanism and new materialism are, among other
things, the philosophical tradition that finally refuses it.

The suppression did not go uncontested. While Bohr's entanglement was
being domesticated by the Copenhagen interpretation, artists were
enacting the same ontology in practice --- not as philosophical argument
but as perceptual fact. Merleau-Ponty identifies Cézanne as a
phenomenologist avant la lettre, and the connection is not incidental.
Cézanne was working at precisely the historical moment when both quantum
physics and phenomenology were beginning to articulate what classical
science and Cartesian painting had systematically suppressed: that the
body does not perceive the world from a detached point of view, and that
matter does not consist of solid objects with fixed contours awaiting
neutral description. When the body moves around in space, it does not
perceive things with the relative sizes objective calculation would
predict. It does not inhabit a flat, geometrical, fully determinate
plane but a milieu, an Umwelt that remains ambiguous, indeterminate, and
resonant with an expressive significance that affects the body's
perception of spatial relations. Merleau-Ponty discerns Cézanne as
trying to capture these perspectival distortions as they occur in
perception, in order to convey "an emerging order," where the object is
"in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes" (Coole,
2010, p. 104). Cézanne's painting brings the material world alive; it
does not measure or represent reality but emulates the way it
materializes in perception (Coole, 2010, p. 106). Coole notes that
Merleau-Ponty explicitly contrasts this with the Cartesian tradition of
classical perspective --- what he calls "Renaissance perspective as a
cultural fact" and "impoverished" as artistic style precisely because
its Euclidean geometry presents nature in fixed, objective coordinates
rather than in the ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant field in which it
actually appears. Cézanne's refusal of that convention is therefore not
an aesthetic choice but a phenomenological position: the attempt to
suspend culturally fashioned perception and uncover the "vertical" world
of "brute" or "wild" perception as it emerges (Coole, 2010, p. 106). And
Cézanne is not alone. The Baroque artists --- Caravaggio, Tintoretto ---
had already been working with folded matter: chiaroscuro as the visual
enactment of Leibnizean ontology, contours effaced, light vibrating in
the pleats of materiality, the rectilinear tracks of Cartesian space
refused in favor of curvature, coiling, and density. The entanglement
Plato suppressed, that Bohr's Copenhagen interpreters bracketed, that
Leibniz's binary was misread to deny --- artists had been living it and
working from it across four centuries of Western art. The 20th century
counter-tradition makes this increasingly explicit and increasingly
methodological. Dada introduces chance, noise, and indeterminacy into
artistic practice as ontological statement, not provocation. The Bauhaus
and Black Mountain College bring together artists, scientists,
musicians, and designers in configurations that refuse disciplinary
separation --- themselves a kind of institutional enactment of the
entangled world. John Cage, working at Black Mountain and drawing
directly on the I Ching --- the relational cosmology that Leibniz had
encountered and stripped to notation --- recovers what got bracketed
out: indeterminacy, chance, noise as information rather than
interruption. His 4'33" is not silence; it is the insistence that the
world is always already sounding, that the frame of attention is the
apparatus. Robert Rauschenberg's combines --- street debris, newspaper,
paint, radio --- are assemblages in Bennett's sense before Bennett named
them: matter with thing-power, distributed agency, the refusal of a
sovereign authoring subject. Shannon's suppressed insight that noise
carries the most information finds its artistic enactment in all of
this: the Dadaist cut-up, the found sound, the accidental score. The
counter-tradition is not incidental to the theoretical project of
posthumanism and new materialism. It is its genealogy. New materialist
art practice as research is, in part, the current methodological
formalization of what Baroque painters, Cézanne, Cage, and Rauschenberg
were already doing.

The suppression of matter by representation was not only contested in
music and physics. Visual art theory ran the same argument, and it ran
it with quantum physics as its resource. Barbara Bolt, working from
painting practice (in Coleman and van der Tuin, eds., *Methods and Genealogies of New Materialism*, 2024), identifies the gap that Western visual theory has
installed since Plato as its founding premise: representation is
second-order data, a substitute that can never be its object, a sign
that circulates within social meaning but cannot be in dynamic relation
to the materiality of its referent (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 269). The influence
of literary theory and cultural studies on visual art deepened the gap
--- reducing visual representation to a sign-system, a complex of
discursive meanings. Bolt traces the limit of even Butler's
sophisticated performativity account: Butler can explain how bodies
materialize through repetition and discourse, but cannot account for
"the materialisation that occurs in the interplay between the matter of
bodies, cultural knowledges or discourse, and the materials of
production" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 271). Matter slips away back into
signification. The return Bolt calls for --- "to the energetic potential
of matter" --- runs via Vicki Kirby's (1997) reading of Bohr: for
quantum physics, representation is not removed from an originary source,
particles are inseparable identities in/of spacetime, and there is no
enclosed local context within which causality might be contained. Bolt
draws the direct implication: "the discoveries made by quantum physics
have direct implications for the relationship between 'reality' and
'representation' in the visual arts. They bring into doubt the
separation of sign and referent and suggest that a continuum exists
between the process of representation and the representation" (Bolt,
2024 [orig. 2003], p. 273). Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) confirm that this suggestion
made Bolt one of the first art scholars to address quantum theory in
relation to visual practice --- a move made in parallel with, not
downstream from, Barad's development of agential realism from the same
quantum sources.

The art history lineage that supports this claim runs deeper than Bolt's individual contribution. Alongside the philosophical counter-tradition traced above --- Baroque folding, Cézanne's phenomenological perception, the Black Mountain assemblage --- there is a parallel track in which artists and art educators were explicitly drawing on particle physics as a resource for rethinking what art does and how it works. Scholars in art education have traced a lineage in which the quantum overthrow of classical determinism --- the insistence that observation is always participatory, that phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatus that produces them --- found its cultural articulation in the dematerialization of the art object and the turn toward process, event, and relation (Sullivan, [year and citation TK]). The trajectory runs: quantum indeterminacy (Bohr, Heisenberg) → pedagogical translation through Bauhaus and Black Mountain (process over product, experimentation over mastery) → Cage's chance operations as explicit quantum analogue → conceptual art's refusal of the fixed object → systems art and early computation (Burnham's *Systems Esthetics*, 1968) → generative and posthuman practice now. Bolt's intervention, originally published in 2003 and republished in Coleman and van der Tuin's *Methods and Genealogies of New Materialism* (2024), names the same quantum resources from within painting practice rather than conceptual art --- arriving at the same conclusion from a different studio position: that the discoveries of quantum physics have direct implications for the relationship between reality and representation in the visual arts (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 273). The two lines --- conceptual art via Sullivan and painting theory via Bolt --- converge on the same claim that Barad will later formalize philosophically: representation is not a second-order picture of a pre-given world but a co-constitutive intra-action with it.

Despite Barad\'s (and multiple other New Mats and PostHums) insistence
that language has too much power, representationalism has trapped us
(entities that interface with the world through words) into fictional
dualities, Cartesian prisons, new levels of Dantean hell where the soul
is trapped in endless reflection with no way out of the ping pong game
to nowhere,

I still really like words. And New Mats and PostHums, whether it is a
case of affection or utility, brilliantly utilize words in all their
grammarly manifestations to help the reader/listener/participant to not
just take away the meaning/definition of a word, but to enter into the
experience of the universe/reality/world as it always already is. Words
become portals into the entanglement, the interrelatedness, the always
becoming of everything, everywhere, all at once.

### An Introduction to the Questions that Guide my Sharing

When I first began trying to write for academic audiences, to fit all my
thoughts into twenty-five linear pages of careful citations that moved
with military precision into the field of battle, where I must fight to
prove I can make sense (not to my immediate colleagues and mentors, but
to the academic world at large), I struggled with the enormity of the
field, the sheer volume of words and ideas and becomings and
connections. I could see them all, all at once, always. In the first
chapter of Jane Bennett\'s Vibrant Matter, she asks how to help people
learn to see the vibrancy of matter, to open themselves up to the
agential swarm, the primordial swerve, to notice, to linger in the world
of material becomings. I was struck and annoyed, because I already live
in the world of vibrancy and frequently I am exhausted with people who
don\'t see as I see, because they frequently have no idea what I am
going on about, like maybe there is a giant rabbit behind my shoulder
whispering into my ear (or at least they think that I think there is a
giant rabbit behind my shoulder whispering into my ear).

After months of wrangling and too many to count false starts in
organizing my thoughts so they made sense in twenty five linear pages, I
realized the audience I care most about making sense to is the audience
I should be writing for, and then I had a vision, a lens, a path that
was clear. I would write to my students, for my students,
past/present/future students. The classroom for me, for the past
twenty-two-ish years (and maybe before that when my hat leaned towards
student), is like an art gallery (my experience/perception/invention of
an art gallery). My favorite thing about making art and showing my art
is when my work, my thought babies, get to go live with other people\'s
thought babies and have conversations and make new thought babies.
Sending my art out to play with other art is like watching my own
children on the playground exploring their surroundings, testing out
their limits, timidly for some of my children, and boldly for others,
making gestures towards the other children, birds, trees, rocks, grass,
babies bunnies just born under the mulch that the kind grounds keeper
surrounded with temporary fencing to keep them safe until the mommy
bunny could move them. Because of the nature of the classroom, or at
least my classroom, the humans that entered the space, physical or
metaphysical, brought their own art, their own thought babies. And it
was my greatest joy and honor to share my thoughts with them and for
them to bless me by sharing their own. So when I write that I write for
my students, it is with the same eagerness to show my new friend the
beautiful caterpillar, the roly poly just unfurled after I dug it from
the mud under the rock, or to co-create/participate/feel/make the
connection, see the interrelatedness of all things shimmer and glow and
our hearts and our brains light up with the happy chemicals of
connection.

During my initial presentation to my comprehensive exam committee I was
reminded that in the beginning as researchers we lay the foundation,
present the ground of what has come before from which our seeds will
grow. We save analysis for later, for comprehensive exams we report. I
honor that intention by carefully listening to the voices of each author
and trying not to assert my own already always thinking. With that said,
it would be irresponsible to not state clearly that I come with a
framing that influences the agential cuts I make and the marks I leave
on bodies.

The following roly poly, apple tree bud, strange smell emanating from I
do not know where, because sharing my thoughts in writing/words/linear
is no different to me than sharing any other delight, is organized for
the sake of making sense, into three questions:

1.  How have other posthumanist theorists conceptualized diffractive
    > practice?

2.  How have new materialist theorists conceptualized art practice as
    > research?

3.  What are the theoretical and methodological distinctions between
    > posthumanism and new materialism?

As I neared the end of researching and started to write, I realized the

#### Walking Talking Minerals

Bennett elaborates on the long view of time and provides a proof from
the first single cell organisms to the evolution of homosapiens, that we
are all made of the same thing, we are boundaryless, and thus humans are
walking, talking minerals. If we are all made of the same things, and
humans have agency, and humans are things, then things have agency. And
vice versa, by inscribing things with thing-power we also elevate the
agency of humans as things with more power in the ethical
configurations/re-configurations of reality than humans believe they
have.

Because Barad grounds herself at the atomic level, we can imagine that
we are all permeable and things moving freely through each other at the
level of electrons, protons, quarks, and other members of the particle
zoo.

#### Bennett, Braidotti, Barad: Ontology

All three theorists reference the essential nature of
reality/time/space/matter as something in a constant state of becoming
and something whose exact nature is unknowable. Bennett situated us in
thoughts of Thoreau\'s The Wild, Lucretius\' primordial swerve,
Adorno\'s the Absolute and non-identity, a life, protean flow, and the
void. Braidotti invited us to Zoe, and Barad to quantum theory,
entanglement, and her frame agential realism. All of these describe a
universe where there are no essential enduring qualities of any thing,
thus the thing itself is not completely knowable. Because reality is
created through specific configurations of space and matter that only
exist as they are in one unit of time, as soon as we think we have
divined some essential nature, some knowable character, the
configuration is gone.

Bennett stages us in assemblage and agential swarms. Braidotti situates
us in cartographies and Barad in entanglements and agential realism.

An assemblage is a unique configuration of things/actants that come
together in a particular space/time/matter configuration. The things act
on each other through a mechanism of call and response (as described by
Derrida). Agency is distributed, thus an assemblage can also be imagined
as an agential swarm. The foundational unit of reality for Bennett is
the thing or actant.

For Barad, phenomena, or unique material discursive arrangements are the
primary building block of reality. Agential realism is her term for how
this reality works: phenomena are the basic unity formed through the
frame of an apparatus to create intra-actions of things thru material
discursive arrangements. Barad created the term \'intra-action\' as a
response to the inadequate misleading term interaction, which
implies/assumes a pre-given body with inherent qualities. Intra-action
defines agency as a mutually constituted distributed, emergent force
(Barad, 2007, p.141). Apparatuses are phenomena, not unique stable
things, constituted as "specific agential practices... through which
specific exclusionary boundaries are enacted," and as "dynamic
(re)configurings of the world." As each apparatus is a unique
material-discursive arrangement, so are the cuts they make, drawing
unique distinctions between subject/object/instrument. Different marks
on bodies indicate configurations of apparatus.

Objectivity can only be obtained through our accountability to the
boundaries we made through the agential cut and the marks we left on
bodies. Barad rejects the \'God\'s trick\', highlighted by Haraway
(1988, p. 581), that outcomes or measurements are ever neutral. Questions
for a diffractive practice notebook might become: What is the apparatus?
What cut does it enact? What/who does the cut exclude? What marks are
left on bodies?

#### A Shared Commitment to an Affirmative Ethics

Bennett\'s ethics are grounded in the understanding that if humans are
also things, then what we do to things, we do to ourselves. Bennett
hopes through this realization that humans will decenter themselves and
take more care in their situatedness in the world/ecology.

Via agential cuts we create moments of objective reality and
accountability for the cuts we make, the differences that make a
difference, and who/what is included and who/what is excluded. This is
the foundation of Baradian ethics.

For Braidotti, the raison d\'etre is affirmative ethics. Bennett also
speaks of a need to not just critique (pointing to this as an explicit
failure of critical and other theories/methods), but to imagine and
create a different future, and different mode of being. Braidotti\'s
theory and methods begin and end with the goal of creating an
affirmation, and intention, of a more ethical reality, and creating the
action steps necessary to get there.

The ontological grounding for Braidotti's affirmative ethics is zoe ---
life as a cosmic force that exceeds and precedes any individual subject.
The subject, in her account, is "an autopoietic machine fuelled by
targeted perceptions" functioning as "the echoing chamber of zoe." This
nonanthropocentric view "expresses both a profound love for Life as a
cosmic force and the desire to depersonalize subjective life-and-death.
This is just one life my life. The life in 'me' does not answer to my
name: 'I' is just passing" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 211). Affirmative ethics
is not optimism. It emerges from this radical depersonalization: because
the individual "I" is not the center of life but a temporary passing of
it, the logic of retribution, rights-claims, and compensation --- all
premised on a stable individual subject --- dissolves. What replaces it
is an "ethics of affirmation" that "suspends the quest for both claims
and compensation and resists the logic of retribution or rights"
(Braidotti, 2010, p. 214). The wound is real. The response is to
depersonalize it, move through it, and affirm the generative force of
life continuing beyond it.

### The Irony of the Rejection of Taxonomies

This section answers the first question: what are the theoretical and
methodological distinctions between posthumanism and new materialism?
The question is answered not by forcing a clean taxonomy --- which would
betray what both frameworks insist about the world --- but by tracing
the different emphases, genealogies, and methodological centers of
gravity that make the two frameworks distinguishable even as they remain
entangled. That distinction matters because it determines what kind of
lens Part Two and Part Three can offer: the specificity of what each
framework contributes to practice depends on being able to say, with
some precision, what each one is and what it is for.

### Origin Stories

Ferrando (2014) new materialism is a branch of posthumanism evolving at
the end of the 1990s when \"nature and culture were recognized as
intrinsically entangled\" (Barad, 2007), as indiscernibly different
terms, thus Haraway\'s \"natureculture\" (2003). The locus event is an
ontological recognition that the human is hybrid, \"not one but
many\"(Ferrando, 2014, p.169), or as Bennett would call \"walking
talking minerals\"(Vibrant Matter) \"We have never been human\" because
\"human is a \'human\' concept\"(Ferrando, 2014, p.170)

Discrimination is an ongoing reconstruction of varying components of
self versus other, aggressive dualisms that commit violence on the
entangled, the borderless, the world of shifting boundaries that are
self-enacted, rather than demanded by an outside. All forms of
discrimination and bias come from rigid hierarchies of cultural, social,
economic value systems. Ferrando traces these proclivities back to
Aristotle, Plato, and the Old Testament, where the world was separated
into hierarchies of the ideal and the profane. Posthumanism in contrast
is a multidimensional network where the boundaries between individuals
are blurred, and the world of matter is conceived and perceived in the
same way the world is in a state of always becoming. Ferrando, 2014

Posthumanism was nurtured by the feminist examination of differences and
how boundaries are drawn when the hegemonic dualistic accounting of the
world, popular since the time of Plato, was distinguished. If we turn
away from the politically convenient polemic of us versus, then how will
we define boundaries/borders to build symbiosis and coexist with less
conflict. Participatory accountings of the production of the self were
gaining popularity, such as Erving Goffman\'s Presentation of Self in
Everyday Life, which demonstrated that conceptions of self/other,
male/female are not easily separated. (Ferrando, 2014, p.169)

Manuel DeLanda and Rosi Braidotti --- independently of one another, and
working from different scholarly fields --- first began using the term
'neo-materialism', or 'new materialism', in the second half of the 1990s
for a cultural theory that does not privilege culture but focuses on
what Donna Haraway (2003) would call 'naturecultures'. Posthumanist
theories emerged from a deep engagement with continental philosophy,
aiming to make the social sciences and humanities more philosophically
informed (Taguchi & Eriksson, 2021).

> Patriarchal thought has limited female biology to its own narrow specifications. The feminist vision has recoiled from female biology for these reasons; it will, I believe, come to view our physicality as a resource, rather than a destiny. In order to live a fully human life we require not only *control* of our bodies (though control is a prerequisite); we must touch the unity and resonance of our physicality, our bond with the natural order, the corporeal ground of our intelligence.
>
> — Adrienne Rich, *Of Woman Born* (1976, p. 40)

The term "new materialism" may have been coined in the 1990s, but the theorists who built it consistently locate its epistemological foundations upstream, in feminist theory's insistence on the body as the site and condition of knowledge. Braidotti names Adrienne Rich's "politics of location" — the methodological claim that knowledge production must begin from the specific, situated, material body of the knower — as "the cornerstone" of the neo-materialist feminist project, tracing a direct line from Rich's 1984 essay through Haraway's situated knowledges (1988) into the standpoint debates that shaped new materialism's early formation (Braidotti, *New Materialism*, p. 22; *Posthuman Knowledge*, p. 59). Bennett, tracing the intellectual lineage of *Vibrant Matter*, identifies "feminist studies of the body" as the larger trend within political theory that put "desire and bodily practices back on the ethical radar screen" — the precondition for her own move toward nonhuman vitality (*Vibrant Matter*, p. xi). She then names the eco- and ecofeminist philosophers — Plumwood, Mathews, Coles among them — as the teachers from whom she learned the "cultivated, patient, sensory attentiveness to nonhuman forces" that vital materialism requires (p. xiv). Rich's formulation was characteristically direct: "Begin not with a continent or a country or a house, but with the geography closest in — the body" (*Blood, Bread, and Poetry*, p. 212). That sentence is not a political slogan. It is an onto-epistemological claim — that situatedness is not contamination but the only honest starting place — and Braidotti and Bennett are both, in their different registers, still standing on that ground. Mąkowska (2022) names what this means structurally: Rich's poetics constitutes an anticipatory new materialism in which "the politics of location is manifested in her medical history and how the history of her life is registered in her flesh" (p. 50), a formulation indistinguishable from what Barad will later call the material-discursive intra-action through which bodies come to matter. The vitality and agency of matter long preceded any theoretical apparatus — named by Heraclitus, tracked by Lucretius, present in every tradition that refused Plato's retreat. What feminist body theory built was not a discovery of that vitality but an apparatus precise enough to critique the specific historical conditions under which it had been suppressed: the humanist, patriarchal, dualist arrangements that concentrated that suppression in the female body and in matter coded as feminine. New materialism did not discover the body. It inherited the apparatus feminist theory had built from it.

Coole & Frost position the "posthumanist" ontological reorientation
similarly as a response to contemporary conditions, where we must
"reorient ourselves profoundly in relation to the world, to one another,
and to ourselves," naming what is at stake as "nothing less than a
challenge to some of the most basic assumptions that have underpinned
the modern world including its normative sense of the human and its
beliefs about human agency" (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 6). The challenge
extends to "material objects and the natural environment" --- to how we
labor on, exploit, and relate to the nonhuman world. Crucially, Coole
and Frost insist that calling this turn toward new materialisms does not
deny "their rich materialist heritage": many contributors to the project
draw inspiration from materialist traditions that long predate the 1990s
coinage. The newness is not in the recognition of material entanglement,
but in the convergence of tools and vocabularies that finally make it
speakable across disciplines simultaneously.

This convergence marked a turn away from the persistent dualisms in
humanist traditions (Ibrisim, 2020). New materialist theories largely
emerged from scholars trained as natural scientists with interests in
science theory, technoscience, and social, feminist, indigenous,
environmental, and animal-rights issues (Taguchi & Eriksson, 2021).

The epistemological break this represents was named most sharply by Haraway. In "Situated Knowledges" (1988), she identifies the "god trick" --- the view from nowhere, the claim to a disembodied, universal perspective --- as the dominant fantasy of western science, and refuses it not in the name of relativism but in the name of a stronger objectivity: one that is partial, embodied, and accountable to its own situatedness. "I am arguing for politics and epistemologies of location, positioning, and situating, where partiality and not universality is the condition of being heard to make rational knowledge claims" (Haraway, 1988, p. 589). This is the direct bridge between Plato's institutionalized retreat from entanglement and posthumanism's and new materialism's response: where the Platonic inheritance authorized the god's-eye view as the only legitimate knowledge, Haraway names it as a trick --- a mask over a specific and interested position that refuses accountability for the cuts it makes. Kuhn had earlier shown that scientific paradigms are not neutral accumulations of truth but historically conditioned frameworks that determine what counts as a problem, what counts as a solution, and what gets suppressed as anomaly (*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 1962). Haraway radicalizes this: the issue is not only that paradigms shift but that the fantasy of the view from nowhere is itself an exercise of power. Posthumanism and new materialism inherit both moves --- Kuhn's paradigm critique and Haraway's situated epistemology --- and extend them into ontology: it is not only knowledge that is situated and partial, but the world itself that is constituted through specific, accountable, intra-active arrangements.

Timeto (2011) traces this as a single continuous argument with three stations. Hayles's constrained constructivism establishes that representations are neither mirrors of reality nor arbitrary constructions — they are consistent or inconsistent with the flux, where consistency means local, interactive, instrumentally efficacious engagement. This is the first refusal of both naive realism and relativism: knowledge is constrained by the world even when it cannot achieve a one-to-one correspondence with it. Haraway's situated knowledges and figuration extend this by insisting that the partiality of the perspective is not a weakness but the condition of accountability — the partial perspective names where it sees from, and that naming is what makes it answerable. Barad's agential realism takes the final step: it is not only the knowing subject that is situated but the phenomenon itself that is produced through the intra-action of apparatus and matter. Representation is not removed from an originary source — it is the cut that produces the things it appears merely to describe. Timeto names the coherence of this lineage: Hayles, Haraway, and Barad are all "diffract[ing] the rays of technoscience" (Haraway, 1997, p. 16) — using diffraction, whether as method or metaphor or ontological claim, to replace the representationalist picture of knowledge as a mirror with a picture of knowledge as an intra-active production. The diffraction metaphor itself originates earlier: Haraway first introduces it in "The Promises of Monsters" (1992, pp. 299–300) in direct engagement with Trinh T. Minh-ha's formulation of "critical difference within." By 1997 Haraway develops it further; Barad (2003) then grounds it in quantum physics and makes it an ontological claim. Diffraction did not begin when Barad named it. It is the name Barad gives to what this entire philosophical lineage was already doing — and that lineage begins with a filmmaker, not a physicist.

Srnicek (2017) groups posthumanism, speculative realism, and
object-oriented philosophy as subgroup variants of new materialism,
though posthumanism is highlighted as introducing concepts of agency,
structure, and epistemology to the umbrella grouping. Smelik (2018), in
contrast, situates posthumanism as the context for new materialism, as
posthumanism introduced the explicit decentering of the human which made
room for things and nature to also have agency. Using Bennett's term,
posthumanism horizontalized and flattened the field.

The standard account of new materialism's origins runs through feminist
science studies (Haraway, Barad), political theory (Bennett, DeLanda),
and philosophy (Braidotti, Grosz). This account is accurate but partial.
Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) argue that studies of art were not merely
retrospective recipients of new materialist theory but "one of the very
milieus in which the call to rethink the involvement of matter in human
practices and in the more-than-human world arose" (Kontturi and Tiainen,
2021, p. 259) --- emerging through painting practice, music scholarship,
performance studies, and practice-led research in the 1990s and 2000s,
in parallel with, and sometimes prior to, the consolidation of NM as a
named theoretical position. Yet the standard origin story assigns art a
subsidiary role: artworks appear on the covers of foundational NM texts
(Cornelia Parker's suspended bricks on Bennett's Vibrant Matter; Thomas
Demand's images on Barad's Meeting the Universe Halfway), evocatively
illustrating theoretical propositions rather than being recognized as
sites where those propositions were first worked out. The consequence,
as Kontturi and Tiainen note, is that art theorists are frequently
invoked as evidence of NM claims while remaining outside the mainstream
discussion currently centered on Barad. For a comps document arguing for
new materialist art practice as research, this gap in the standard
genealogy is not incidental. It is the gap the document exists to close.

Adrienne Rich named the project in 1973. In "Diving into the Wreck," she describes descending not to recover the myth of the wreck but the wreck itself — "the thing I came for: / the wreck and not the story of the wreck / the thing itself and not the myth" (*Adrienne Rich's Poetry and Prose*, ed. Gelpi and Gelpi, p. 67). This is the epistemological stance the entire genealogy traced above requires: the refusal to accept the inherited story — the dualist framework, the representation standing in for the thing, the concept exhausting the object — and the insistence on returning to the material itself, to what was there before the mythology overlaid it. Rich was practicing this in poetry two decades before new materialism named it as theory. The origin story does not begin with DeLanda and Braidotti in the 1990s. It begins wherever someone refused the wreck-as-story and dove toward the thing itself.

### The Transversality of New Materialism

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn (2010) offer the most concise positive characterization of what new materialism is *for*, as distinct from what it is against. Where the standard account emphasizes new materialism's refusals — of dualism, of representationalism, of the linguistic turn's monopoly on meaning — van der Tuin and Dolphijn name its affirmative preoccupations directly: "Not primarily interested in representation, signification and disciplinarity, new materialism is fascinated by affect, force and movement as it travels in all directions. It searches not for the objectivity of things in themselves but for an objectivity of actualisation and realisation. It searches for how matter comes into the agential realism and how matter is materialised in it. It is interested in speeds and slownesses, in how the event unfolds according to the in-between, according to intra-action. New materialism argues that we know nothing of the (social) body until we know what it can do" (van der Tuin and Dolphijn, 2010, p. 153).

The closing sentence — "we know nothing of the (social) body until we know what it can do" — is Spinoza's claim, restated. It is also the methodological consequence of everything the origin stories section has been building: if matter is generative rather than inert, if bodies are defined by their capacities rather than their essences, then the question is never what something is but what it does, what it enters into, what it makes possible. New materialism's transversality — its capacity to cut across disciplines, paradigms, and the linear spatiotemporalities of epistemic trends — follows directly from this orientation. A framework fascinated by affect, force, and movement cannot be contained within a discipline. It travels in all directions because the assemblages it studies do.

### Position as Critique

Posthumanism tends to begin from a critique of the figure of "Man" (the
humanist universal), and it concentrates methodological energy on
subjectivity, power, and ethical/political reconfiguration in "posthuman
times." Braidotti's "posthuman predicament," for example, explicitly
couples posthumanism (critique of "Man") with post-anthropocentrism
(critique of species hierarchy / human exceptionalism), and treats the
subject as non-unitary, embedded, and in-process.

New materialism tends to begin from a critique of matter as inert and of
knowing as representation. It concentrates methodological energy on
material agency, assemblage, distributed causality, and nonhuman
participation in public life. For Jane Bennett "vital materialism" is
explicitly a worldview of vibrant matter and "always-becoming," and
diffractive practice becomes the perceptual-ethical labor required to be
faithful to that reality (e.g., training perception toward nonhuman
vitality, distributed agency, and non-binary emergent causality).

In practice, these two emphases overlap constantly. Braidotti's
zoe-centered orientation of a life as generative force, for example,
often reads "new materialist" in its stress on impersonal vitality
beyond the human. Meanwhile Bennett's account is openly political and
ethical, not "just ontology," as it links perceptual shifts to humility,
self-interest redefined, and ecosocial action.

### The Researcher as Apparatus

The critique of researcher bias has traveled a specific arc across
fields. The positivist ideal --- the view from nowhere, the neutral
observer, the God's trick Haraway names --- gave way, under pressure
from feminist, postcolonial, and interpretive traditions, to the demand
for positionality disclosure. The researcher was asked to name her
location: her race, gender, institutional affiliation, theoretical
commitments. This disclosure was framed as a corrective --- a way of
flagging potential distortion so that readers could adjust. The
assumption remained that bias was a contamination to be acknowledged
and, where possible, minimized. Research was still understood as
something that would ideally happen without a situated subject in the
way.

New materialist and posthumanist frameworks reject the premise. If the
world is always-becoming through intra-action, if there is no phenomenon
prior to the apparatus that produces it, if matter and meaning
co-constitute each other through specific material-discursive
arrangements --- then there is no view from outside. The researcher is
not adjacent to the research. She is part of the apparatus. Her
situatedness does not introduce error into an otherwise clean process;
it is constitutive of the process itself. Barad is unequivocal: the
apparatuses that produce phenomena are not separate from what they
produce. The agential cut that makes a phenomenon legible is always
already enacted from within the entanglement, never from outside it. On
this account, the demand that a researcher minimize her bias is a demand
that she pretend to be something the ontology says she cannot be.

This move — from situatedness as contamination to situatedness as constitutive — was not invented by posthumanism. It was worked out first in feminist practice. Rich's "politics of location" is its founding gesture: the demand to begin not with a pretense of neutrality but with the body, the specific geography closest in, as the only honest epistemological starting point. Braidotti traces the line from Rich directly into new materialism's situated epistemology (*New Materialism*, p. 22), and Mąkowska (2022) shows that what Rich was enacting in her poetics — mapping her own flesh as medical history, sociopolitical inscription, and epistemological ground simultaneously — is structurally identical to what Barad will later formalize as the researcher's position within the apparatus. The apparatus argument is not a posthumanist novelty. It is feminist body theory formalized. Haraway's "God's trick" critique — the demand to abandon the view from nowhere — is the same critique Rich was making in 1984 when she insisted that there is no speaking from outside one's location, only from within it. What changes between Rich and Barad is vocabulary and philosophical frame. The underlying claim — that the knower is always already inside what she studies, that this is not a problem to be corrected but a condition to be worked — is continuous.

The artist-researcher takes this one step further, but the move is not
toward greater centrality --- it is toward a more precise account of her
position within a distributed apparatus. Braidotti's cartography is not
the map one neutral observer makes of a pre-existing terrain. It is the
specific trajectory of a specific nomadic subject moving through a
specific configuration of forces, in intra-action with the other human
and nonhuman actants that constitute the apparatus alongside her. The
knowledge produced is not generalizable in the traditional sense. It
does not claim to represent what any researcher would find. It claims
something more precise and more honest: this is what became thinkable
through this particular assemblage --- this subject, this material, this
set of encounters, this configuration of time/space/matter --- and the
research artifact produced through it is offered as evidence to other
entities capable of perceiving it. The situated cartography is not the
packaging around the findings. It is the findings.

This is what it means for the artist to be a participant in the
apparatus rather than its origin. The apparatus in Barad's sense is the
whole material-discursive arrangement through which phenomena come into
being --- the specific configuration of bodies, instruments, concepts,
histories, matter, and practices that makes certain things visible and
others invisible. The artist-researcher is one actant within that
arrangement, not its sovereign. She does not produce the research
phenomenon alone; she enters into intra-action with materials, spaces,
other bodies, institutional structures, and the accumulated force of
prior encounters, all of which participate with equal ontological weight
in what gets made. What the artist-researcher contributes is a specific
transposition framework --- the unique mediation through which this
assemblage produces a research artifact that can be shared as evidence
with other perceiving entities. Her situatedness is not the whole
apparatus. It is the particular node through which the apparatus becomes
this inquiry, generating this knowledge, for this community of
reception. Specificity is not a limitation on generalizability. It is
the condition of possibility for any knowledge at all.

### When the Human Got Out of the Way

It is worth noting that new materialism is not new in any absolute sense.
Systems of thought that refuse the human/nonhuman divide and insist on
the agency and vitality of the material world have long informed
Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and valuing (Pennycook, 2024; Ros
Sole, 2020), as well as Spinoza's monism and the idea of multitude as
the agent of potentia (Torosyan, 2021, p. 55). What the 1990s coinage
names is not a discovery but a consolidation — the moment when these
traditions found common vocabulary across disciplines simultaneously.

Understanding what new materialism is positioning itself against
requires a brief sketch of what it replaces. The dominant modern account
of matter --- Cartesian-Newtonian in its inheritance --- treats matter
as passive, inert stuff to be acted upon by an external force, whether
that force is the human will, divine providence, or the laws of physics
imposed from without. As Coole and Frost describe it, this framework
"yields a conceptual and practical domination of nature as well as a
specifically modern attitude or ethos of subjectivist potency" (Coole &
Frost, 2010, p. 9): the observer classifies matter from a distance,
aided by theories that enable the manipulation and reconfiguration of
matter on an unprecedented scale. The human subject is the agent; matter
is the object of that agency. New materialism defines itself precisely
as an alternative to this picture --- not by reversing its terms, but by
refusing the distinction that makes it possible.

Bennett (2010) provides an exhaustive, yet not exclusive, accounting of
vitalist/materialist movements that predated her own vital materialism.
Materialism as defined by:

> In philosophy and metaphysics, materialism is a form of monism holding
> that matter is the fundamental substance of nature, so that all
> things, including mind and consciousness, arise from material
> interactions and depend on physical processes, including those of the
> human brain and nervous system. (Wikipedia, 2026)

Cheah (dat) exposes that new materialism \"is a (quasi-)transcendental
ground that has been obscured by traditional ontologies.\"

### All Things Matter but What About Me?

Posthumanism and new materialism are entangled theories and frequently
tossed back and forth as interchangeable. There exists more posthumanism
in the literature without new materialism than exists new materialism
without posthumanism. This does not mean that new materialism is a
subset of posthumanism because that implies a hierarchy. New materialism
has its own origin story unique from posthumanism, and at some point
(1990\'s according to Wolfe, 2010, echoed by Ibrisim, 2020) they began
to lean more on each other as necessarily twinned frames through which
to navigate the world. I contend they are emerging continuums of
difference, co-constituted, and their combined momentum pushing towards
new ethics of what counts as the real, what counts as agency, and what
counts as knowledge.

Posthumanism\'s position in the entanglement is best illustrated (as a
Braidottian nomadic subject on the cartography of human thought) by Cary
Wolfe\'s Introduction to the edited collection *What is Posthumanism?*
Wolfe provides two origin stories and progenitors of the thread of
connection: Foucault in the 1960s and circa World War II 1946-1953 Macy
Conferences on cybernetics and the subsequent invention of systems
theory. Quoting Wolfe quoting Foucault (Wolfe, 2010, xii), \"the
historical appearance of this thing called \'man\' was not\":

> the transition into luminous consciousness of an age-old concern, the
> entry into objectivity of something that had long remained trapped
> within beliefs and philosophies: it was the effect of a change in the
> fundamental arrangements of knowledge. As the archaeology of our
> thought easily shows, man is an invention of recent date. And one
> perhaps nearing its end.
>
> If those arrangements were to disappear as they appeared, if some
> event of which we can at the moment do no more than sense the
> possibility---without knowing either what its form will be or what it
> promises---were to cause them to crumble, as the ground of Classical
> thought did, at the end of the eighteenth century, then one can
> certainly wager that man would be erased, like a face drawn in sand at
> the edge of the sea.

Posthumanism hinges on varying intensities of belief that man is an
invention/transient/ephemeral/\"there is no there there\" (Bennett
admonishing Lucretius, 2010, 18)(see also Gertrude Stein *Everybody\'s
Autobiography*, 1937), in constant peril of erasure.

While all posthumanist scholars have moved beyond the modernist
reification of Man as uniquely special in the world/reality, Wolfe
(2010) reminds us that some scholars still hold on to the notion that
man is still special, just less special than we previously believed.
Srnicek (2017) notes that some posthumanist approaches maintain
important distinctions between human and nonhuman systems, such as the
human capacity for self-reflection and intentional action. (De Liaño &
Fernández-Götz (2021) argue some New Humanism (as opposed to
posthumanism) elements may remain valuable for current societal
challenges; humanist perspectives need not be wholly rejected. Blok
(2024)argues for a lingering human exceptionality by rejecting the idea
that conativity and responsivity are shared symmetrically by all
material entities; Blok argues instead that some entities are conative
(e.g., rocks, sand dunes) but not responsive, limiting hybridisation
(horizontally distributed agency). This reluctance to completely
decenter the human (in general) points to the difficulty to heed the
ethico-onto-epistem-ological call to decenter the self (as an
individual).

The posthuman framework of new materialism proposes (and I would assert
requires) an entirely non-anthropocentric view, removing the human
subject completely from the center and instead focusing on complex
assemblages where humans and things interconnect (Smelik, 2018). New
materialisms add to posthumanism the adoption of a monist, Spinozian
conception of matter (Abadia, 2018). Braidotti (2019) establishes that
the critical posthumanities are conceptually founded on a neo-Spinozist
monistic ontology that assumes radical immanence and the primacy of
intelligent and self-organizing matter. So in this case, the posthuman
sits within the new materialist cradle. Posthumanism answered the
question of who am I with: we are "material networks of relations,
fluctuant becoming in symbiotic interaction with the 'others', the
environment, our surroundings; we are constant potentials" (Ferrando,
2014, p. 171). Nothing is independent, not even the instructions for our
evolution.

Not all new materialisms, however, are posthumanist in the sense this document uses that term. Gamble, Hanan, and Rossdale (2019) identify three distinct strands within the broader new materialist field: vital materialism (Bennett, drawing on Deleuze's reading of Spinoza and Leibniz), which treats matter as inherently vibrant and agential; negative materialism, which includes speculative realism and object-oriented ontology (Harman, Meillassoux, Morton), approaches that treat matter as non-relational to thought, with thought having emerged ex nihilo to humans alone; and performative materialism (Barad, Kirby), which centers the intra-active and entangled transformation of observer and observed, where epistemology and ontology are not separate but symbiotic intra-actions. This document draws on vital and performative materialisms and explicitly excludes the negative strand. Speculative realism and OOO reinstall a separation between matter and thought that is the very condition posthumanism and new materialism were built to refuse. The two strands this document uses share the posthumanist ontological commitment to co-constitution: matter is not inert, the human is not sovereign, and the knowing subject is part of what is known. Barad's posthumanist performativity is the precise hinge where these two strands converge --- it is, as Militsi (2021) summarizes from Barad (2003, p. 808), a "diffractive elaboration" on Butler's and Haraway's contributions, simultaneously completing posthumanism's material decentering of the human and grounding new materialism's account of matter as co-constitutive rather than merely acted upon.

### Ethico-Onto-Epistem-Ology 

\"These theories have ontological and epistemological commitments that
are distinct from those in other traditions.\" (Siffrinn and Coda, 2025,
p.198)

Wolfe (2010) posited that a new understanding of the world\'s
becoming/ontology, required a new epistemology/way of knowing that
matched the world as it is. Karen Barad instantiated the term
*ethico-onto-epistem-ology* (2007, p.90) to elucidate the necessarily
entangled ontologies, epistemologies, and ethics of engagement with the
world. See the all encompassing nature of
[[Kudzu]{.underline}](https://www.nature.org/en-us/about-us/where-we-work/united-states/indiana/stories-in-indiana/kudzu-invasive-species/).

Fox & Alldred (2021, need page) summarize Barad's DM as
"ethico-onto-epistemology," where a researcher's "responsibility for the
practices of research and how its outputs make a difference (ethics),
that which is observed (ontology), and the research apparatus...
(epistemology)" are inseparable.

The ontological commitments of new materialism have roots in a vitalist
tradition whose scope extended well beyond biology. As Coole and Frost
trace in their introduction to *New Materialisms*, critical vitalism
posited a generative principle responsible not only for morphological
development in organisms but for "the progressive development of
personality and history" --- an isomorphism between physical and
psychological orders, matter and meaning, that the dominant mechanist
tradition systematically suppressed (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 49). This
is the same suppression named in the Plato argument above: matter as
intelligent, self-organizing, and generative was repeatedly displaced by
matter as passive, inert, and awaiting inscription by an external force.
The ethico-onto-epistemological insistence of Barad, Bennett, and
Braidotti is, among other things, a refusal of that displacement --- an
insistence that matter is "always already an ongoing historicity"
(Barad, 2003, p. 822) and that any ethics adequate to this reality must
take the vitality of matter seriously, not as metaphor, but as
condition.

Bennett and Braidotti share that stance. The foundational goal of
Bennett\'s 2010 *Vibrant Matter: A Political Ecology of Things* was the
hope for a new ethics of entanglement, where humans realized that if
they are made of the same stuff as all other things, if humans are
\"walking, talking matter\" (*Vibrant Matter* [page TK]), then maybe "we are not one body or
embodied, rather we are an array of bodies," and therefore "we must
cultivate a talent for remembering it, because this grounds us in an
emergent reality" (Bennett, *Vibrant Matter* [page TK]).

Posthumanism views human life as a decentralized human-technology
relation, alliance, or network, rejecting traditional dichotomies
between humans and machines and aiming for total symmetry. (Blok, 2024)

Posthumanism and new materialism share a non-dualistic,
non-representationalist, entangled understanding of nature and culture
(Coole & Frost, 2010). Philosophically, posthumanism and the new
materialisms presuppose that knowing and being are inseparable (Coda,
2024).

\"Epistemological practices are necessarily ontological practices; to
know is to be part of the world's continual construction\" (Coda, 2024).

Method must be grounded in ontology: if a theory's ontology is
relational, processual, and non-deterministic, then "method" cannot
remain representational (as if it were a neutral mirror).

The move from representationalism to relational ontology consistently generates one objection: that in rejecting absolutes, PH and NM embrace relativism. If there is no view from nowhere, if knowledge is always situated, if the apparatus is always part of what it produces — then how is any account better than any other? This is the charge that sank postmodernism's ethical project, and both Barad and Braidotti are aware of it. The answer runs through three moves made in sequence.

The first is Hayles's constrained constructivism (1997): representations are neither mirrors of an independent reality nor purely arbitrary constructions. They are consistent or inconsistent with what Hayles calls "the flux" — the real but unreachable ground of things. Consistency means local, interactive, instrumentally efficacious engagement; it replaces the true/false binary with a not-true/not-false relation that "rides the cusp" between the world as it is and the world as we can construct it (Hayles, 1997, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 157). This is not relativism — it is accountability to the constraints the world imposes on representation, even when those constraints cannot be specified in advance.

The second is Haraway's situated knowledge: the partial perspective is not a weaker objectivity but a stronger one, because it is accountable for the position from which it sees. The god-trick — the view from nowhere — is not neutral; it is a specific and interested position that refuses accountability for the cuts it makes. Situated knowledge names the cut and owns it.

The third is Barad's agential cut as the locus of ethical accountability. The cut is not arbitrary — it is specified by the apparatus, produces determinate marks on bodies, and can be described, compared, and held accountable. Braidotti's nomadic cartography enacts the same move at the level of subject formation: the nomadic account is grounded not in a universal standard but in the specific political and material coordinates of the subject's location. Neither framework produces relativism. Both produce accountability without universalism — which is precisely what the ethico-onto-epistemological framework names: responsibility is located in the intra-action, not outsourced to an external canon.

then how could we continue to reject knowledge not constituted as a

The transversality van der Tuin and Dolphijn trace through new materialism — its tendency to cut across disciplines, paradigms, and temporal boundaries rather than settling into any one territory — finds empirical confirmation in what is happening in applied research fields right now. Siffrinn and Coda's (2025) survey of posthumanist and new materialist research in applied linguistics finds that "these theories are being operationalized by scholars in the field on a larger scale than is being returned in research databases" (p. 194) — which means the transversal movement has already outrun its own documentation. The theories travel faster than the institutional categories built to classify them.

What that operationalization looks like in practice is a shift from representational to relational units of analysis. In applied linguistics contexts, Siffrinn and Coda (2025) work primarily through assemblage thinking — arguing that once the assemblage is taken as the basic unit of inquiry in that field, "there cannot be a dividing line between word and thing, nor can there be an autonomous agent who stands outside of an empirical field imposing higher order meaning on an assemblage or its constituent parts" (p. 195). The specific unit varies across the broader field: for Bennett it is the thing and its distributed agency within the assemblage; for Barad it is the phenomenon — the material-discursive arrangement produced through the agential cut, which precedes and constitutes the things that appear to interact within it. What these diverge on is where to locate the basic unit. What they share is the refusal of any autonomous agent standing outside and imposing meaning from above. Canagarajah (2018a) describes this in terms of diverse semiotic resources working together — playing "a collaborative role," shaping "each other," capable of being "agentive" (p. 39) — and scholars across applied linguistics, education, and environmental research are using relational thinking as their analytical frame rather than discrete, pre-given objects (Appleby & Pennycook, 2017; Weideman, 2017).

Affect is central to what this shift makes visible. Where representational methods attend to what data means, relational methods attend to what data does — and what it does often operates below the threshold of representation. Affect, as Colman (2010) puts it, is "the change, or variation, that occurs when bodies collide, or come into contact" (p. 11, cited in Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 196), enabling attention to the multisensory, intensive dimensions of an assemblage that conventional methods cannot register. Data, in this frame, has "an active materiality whose vibrancy might be to perturb rather than to be seen" (Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 197, citing Frimberger et al., 2018). The point is not that findings become invisible but that what counts as a finding shifts: research "can function as an event of becoming, one in which 'decisions have to be made for the moment, by the moment'" (Thrift, 2007, p. 217, cited in Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 198). The researcher is not separate from what is being studied — she is part of the apparatus that produces the phenomenon she is studying (Siffrinn and Coda, 2025, p. 198) — which is the empirical confirmation of what Barad names theoretically as intra-action.

### Deleuze Called for a Diffractive Practice

In Cheah's essay "Non-Dialectical Materialism" (in Coole and Frost, 2010) on the philosophical juxtaposition between the materialist views of Derrida and Deleuze, he focuses on their shared agreement "on the coming of the new --- the advent of the entirely other that disrupts presence or the opening of actuality to multiple becomings." The effectivity of both materialisms lies in the urgency of rethinking the ontological bases of, well, everything everywhere all at once. Deleuze explicitly called for a diffractive practice.

Cheah identifies in Deleuze a third emphasis beyond the critique of representation and the affirmation of assemblage: the "(quasi-)transcendental ground" (Cheah, 2010, p. 89) that "indexes virtual singularities prior to their actualization as forms, and to the in-between of already actualized forms that are always pulsing with singularity and virtual force" (p. 88). This ground is the condition of possibility for the actual — not a stable substrate beneath events but the generative field from which events arise and into which they dissolve. There is no intentional subject or object here; both are always contingent, always in a state of becoming. The subject is generated from this transcendental field, made up of pre-individual and impersonal singularities (Cheah, 2010, p. 85). This is the same condition the document's opening section names under multiple portals: the Wild, the void, zoe, a life, the plane of immanence. Deleuze arrives at it through what he calls the virtual — and he does so in explicit dialogue with Kant's noumenon, the thing-in-itself that exists beyond what any apparatus of measurement can reach. Where Kant treats the noumenon as permanently unknowable and therefore methodologically irrelevant, Deleuze refuses that move: difference is not phenomenon but "the noumenon closest to the phenomenon" (Cheah, 2010, p. 84, citing Deleuze, p. 222). The virtual is real. It generates the actual. It cannot be measured, only approached — which is why, in the language this document has already established, lingering is the practice it calls for.

For Deleuze, matter as the plane of immanence is a dynamism of differentiations, speeds, and flows prior to any organized form. Following Hjelmslev, Deleuze and Guattari define matter as "the unformed, unorganized, nonstratified, or destratified body and all its flows: subatomic and submolecular particles, pure intensities, prevital and prephysical free singularities" (cited in Cheah, 2010, p. 87). The material body is not an organism but an aggregate whose elements vary according to their connections and relations of movement and rest — a body without organs whose constitution is always provisional, always in process. "Materiality is nothing other than the plane of immanence" (p. 86). Every actual object carries a virtual content; actualization is not the realization of a pre-formed possibility but the expression of a difference that was real before it was determinate. The virtual "must be defined as strictly a part of the real object --- as though the object had one part of itself in the virtual into which it is plunged as though into an objective dimension" (Cheah, 2010, p. 84, citing Deleuze, p. 209). The actual object is not the destination of a one-way process. It emits new virtuals as it actualizes, bringing itself back into relation with the field of differential relations in which it can always dissolve and become otherwise.

Bennett renders Deleuze's virtual in her own key: "a life" --- not my life or your life, but what she calls "a pure event, freed from the subjectivity and objectivity of what happens," "a subjective current visible only fleetingly," inhabiting "that uncanny, non-time existing between the various moments of biographical or morphological time" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 53). It is "a restless activeness, a destructive creative force present that does not coincide fully with any specific body" (p. 53). Through Nietzsche, Bennett names its character: "a monster of energy that does not expend itself but only transforms itself, a play of forces and waves of forces, at the same time one and many, a sea of forces flowing and rushing together, eternally changing" (Nietzsche, cited in Bennett, p. 53). A life "tears the fabric of the actual without ever fully coming out in a person placed or thing"; it is "matter of movement or matter energy, a matter in variation that enters assemblages and leaves them" (p. 53). It is, finally, "a vitality, proper, not to any individual but to pure eminence --- that is not actual though it is real" (p. 53). Vibratory. Liquid. Virtual. A particle force without a position. This is the same condition Deleuze names through the noumenon, Braidotti through zoe, Barad through superposition. The words are different portals into the same field --- and diffractive practice is the attempt to cultivate enough attentiveness to feel the field moving beneath the things.

This is why diffractive practice asks not what differences exist between things but what differences are being made, and what those differences make possible. Bateson named this the ground of information itself: a difference that makes a difference. Where comparative analysis maps differences as properties already residing in discrete objects, diffractive analysis traces the patterns that emerge when differences intra-act — attending to what the encounter produces rather than what the objects were before it. For Barad, diffraction patterns are precisely "patterns of difference that make a difference": not a record of where things sit in relation to one another, but a tracing of the effects difference generates as it moves through a field. The question shifts from "what is X" to "what does this configuration of differences produce, and what does it foreclose." This is Deleuze's dice throw made methodological: Being is constituted each time by events of chance, throws that give rise to different singularities or commencements (Cheah, 2010, p. 83). Ontology is the chaosmos from which the cosmos emerges. The researcher does not stand outside this process as its describer. She is one of the throws.

The distinction between posthumanism and new materialism established across Part One is a theoretical distinction — it maps different emphases, genealogies, and methodological centers of gravity. What confirms it from the direction of practice is that the protocols researchers have developed under the posthumanist and new materialist umbrella reproduce exactly the division of labor the theoretical account predicts. Lambert (submitted) uses NM concepts — intra-action, assemblage, spacetimemattering — to operationalize the PH ethical stance of refusing the humanist reflective practitioner. Hill (2017) frames the three modes of becoming-diffractive in explicitly PH terms — the decentered subject, the refusal of the individual teacher as autonomous agent — but operationalizes them through NM conceptual tools: intra-action, becoming-with, assemblage, affect. Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025) develop their six transposition practices on the PH side of the distinction — critique of Man, nomadic subject, affirmative ethics — but anchor them in a NM ontology of relational becoming. In each case, PH provides the critical-ethical vector that says why the practice matters and what is at stake, and NM provides the material-ontological vector that says what the world is made of and how phenomena are constituted. Neither framework can do the other's work. The practice texts confirm the theoretical distinction not by citing it but by enacting it.

This is where Part One opens onto Part Two. If posthumanism and new materialism together establish that the world is relational, processual, and virtual before it is actual, then the question becomes how to inhabit and work within that world as a researcher and practitioner. Diffractive practice is the response to that question — and it is the subject of what follows.

## Part Two: Diffractive Practice

This section answers the second question: how have posthumanist theorists conceptualized practice that honors the ontological commitments of posthumanism and new materialism? The original framing of this question named "diffractive practice" as its target. That framing remains useful but requires widening. A Google Scholar search for texts addressing diffractive practice and transpositions together returns zero results — the two are not yet named as a joint category in the literature. What the literature does show is a family of protocols and orientations developed under different labels — diffractive practice (Barad, Haraway), transpositions (Braidotti), becoming-diffractive (Hill), diffractive watching (Militsi), mosaic listening (Clark and Moss, extended by Dudley) — that share the same ontological ground without sharing a common name. The umbrella that holds them together is not a label but a set of commitments: rejection of representationalism, decentering of the human, distributed agency, accountability to the apparatus rather than to an external canon, and ethics as ongoing negotiation rather than fixed rule. Under this umbrella, what Part One established as the PH/NM distinction maps onto a methodological division of labor: posthumanism provides the critical-ethical vector — what is at stake, who is centered, what counts as justice — while new materialism provides the material-ontological vector — what the world is made of, how phenomena are constituted, where agency is distributed. Protocols labeled as diffractive practice or transpositions are what happens when both vectors are put to work simultaneously. Part Two traces how different theorists have developed that simultaneity, and what it looks like when it is operationalized in research and art practice.

### Theory into Method

Posthumanist methods often map subject-formation and power in the
present (cartographic, genealogical, figural, affirmative). Braidotti's
"cartography" is explicitly presented as a grounded, selective mapping
of forces with a nomadic subject as the protagonist: tracking what we
are ceasing to be and what we are becoming, using concepts as
navigational tools rather than final definitions. Braidotti insists that
critique not stall at diagnosis, asserting an "affirmative" pivot,
giving the thinking entity/human something to do with the theory, a way
to be, a position from which to move (Wolfe, 2010).

In contrast, new materialist methods do not center the human as primary
actant in the world, instead training perception toward nonhuman agency
and distributed emergent causality/assemblage/attunement/lingering.
Things have agency and their own thing-power whether humans recognize it
or not. New materialism is the most relevant philosophical framework for
rethinking the relationship to the complex environmental and social
ecologies that define the posthuman era (Braidotti, 2023).

A further trait of the new materialisms that distinguishes them from
posthumanism's more oppositional orientation is their antipathy toward
oppositional ways of thinking itself. As Coole and Frost observe, new
materialist exponents "generally decline to locate themselves explicitly
through critiques of ontological dualism such as one finds in
Cartesianism: they prefer a creative affirmation of a new ontology"
(Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 9). The prevailing ethos of new materialist
ontology is consequently "more positive and constructive than critical
or negative: it sees its task as creating new concepts and images of
nature that affirm matter's immanent vitality." Such thinking is
accordingly post- rather than anti-Cartesian. It avoids dualism or
dialectical reconciliation by espousing a monological account of
emergent matter --- drawing inspiration from alternative ontologies such
as Spinoza's, developed less contemporaneously with Cartesianism than
alongside it, yet which "until recently enjoyed a far more subterranean
or subjugated existence" (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 9). This Spinozian
undercurrent --- matter as self-organizing, intelligent, immanently
generative --- is precisely the tradition that Braidotti names as the
neo-Spinozist monistic ontology at the foundation of the critical
posthumanities.

This is not a claim unique to posthumanism or new materialism. Science and technology studies arrives at the same conclusion from a different direction. Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch (2020), in their introduction to *Dialogues between Artistic Research and Science and Technology Studies*, state it directly: "research methods do not only observe and represent materials, issues, and events but in fact act upon and intervene in these materials, issues, and events" (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 7). Research not only analyzes, documents, and informs — it performs realities and ontologies, and reforms and transforms them through the act of researching. This is the STS version of what Barad names theoretically as the apparatus: the instrument of knowing is not neutral with respect to what it knows but constitutive of it. The convergence of PH/NM and STS on this point is significant. It means the claim is not a philosophical preference or a political commitment specific to feminist theory. It is what careful empirical study of how research actually works shows to be the case, across disciplines, when the backstage labor of science and research is subjected to scrutiny. Method is always already intervention. The question is whether the researcher acknowledges it.

### Relational Worlds Need Relational Methods

If the world is constituted through relation and intra-action — if phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, if agency is distributed across assemblages of human and nonhuman actants, if matter is vibratory and generative rather than inert — then methods that treat phenomena as discrete, stable, and pre-given do not merely produce incomplete findings. They produce a systematically distorted account of what the world is. The mismatch between a relational ontology and a representationalist method is not a technical problem to be corrected through rigor. It is an ethical problem: to impose a subject-object framework on a relational world is to enact the very suppression of vitality, agency, and entanglement that posthumanism and new materialism were built to refuse.

Bennett names the perceptual root of the problem directly. Without a cultivated, countercultural practice of attending to what exceeds the subject-object split, "the world appears as if it consists only of active human subjects who confront passive objects and their law-governed mechanisms" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. xiv). This appearance is not a neutral description. It is the product of trained perceptual habits — habits that are, as Bennett insists, politically consequential. A world that appears to consist only of active subjects and passive objects is a world that authorizes domination, extraction, and the suppression of the distributed agency that runs through every assemblage. Relational methods are not a preference or a philosophical style. They are the attempt to correct a trained perceptual distortion that has been doing political damage for as long as the subject-object split has been the default frame.

Developing the capacity for relational perception requires what Bennett calls foundational comportments: an open-minded, open-ended willingness to suspend the pre-conceived; an anticipatory readiness to encounter thing-power before it has been named or categorized; and "a perceptual style open to the appearance of thing-power" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 5). This last phrase matters because it frames the researcher's orientation not as neutrality but as a trained receptivity — a readiness cultivated in advance of any specific encounter. Bennett traces this orientation to Thoreau's instruction to practice "the discipline of looking always at what is to be seen" (p. 5): not looking for what you expect, not looking to confirm what you already know, but sustaining the open discipline of looking itself as a practice.

This perceptual discipline connects directly to van der Tuin and Dolphijn's (2010) argument that new materialism requires cartography rather than classification as its methodological posture. Classification is a territorializing gesture: it assigns new phenomena to pre-existing categories, confirms what is already known, and forecloses what the encounter might otherwise disclose. Cartography is generative: it maps forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold, producing a "living map" — Braidotti's phrase — rather than a fixed taxonomy. The shift from classification to cartography is the methodological consequence of the ontological shift from substance to relation. Relational worlds cannot be classified into stability. They can only be mapped in motion.

Fox and Alldred's (2021) work in applied research domains — education, health, sustainability — demonstrates what this looks like in practice. Diffractive method is particularly generative precisely in research situations that are already assemblages of institutions, bodies, technologies, and affects, because it refuses to quarantine any of those elements as mere context. The research situation itself — the classroom, the clinic, the policy environment — is treated as an active participant in what gets known, not as a neutral container for findings that then travel elsewhere. The question of how findings travel into policy and practice contexts is itself a question about assemblage: what configurations make certain knowledge mobile, and which suppress it.

### Refusal of Exhaustive Capture

Braidotti states this as a methodological principle of cartography:
"Being grounded, any cartographic account is necessarily selective, partial and never exhaustive" (Braidotti, 2018).

This matches van der Tuin & Dolphijn's argument that
classificatory/territorial logics can't "capture" theory-formation's
nonlinearity; new materialism instead de-territorializes and becomes
"cartographical."

Once method is recognized as boundary-making, exhaustiveness becomes not
only impossible but also undesirable as a governing fantasy.
Boundary-making is "a doing" that entails "constitutive exclusions," and
that is precisely why diffractive practice must treat partiality as a
form of rigor.

And the project's curatorial framing (Kontinuum) ties the
"human/non-human" debate to matter as meshwork, and frames artistic
research as engaging a "material continuum" of
impurities/mixtures/singularities that resist categorization — "special cases and exceptions... are the rule" (Kirschner, 2016).

The refusal of exhaustive capture is not only a posthumanist or new
materialist methodological principle. It originates in artistic and
feminist practice. Trinh T. Minh-ha articulates it in 1990, working as a
filmmaker and theorist, nearly two decades before Barad's agential
realism consolidates the argument philosophically. In "Documentary
Is/Not a Name," Trinh insists that "meaning should be prevented from
coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown" and that what
persists between the meaning of something and its truth is "the
interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth
congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). This interval is not a methodological
deficiency. It is the condition of possibility for knowledge that does
not replicate the epistemic violence of totalization. Her 1992 interview
develops this directly: speaking nearby rather than about means "a
speaking that does not objectify, does not point to an object as if it
is distant from the speaking subject or absent from the speaking place.
A speaking that reflects on itself and can come very close to a subject
without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, p. 87). This is
a practice of partial, situated, non-exhaustive knowledge --- prior to
and independent of Haraway's situated knowledges (1988) and Barad's
agential cuts (2007), not derived from them.

The result of this citational movement is that an insight first articulated by an artist-filmmaker working in 1990 becomes legible within the NM theoretical canon only when it has been absorbed into feminist science studies. Trinh is acknowledged in secondary literature; her practice is the origin point. This is one instance of the broader pattern Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) identify: art and artists are frequently cited as evidence of NM claims while remaining outside the mainstream theoretical discussion centered on Barad. Restoring Trinh to her place in the genealogy is not a corrective addition. It changes what the refusal of exhaustive capture is — from a theoretical position derived from quantum physics and philosophy to a practice that filmmakers were enacting before theorists named it.

### Navigating the Field

Nothing exists as an individual entity until the agential cut, when we
experience a unique material discursive arrangement thru a particular
apparatus that allows for the measurement of phenomena, not things, but
the intra-action of things in their ongoing becoming.

Bennett spoke of Derrida\'s call and response as how we intra-act with
things in the agential flow. Barad points to particle flows propelled by
the mechanisms of intra-action of electrons and their kin.

Agential swarms/assemblages need a mechanism of intra-action. Actants
are the individual elements that have the capacity to affect other
entities and themselves be affected.

As navigators we are the nomadic subjects, moving through a cartography
of reality/the Zoe as it is in its specific time/space/matter
configuration. Our movement along a path is guided by our affirmative
ethics.

Trinh T. Minh-ha is among the most precise illustrations of this figure.
As a Vietnamese filmmaker working in Senegal, the United States, and
across the postcolonial world, she is not metaphorically nomadic. She
inhabits multiple positions simultaneously without resolving them into a
stable identity. She describes her practice as "a constant flow from the
inside out and outside in," and insists that the challenge lies in
"materializing a reality" in which the personal is not naturally
political but every form of knowledge carries a specific location
(Trinh, 1992, p. 82). Her films --- Reassemblage (1982), Naked Spaces:
Living is Round (1985), Surname Viet Given Name Nam (1989) --- do not
represent nomadism as subject matter. They enact it as method: refusing
the position of the expert-observer who speaks about, insisting instead
on a "speaking nearby" that "reflects on itself and can come very close
to a subject without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, p.
87). The nomadic cartography does not claim to map a fixed territory. It
traces a movement through a territory that is itself in motion,
attending to what becomes visible from this location at this moment ---
and offering that trace as evidence rather than conclusion. Fuser (2019)
reads Trinh's entire cinematic project through Braidotti's nomadic
subjectivity, arguing that her aesthetics produce a "politics of
speaking nearby" that structurally destabilizes the gaze of alterity and
challenges the epistemic authority of the Western observer (Fuser, 2019,
pp. 107--121). The nomadic subject, in Trinh's practice, is not a
philosophical figure. It is a filmmaker with a camera, a specific body,
a specific history, working in specific material conditions, refusing
exhaustive capture.

Braidotti's cartography is "a non-hierarchical and non-linear mapping"
where "any form of encounter with the world... constitutes a legitimate
resource of meaning." Forgetting to Forget, on Rosi Braidotti

Cartography as method is explicitly a politics of location as much as a
navigation tool. "Situated locations draw a cartographic map of power
relations and thus can also help identify possible sites and strategies

What connects Braidotti's cartography to the Adornian three steps that

willingness to be the clown, the practice of lingering --- these are the
comportments of the nomadic subject moving through a cartographic field,
refusing the fantasy of a view from above, staying inside the
entanglement. Cartography is not a map you consult before entering the
terrain. It is what you produce by moving through it accountably.

### Why We Need the Metaphor/Phenomena of Diffraction

Barad warns us about the \"representationalist trap of geometrical
optics\" and moves us toward an onto-epistemo-ethics of diffraction
versus reflection.

Barad's account explicitly contrasts reflection (mirror-like
representation) with diffraction (patterns that register relational
difference and histories of intra-action). Diffraction \"does not
produce \'the same\' displaced, as reflection and refraction do.\"
(Barad quoting Haraway) Diffraction is not concerned with the sterility
of difference, but the fecundity of the effects those differences make.
The interference patterns produced are both a cutting apart and a
cutting together holding the same time/space/matter in a singular
phenomena/material discursive arrangement. Diffraction is an iterative
(re)configuring of patterns of differentiating-entangling, through which
boundaries are enacted and relata emerge. Relata do not pre-exist these
relations/phenomena, nor do they exist after the agential cut that marks
the boundary. There can be no same displaced because there is no same.

Haraway's account of diffraction vs. reflexivity runs directly: "reflexivity, like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere, setting up the worries about copy and original and the search for the authentic and really real." What is needed instead is "to diffract the rays of technoscience so that we get more promising interference patterns on the recording films of our lives and bodies" (Haraway, 1997, p. 16).

In the Transpositions project's "Staging Collisions" discussion, data is
explicitly framed as not simply "given" but produced by an apparatus;
data is "facta," emerging from interactions among tools, measuring
devices, analysis methods, and researchers' expectations. 

Braidotti supplies a compatible ontological "ground tone" for why a
diffractive stance makes sense: a neo-Spinozist monistic ontology of
radical immanence (matter as intelligent/self-organizing), with the
knowing subject as relational, embodied, embedded, affective, and
accountable (not transcendental consciousness) (Braidotti, 2018).

### How Each Theorist Conceptualizes Diffractive Practice

The umbrella framing established above names a family of protocols that share ontological commitments without sharing a label. Within that family, each theorist has developed a distinct version of what the practice requires. The distinctions matter — not because they produce incompatible frameworks but because each version emphasizes different aspects of the same ground, and understanding what each one contributes makes the whole framework more navigable for a practitioner.

**Barad: diffraction as material-discursive apparatus work.** For Barad, diffractive practice is inseparable from her concept of the apparatus. The apparatus is not a tool the researcher picks up and puts down. It is the material-discursive arrangement — the configuration of instruments, concepts, bodies, institutions, and practices — through which phenomena are produced. The researcher is always already part of the apparatus; there is no view from outside. Diffractive practice, in Barad's version, means making the apparatus explicit: naming it, specifying the agential cuts it makes, and tracing the marks it leaves on bodies. The agential cut is not a free interpretive choice — it is the determinate event through which boundaries are enacted, relata emerge, and accountability is located. Diffractive practice is rigorous precisely because it specifies these cuts rather than naturalizing them. The question it asks is not "what does this mean" but "what does this configuration of differences produce, and what does it foreclose." Its ethical force comes from the insistence that exclusions are constitutive — what the apparatus does not produce is as significant as what it does — and that the researcher is response-able for those constitutive exclusions (Barad, 2003; 2007).

**Haraway: diffraction as figuration and situated partial perspective.** Haraway introduces diffraction as a feminist optical metaphor before Barad formalizes it philosophically, and she does so in explicit dialogue with Trinh T. Minh-ha. In "The Promises of Monsters" (1992), Haraway quotes Trinh's formulation of "difference" as "a 'critical difference within,' and not as special taxonomic marks grounding difference as apartheid," and extends it: "Diffraction does not produce 'the same' displaced, as reflection and refraction do. Diffraction is a mapping of interference, not of replication, reflection, or reproduction. A diffraction pattern does not map where differences appear, but rather maps where the effects of differences appear" (Haraway, 1992, pp. 299–300). This is the origin of the diffraction metaphor in feminist theory — generated from a reading of Trinh, not from quantum physics alone. By 1997, in *Modest Witness*, Haraway develops diffraction into what Barad calls a "fourth semiotic category," connecting it to her broader critique of reflexivity: "reflexivity, like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere," whereas diffraction produces genuinely new interference patterns (Haraway, 1997, p. 16). Haraway's version of diffractive practice is inseparable from her commitment to figuration — representations that are "performative images that can be inhabited," partial maps accountable to their situatedness (Haraway, 1997, p. 11). Barad in turn takes Haraway's diffraction metaphor and grounds it in quantum field theory, making the metaphor into an ontological claim: phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, and diffraction is not only a way of reading but a description of how the world is constituted (Barad, 2003, citing Haraway, 1992, p. 300).

**Braidotti: transpositions as nomadic creative leap.** Braidotti does not use the term "diffractive practice" as her primary label, but her transpositions enact the same ontological commitments through a distinct vocabulary and procedural emphasis. Where Barad's version centers the apparatus and the agential cut, and Haraway's centers the situated partial perspective and figuration, Braidotti's centers movement, becoming, and the creative reconfiguration of imaginary formations. Transposition is drawn from music — the operation of playing the same piece in a different key, producing displacement and dissonance that opens new possibilities — and from genetics — the capacity of elements to move across and transform the field they enter. The transponent is a hybrid agent of change whose communication is transformative, actualizing latent/virtual affinities and altering the field it enters (Braidotti, 2006; 2011). Diffractive practice in Braidotti's version is nomadic: it is a refusal of fixed identity, a cartographic tracking of what we are ceasing to be, and an affirmative orientation toward what is becoming possible. The accountability structure is the nomadic ethics of location — the account is grounded in the specific political and material coordinates of the subject's position, not in an external canon of verification. Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025) operationalize this as six practices drawn from Braidotti's oeuvre: embracing a relational ontology of becoming, critiquing imaginary formations, preparing the creative leap, accounting for location and positioning, storying the in-between space, and developing new frames of resonance. These six practices are the authors' own operationalization — Braidotti does not prescribe this sequence herself — but they are accountable to Braidotti's theoretical commitments and serve as one worked example of what transpositions look like when converted into research methodology.

**Bennett: diffractive practice as perceptual and ethical retraining.** Bennett does not use the term "diffractive practice" either, but her account of what a vital materialist methodology requires is the clearest statement in the literature of what the practice demands as a personal and political orientation. For Bennett, the central obstacle is perceptual: the trained habit of seeing the world as consisting only of active human subjects and passive objects. Correcting this requires the four Adornian techniques — making conceptualization itself an explicit object of thought, attending to the qualitative singularities of the object, exercising the utopian imagination, and admitting the clownish element — as well as the specific comportments of lingering, openness, and anticipatory receptivity (*Vibrant Matter*, pp. 5, 16–18). Bennett's version of diffractive practice is the most explicit about the affective and ethical dimensions: the goal is not only epistemological accuracy but a reconfigured relation to the world, one that produces what she calls "enchantment" — not a naive romanticism but the productive disorientation of discovering that the world is stranger and more agentive than the familiar frame allowed. This enchantment is the experiential condition for the political consequences that follow: if things have vitality, if agency is distributed, then the politics of domination that depends on a world of passive objects and active subjects is no longer self-evidently rational.

**Where they converge and where they diverge.** The four versions share an ontological ground (relational, material-discursive, distributed agency, ongoing becoming), a methodological posture (refusal of exhaustive capture, accountability to the apparatus and its cuts), and an ethical orientation (responsibility is located in the intra-action, not outsourced to an external canon). Where they diverge is in emphasis and entry point. Barad enters through physics and the apparatus — the most rigorous account of how phenomena are produced. Haraway enters through feminist technoscience and figuration — the most politically explicit account of whose knowledge counts and from where. Braidotti enters through philosophy and nomadic ethics — the most explicit account of how the subject navigates and transforms imaginary formations. Bennett enters through affect and perception — the most explicit account of what the practice demands of the practitioner as an embodied, sensing being. A researcher using this framework does not have to choose between these versions. She draws on all four, letting each contribute what it does best: Barad for apparatus specification and accountability, Haraway for situatedness and partial perspective, Braidotti for the creative leap and cartographic movement, Bennett for the perceptual and ethical retraining that makes any of the others possible.

### How Do We Comport Ourselves for this Reality

For Bennett, diffractive practice becomes a discipline of attending to thing-power, broadening the concept of self-interest, and cultivating a more horizontal account of human and nonhuman actants --- because otherwise we remain trapped in the politics of domination produced by the subject-object split. Baradian diffraction pressures both posthumanism and new materialism to refuse representational shortcuts. The question then is not whether to comport ourselves differently but how.

Adorno, in Bennett's reading of Negative Dialectics, did not only diagnose the problem of nonidentity — the permanent gap between any thing and any concept of it. He laid out specific techniques for training oneself to detect and accept that gap rather than suppress it. These are not theoretical positions but practical comportments, intellectual and aesthetic exercises for navigating a world that will never go entirely into its concepts.

The first technique is to make conceptualization itself an explicit object of thought — to become aware that the process of concept-formation automatically obscures the inadequacy of its own products. Critical reflection can expose this cloaking mechanism. The exposure intensifies the felt presence of nonidentity and works against the hubris of conceptualization, producing what Bennett describes as a recursive or metacognitive thinking style: habitually questioning one's own thinking, remaining attentive to what any concept is leaving out. This is the ouroboran move — using reflection on conceptualization to loosen the grip of conceptualization.

The second technique is to attend to the qualitative singularities of the object — to resist flattening the thing into what the concept needs it to be, and instead stay with the specific, irreducible ways in which it pushes back. This is where the shift from reflection to diffraction lives: not mirroring the object back through a pre-given frame, but registering the interference pattern produced when the object's specific qualities meet the apparatus of attention.

The third technique is to exercise what Adorno calls the utopian imagination — to imaginatively recreate what conceptualization has suppressed, to recover "the possibility of which reality has cheated the objects and which is nonetheless visible in each one" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 16). The task is to take note of the invisible field that surrounds and infuses the world of objects and to return those denied possibilities to view.

The fourth technique is to admit a playful element into inquiry — to be willing, as Adorno puts it, to adhere to one's "clownish traits." The clown is both humble and brave: willing to present reality in configurations that others might read as nonsense, willing to be wrong in public, willing to loosen the prestige of mastery as a posture. This is the courage that queering, making-strange, and worrying-the-concept all require. Together, Bennett summarizes, the four techniques constitute "the self-criticism of conceptualization, a sensory attentiveness to the qualitative singularities of the object, the exercise of an unrealistic imagination, and the courage of a clown" — a practice designed to replace the rage against nonidentity with a respect for it, a respect that chastens the will to mastery and can transmute the anguish of nonidentity into ameliorative political action (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 16).

The ethical consequence of these techniques is what Bennett calls lingering. Vital materialists "try to linger in those moments during which they find themselves fascinated by objects, taking them as clues to the material vitality that they share with them. This sense of a strange and incomplete commonality with the out-side may induce vital materialists to treat nonhumans — animals, plants, earth, even artifacts and commodities — more carefully, more strategically, more ecologically" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 18). Lingering is not passivity or delay. It is the active refusal to rush past an encounter before the thing has had time to register its own pressure on the inquiry. It is holding space for the object — deferring the imposition of higher-order meaning long enough for the assemblage to disclose what it is doing. The political stakes are direct: a diffractive practice that lingers is one that keeps noticing distributed agency rather than quarantining it inside the human, that resists the moralized politics of binary good and evil by staying with the complexity of confederate causation.

Guattari widens this from a practice of individual perceptual retraining to a structural claim about the conditions under which ecological thinking becomes possible at all. His argument in *The Three Ecologies* is that the problem we face is not simply environmental decay but a disease afflicting all three "ecological registers" simultaneously: the environmental, the social, and the mental (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 114). These are not discrete domains. They are, as Guattari insists, "interchangeable lenses or points of view" that together form a single whole — and any attempt to address one while leaving the others intact will not reach the depth required. The greening of the economy, the redistribution of wealth, the extension of rights: none of these can develop beyond the superficial without new modules of subjectification, new "micropolitical and microsocial practices, new solidarities, a new gentleness, together with new aesthetic and new analytic practices regarding the formation of the unconscious" (Guattari, qtd. in *Vibrant Matter*, p. 114). Guattari calls this thinking transversally: fixing the mind's eye on the interlacing of the mechanosphere, the social sphere, and the inwardness of subjectivity simultaneously, because these three worlds form a single whole.

Diffractive practice is the cultivation of that traversal style. It is not a method applied to a pre-given object. It is a trained orientation — perceptual, intellectual, aesthetic, and political at once — adequate to a world where the environmental, the social, and the mental are always already intra-acting, and where the source of effects is never a single agent but an assemblage whose distributed agency calls for a distributed response.

### Protocols Under the Umbrella

The thinker-by-thinker account above describes how each major theorist has conceptualized diffractive practice at the level of orientation and commitment. What the practice looks like when operationalized as a protocol — when a researcher actually tries to work diffractively — has been developed by a range of practitioners working across fields, each arriving at similar structures through different routes.

Lambert (submitted) develops five conceptual entry points for diffractive practice in teacher education: affect, intra-action, becoming-with, assemblage, and spacetimemattering. Each is a conceptual tool derived from posthumanist inquiry that reorients the practitioner away from the humanist, linear, product-oriented logic of reflective practice and toward an "otherwise" practice adequate to the complex, relational, non-linear nature of teaching encounters. Crucially, Lambert does not offer these as a protocol to replace reflection and explicitly avoids a recipe for implementation. They are entry points — ways of beginning to think differently, not steps to follow. Diffraction in Lambert's framing is an orientation, not a procedure: "not an 'either/or' alternative but an 'otherwise', expanded way of thinking" (Lambert, submitted, p. 3). This is consistent with the broader literature: every serious account of diffractive practice warns against treating it as a recipe precisely because a recipe reinstalls the linear, product-oriented, closure-seeking logic the practice is designed to resist.

Hill (2017) makes the most useful structural distinction in the literature for practitioners: the difference between **diffractive reading** and **experimenting with a diffractive apparatus**. Diffractive reading occurs outside the event — it is a way of analyzing or interpreting material after the fact, reading texts, data, or experiences through one another to trace the interference patterns they produce. It is, Hill argues, analogous to Schön's reflection-on-action: retrospective, outside the immediate encounter, producing new understandings that may then inform practice. Experimenting with a diffractive apparatus occurs in situ — it is the practitioner's intra-action with other bodies during an educative or research event, contributing to particular configurations of reality as they unfold. This is analogous to reflection-in-action: embedded in the encounter, unfolding in real time, attending to what intra-action is producing rather than representing what was produced. Both are material-discursive practices that configure the world in particular ways; neither is merely interpretive. The distinction matters because it maps onto different modes of being a researcher: one retrospective and analytical, one embedded and generative. Hill identifies three modes of becoming a diffractive practitioner: becoming-with the world, displacing and diffracting the selves who teach, and embracing difference and interference (Hill, 2017, pp. 6–14). These three are not sequential steps but orientations that interpenetrate — each requires the others, and all three together constitute what it means to shift from a reflective to a diffractive stance.

Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025), working in organizational studies, operationalize Braidotti's transpositions as six practices: embracing alternative ways of seeing and understanding the world, assessing and critiquing imaginary forms, preparing to make the creative leap, accounting for locations and positioning, storying the in-between space, and developing new frames of resonance. As noted in the thinker-by-thinker account above, these six practices are the authors' own derivation from Braidotti's oeuvre — they are not steps Braidotti herself prescribes. This is an important methodological point: scholars build protocols from theoretical frameworks all the time, and the test of such a protocol is not whether the theorist endorsed it but whether it remains accountable to the theoretical commitments of the framework it derives from. Martin et al.'s practices do: each is grounded in Braidotti's relational ontology of becoming, her critique of imaginary formations, her nomadic subject position, and her affirmative ethical orientation. The protocol is an operationalization, not a transcription.

The Mosaic Approach (Clark and Moss, 2001), developed for listening research with young children, represents a different kind of case: a protocol that satisfies the umbrella criteria without using NM vocabulary and without having been derived from NM theory. It predates Barad's *Meeting the Universe Halfway* by six years and developed independently through three roots — participatory rural development, Reggio Emilia's pedagogy of listening and relationships, and a rights-based view of young children as experts in their own lives. Yet it enacts the same ontological commitments: knowledge is co-constructed between researcher and participant through multiple simultaneous modes; no single method or perspective has priority; the non-verbal, embodied, and material are data alongside the verbal; the research is ongoing and open-ended rather than closure-seeking; and the researcher is a visible part of the process rather than a neutral observer. Dudley (2024) brings the Mosaic Approach explicitly into contact with Braidotti's nomadic ethics and Barad's ethico-onto-epistemology, using the Mosaic tiles as a material-discursive apparatus and nomadic ethics as the accountability framework. That convergence is possible precisely because the Mosaic Approach was already doing diffractive practice before the label existed. This is the same pattern the document has traced elsewhere: practice arrives at the ground that theory then names. The Mosaic Approach is another instance of the argument that what posthumanism and new materialism discovered was not new — what they did was finally refuse to suppress it.

The convergence of these protocols under the umbrella — Barad's apparatus work, Haraway's figuration, Braidotti's transpositions, Bennett's perceptual retraining, Lambert's five entry points, Hill's two modes, Martin et al.'s six practices, Clark and Moss's mosaic listening — is not accidental. It is the signature of a shared ontological ground. Each arrived at similar structures from different domains, different vocabularies, and different institutional contexts. What they share is the refusal to treat knowledge as extraction from a pre-given world, and the insistence that accountability is located in the specific, specifiable apparatus of the inquiry rather than in correspondence to an external canon of truth. Research does not need to be checked against some outside verifiable standard. It needs internal consistency — coherence with its own ontological commitments and accountability to the apparatus, the cuts, and the marks it leaves on bodies (Militsi, 2021; Timeto, 2011).

## Part Three: New Materialist Art Practice as Research (Practicing Diffraction)

#### Key Nodes/Ponderings from which to Build a New Materialist Art Practice

This section answers the third question: how have new materialist
theorists conceptualized art practice as research? Parts One and Two
established the ontological and methodological ground --- a relational
world that resists representationalism, and a set of diffractive
practices for inhabiting it. Part Three asks what it means to work as an
artist-researcher within that ground: what the studio and the lab become
as research apparatuses, what kinds of knowledge making is a practice
can produce, and how the specific operators of diffraction,
transposition, intra-action, and seeding function when the medium is not
text or data but matter, encounter, and making.

Language/representationalism has too much power. How can we claim to
understand a thing better through its abstraction/its signifier, than
through experience with the thing itself?

If the world is in a continuous state of becoming, then any objective
knowledge we can hope to claim is only knowledge within an open ended
field. Knowledge is
phenomenological/participatory/open-ended/emergent/entangled/generative.

What grounds this claim is not speculation but ontology. Matter itself,
as Barad insists, is not passive or awaiting signification from an
external source: it is "always already an ongoing historicity" (Barad,
2003, p. 822). It does not require the mark of culture or history to
complete it. The implication for art practice as research is immediate:
if matter is already agential and already historical, then working with
matter --- attending to it, making with it, being rearranged by it ---
is already a form of knowing. The artist-researcher does not impose
meaning on inert material. She enters into an intra-action with matter
that was already in motion.

### What is Research?

Research is an assemblage made of tools, recording and analysis
technologies, software and hardware, theoretical frameworks,
literatures, researchers, editors, ethics committees, end users, and the
data outputs---where outputs "derive from the multiple affects between
these human and non-human materialities."

(Fox & Alldred, 2021)

Under an agential realist account, this definition of research gains
additional precision. Phenomena --- the basic units of reality for Barad
--- "are not the mere result of laboratory exercises engineered by human
subjects. Nor can the apparatuses that produce phenomena be understood
as observational devices or mere laboratory instruments" (Barad, 2003,
p. 815). Research is not the neutral observation of pre-existing objects
but the co-constitution of phenomena through specific
material-discursive arrangements. The apparatus is not separate from
what it produces; it is part of the phenomenon itself. This means that
the decisions a researcher makes --- what apparatus to build, what cut
to enact, what to attend to and what to exclude --- are not
methodological conveniences but ontological acts. Research, on this
account, is world-making.

Agency, correspondingly, is not something the researcher possesses and
applies. "Agency is a matter of intra-acting; it is an enactment, not
something that someone or something has" (Barad, 2003, p. 826). It is
"about the possibilities and accountability entailed in reconfiguring
material-discursive apparatuses of bodily production including the
boundary articulations and exclusions that are marked by those practices
in the enactment of a causal structure." Research as assemblage --- the
Fox & Alldred formulation --- and research as agential intra-action ---
the Baradian formulation --- are naming the same condition from
different angles: the researcher is inside the phenomenon, not above it.

Rheinberger frames research as an "epistemic encounter" within
experimental systems where the object of inquiry is not fully known in
advance --- what he calls "epistemic things" --- and where research
trajectories are non-teleological: driven from behind rather than
oriented toward a predetermined omega (Rheinberger, Raw Flows, p. 17).
Studios and laboratories are, in this account, comparable working
environments --- not identical, but analogous in their capacity to allow
the fruitful interaction between the known and the not-yet-known that
characterises research proper. What becomes knowable is not independent
of the material arrangements that make inquiry possible; it depends
entirely on how instruments transform matter to render phenomena
accessible. In X-ray crystallography, fluid biological material must be
made crystalline to enter the research regime. In electron microscopy,
vacuum conditions force liquids to evaporate and require elaborate
preparation that produces replicas rather than originals. The
boundary-work --- wet/dry, hard/soft, living/nonliving --- is not
incidental to the research; it is where the research happens. For new
materialist art practice as research, the implication is immediate: the
studio is not a private expressive room waiting to be supplemented by
scholarly commentary. It is an experimental system that materially
conditions what can appear, be handled, and be known.

Moñivas Mayor pushes this apparatus logic into explicitly studio-based
terrain by arguing that when the object of inquiry is fluid processes
and fluid imaginations, method must itself become fluid. The studio/lab,
in her account, is an ecology: a permeable environment of maps, notes,
vessels, instruments, and experiments always in readiness, where the
methodological inventory includes testing observation points,
researching theoretical sources, experimenting with documentation
formats (field notebook, sketch, photo, video), and long durations of
waiting, meals, and walks that contextualize what gets produced (Moñivas
Mayor, Raw Flows, p. 112). Her key methodological claim is that fluid
rhythms and unpredictability do not merely complicate research --- they
reshape how practice must be conceptualized and how it must be analyzed.
She draws on the Deborah number to show why conventional observation
regimes produce "solids" out of processes: normal timeframes make a
solid of any substance, which forces a rethinking of object-centered
epistemologies (Moñivas Mayor, Raw Flows, pp. 112--114). The studio/lab
becomes the place where this crisis is lived as a research condition ---
learning to look and listen from the blurry margins of rationality
rather than resolving it through abstraction. Crucially, she treats
accidents as epistemic events: a spill produces an unanticipated form
that becomes a new priority and redirects the inquiry. Rather than
eliminating such noise, she argues for methodological distance from
exactness to admit surprise, play, and reflection, insisting that
analysis must combine documentation with tool and material knowledge and
imagination (Moñivas Mayor, Raw Flows, p. 122). Her culminating
formulation names the whole laboratory as the epistemic medium itself: a
"condenser of flows and mutations" and a "catalyst of imagination,
experimentation and knowledge." The studio/lab is not where research
happens and is then written up. It is the apparatus through which
research happens at all.

### What is Art Practice as Research?

Kirkkopelto (2015) provides the most precise account of what distinguishes artistic research from art practice. The distinguishing feature is not medium, not institutional location, and not the presence of a written reflection — it is the conversion of the artistic medium into a medium of research. When that conversion happens, the artist becomes "the primary expert of the medium that she herself has created" (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49), responsible not only for the work but for making its consequences traceable and communicable. A medium is not only a path or method but also the material and technical ground on which that path is traced: a place for placing and a happening. As an artistic medium changes into a medium of research, it moves toward the unknown while remaining unable to fully accomplish the transition it communicates — "it remains suspended between two stages; it can only suggest the transition by repeating it endlessly but always anew" (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49). This conditionality is not a failure. It is what makes research in this mode distinct: always oriented toward something it cannot yet fully articulate.

Transposition is the name Kirkkopelto gives to the logic of this shift, drawing on two sources. From music, transposition indicates variation and displacement that produces new harmonics without abandoning the original structure — nomadic yet accountable. From genetics, it draws on Barbara McClintock's work on transposable elements: the organism is the host environment of the genetic sequence and plays an interactive, determining role in what gets transmitted. Together these sources explain why transposition carries both nomadic drift and material embodiment. It is not merely conceptual movement but a materially situated operation in which the host environment — the organism, the medium, the artist — shapes the very information being transposed. As Braidotti formulates it, transposition is "a qualitative leap that produces a materially embedded in-between space" (Braidotti, 2006, p. 26). Haraway makes the genetic logic explicit in a passage Braidotti cites directly: "A gene is not a thing, much less a master molecule, or a self-contained code. Instead, the term 'gene' signifies a mode of durable action where many actors, human and non-human, meet" (Haraway, 1997, p. 142, cited in Braidotti, 2006, p. 27). Neither medium nor artist pre-exists the transposition as a stable entity. Both emerge transformed. Both act as apparatus.

Transposition makes research necessary because consequences are not guaranteed in advance. When a procedure, device, or concept shifts fields, it enters what Kirkkopelto calls an "afterlife" whose outcomes are unpredictable and must be investigated rather than assumed (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49). This is what distinguishes artistic research from artistic practice: not the institutional address, but the commitment to making the transposition's consequences traceable. The result must establish itself discursively in relation to existing practices and account for how it changes the state of affairs — specifically, whether it achieves invention rather than mere innovation. Kirkkopelto's criterion for evaluation is: to what extent can the outcome take into account and reclaim its own instituting aspects and responsibility for them?

The artist-researcher, on this account, is herself a transponent — a hybrid who does not surrender to either side of the transposition and does not fuse them. She makes latent/virtual affinities appear in the surrounding field that were previously unactualized, through the structure of the transposition itself rather than through special authority. The medium and the artist are both apparatus. Neither is sovereign. What they produce together — the diffraction pattern of their encounter — is the research. Schwab names the risk: if the transposition works, new understanding will be gained; if it fails, the outcome is forced acting, incoherent scripts, and pointless monologues. In the absence of deeper historical grounding, there is no other choice but to take this risk (Schwab, in *Transpositions*, 2018). The artist-researcher does not apply an established method to a question. She stages the conditions under which the question and its possible answers become thinkable.

Kirkkopelto's criterion names a standard that practitioner-researchers were meeting before the institutional framework for artistic research existed. Trinh T. Minh-ha is the clearest example. Working as a filmmaker, composer, writer, and theorist from the 1980s onward, Trinh produced work that refuses every available disciplinary container while being rigorously accountable to the conditions of its own production. Anthropologists, film studies scholars, postcolonial theorists, and feminist theorists all claimed her; she refused all of them as complete containers without abandoning any as partial resources. "Anthropology is just one site of discussion among others in my work," she says (Trinh, 1992, p. 83) — what matters is not the objects but the relationships drawn between them.

In "Documentary Is/Not a Name" (1990), Trinh names the governing fantasy of documentary filmmaking — that the camera gives direct access to reality, that truth can be caught without the mediating activity of the apparatus — as the Cartesian division between subject and object that perpetuates "a dualistic inside-versus-outside, mind-against-matter view of the world" (Trinh, 1990, p. 83). Against this she insists on the interval: "what persists between the meaning of something and its truth is the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). The methodology she develops from this commitment is speaking nearby rather than about — a speaking that can come very close to a subject "without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, p. 87). It is a practice of partial, situated, non-exhaustive knowledge, developed in film and writing prior to and independent of the NM/PH vocabulary that would later name it. Speaking nearby is not technique. It is an attitude: a way of positioning oneself in relation to the world that must be materialized in every element of the work — verbal, musical, visual.

Trinh's position in this document is specific. She is not a theorist who discusses art practice as research. She is an artist-researcher who was practicing the refusal of exhaustive capture, the interval, the accountability of the apparatus, and the responsibility of the instituting gesture — all before those terms entered academic circulation. The citational chain is now traceable: Trinh articulates the "critical difference within" and the practice of speaking nearby in the late 1980s and early 1990s; Haraway (1992) quotes Trinh directly and develops the diffraction metaphor from that encounter; Barad (2003) quotes Haraway (1992) and grounds the metaphor in quantum field theory as an ontological claim. What began as a filmmaker's methodology becomes a feminist theoretical metaphor and then a physical ontology. The chain runs backward from theory to practice. Restoring Trinh to the genealogy of APAR changes what the question is — from how artistic activity meets academic standards of knowledge-production, to how practices that were already producing knowledge get recognized as doing so by the institutions that initially excluded them.

### What is New Materialist Art Practice As Research?

A new materialist art practice as research is a generative process of
co-creating with material a diffraction grating that produces patterns
that would not appear through other methods/frames. The boundaries are
the consequences of the grating's design, providing a tool for research
accountability for the "constitutive exclusions."(Barad,
2003_Posthumanist) Each decision is a cut that together produces a
locally determinate diffractive pattern.

The insistence on "co-creating with material" is not metaphor. It is an
ontological commitment grounded in what Barad calls the mutual
entailment of the material and the discursive: "The relationship between
the material and the discursive is one of mutual entailment. Neither is
articulated/articulable in the absence of the other; matter and meaning
are mutually articulated. Neither discursive practices nor material
phenomena are ontologically or epistemologically prior. Neither can be
explained in terms of the other. Neither has privileged status in
determining the other" (Barad, 2003, p. 822). For new materialist art
practice as research, this means that the material the artist works with
is not a passive medium awaiting the inscription of the artist's idea.
It is a co-constitutive force whose specific resistances, affordances,
textures, and behaviors shape what becomes thinkable and sayable through
the encounter. The diffraction grating is not designed and then
executed. It emerges through the intra-action.

The Enchantment of Textiles gives a full-scale case study of this
apparatus logic in an interdisciplinary lab setting. It begins with
museum research into historic embroidered metalwork --- materials,
techniques, cultural meanings --- and then translates those histories
into responsive textile systems using sensors, microcontrollers, textile
antennas, and embedded circuits. The catalog explicitly frames the lab
as a collaborative assemblage of artists, engineers, and researchers,
with textiles as performative tools rather than passive media. Agency is
distributed explicitly: viewers can "play the sorcerer," but quickly
discover that power is in fact distributed and accumulates only through
the specific arrangement of surrounding objects, citing Bennett's
heterogeneous assemblage claim directly. The textiles are not merely
objects to be looked at; they "solicit human perception" while also
"perceiv\[ing\] and interact\[ing\] with one another," broadcasting and
receiving via antennas and responsive components. Research-creation of
this kind, the catalog insists, "cannot be deemed successful in simple
or finite terms" and does not yield a singular discovery but instead
"opens up new fields of understanding" --- a research outcome to be
assessed through unfolding interactions and capacities rather than
through closure. The "Critical Methodologies" section of the same
catalog makes the research method explicit: sensory ethnography as an
experimental iterative-inductive process for studio/lab environments,
producing knowledge through embodied sensory engagement and explicitly
refusing universality. Neither human nor material agency is privileged;
what matters is the temporally emergent influence of ongoing practice.
"Tuning" becomes the descriptor for shared adjustment across the
research team and the researcher's active role in learning --- goals
redefined, decisions shared, embodiments of practice emerging in real
time through sensory engagement with material systems.

SymbioticA provides a different inflection of the same apparatus logic,
one in which the institutional entanglements of lab-based art practice
are not incidental to the work but constitute it. SymbioticA is
described as an artistic research laboratory inside a university, where
artists work in scientific laboratories with scientific techniques and
staff. What counts as the "work" in a SymbioticA project is not only the
material outcome but the processes that make it possible: securing
permissions from ethics committees, negotiating agreements with lab
managers, arranging ongoing care responsibilities. The work consists,
then, of these processes --- and it produces a difference to science
precisely by following scientific methods while staging that difference
as the research contribution. This is a strong formulation of new
materialist ethics as infrastructure: responsibility is not a moral
sentiment attached to the research after the fact, but a network of
material practices --- protocols, caretaking, institutional permissions,
risk exposure --- that condition what research can occur and what lives
are involved. The political context is not bracketed: the vulnerability
of biology-based art practice to state intervention (the Kurtz case) and
the framing of contemporary biotechnology institutions as a "society of
control" are explicit stakes of the work.

Transposition names the recomposing side of this: not merely reading or seeing patterns, but building the conditions that make new patterns possible. Transposition is the logic of a shift itself — what is shifted becomes a transponent, a hybrid agent of change whose communication is transformative, actualizing latent/virtual affinities and transforming the field it enters. Diffraction and transposition together constitute the full research movement: diffraction traces the interference patterns the apparatus produces; transposition stages the conditions that make new patterns possible. Neither can do the other's work.

Diffraction as a research method creates rigor through the ongoing
acknowledgement of the agential cuts. Diffractive rigor means: I name
the apparatus, name the cut, and describe what the pattern makes visible
and what it necessarily renders quiet.

Art practice becomes research through the creation of an apparatus that
materially conditions what becomes perceptible as a
product/artifact/data. Apparatuses are "exclusionary practices of
mattering through which intelligibility and materiality are
constituted."

(Barad,2003,Post)The apparatus does not provide representational
objectivity, but a boundary space whose cuts can be specified, tuned,
compared, and held accountable.

Artistic research develops through operations, not representation — practice-as-research is not illustration but epistemic movement generated by staged operations and their shifts. Transposition, in the precise definition that runs through Braidotti and Kirkkopelto, is a creative leap that opens an in-between space — zigzagging, crossing — where difference is not reduced to identity. It operates through a transponent whose communication is transformative: actualizing latent/virtual affinities, transforming the field it enters. The apparatus does not provide representational objectivity but a boundary space whose cuts can be specified, tuned, compared, and held accountable.

#### Case Study 1: Kristina Andersen

Kristina Andersen describes her practice with unusual directness: "I
oscillate between reading and making. Often the objects I make can be
seen as diagrams of ideas, of manifestations of the relationships
between one notion and another" (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). This opening
statement contains, in compressed form, the onto-epistemological stance
that new materialism theorizes more elaborately: knowledge is not
separable from the material forms through which it is worked out, and
the relationship between one notion and another becomes thinkable only
by being given body. The making and the reading are parallel, mutually
disturbing processes --- not sequential stages of research.

The objects she makes are "magic machines": hypothetical, non-functional
props made in short concentrated bursts, guided by desire and fear
rather than skill and technological possibility, built "to give body to
dim concerns and questions, to consider the potential reality of a world
in which such a machine might exist, and in a sense to rehearse how to
live in such a world" (Andersen, 2016, p. 44). The phrase "dim concerns"
is the key. The machine is not built to illustrate a concept already
formed. It is built because the concern has not yet achieved conceptual
clarity --- because the only way to find out what it is, is to make
something from it. As Valente puts it in a passage Andersen cites:
"First, you build the machine, then it tells you what it's for"
(Valente, 2013, cited in Andersen, 2016, p. 44). The machine speaks
back. It has its own rules and possibilities, not fully determined by
the maker's intention. This is Bennett's thing-power enacted at bench
scale: the object is not inert matter awaiting inscription but a
participant in producing the knowledge the encounter generates.

In the thesis, Andersen theorizes this dynamic through "leaky objects."
The building process is "a dialogue between the intentions of the maker
and the limitations of the material, and the object itself. Each small
modification opens up new possibilities for the ongoing building
process" (Andersen, 2012, p. 87). Workshop outcomes are "leaky" because
they absorb and leak knowledge and complexity as they appear. In
presentation and discussion, "a clear multi-directional connection
appears between the words, the material, the model and the maker" --- a
bringing-forth that Andersen calls "an almost poiesis-like process of
calling something into existence that was not there before" (Andersen,
2012, p. 87). The object does not receive the maker's meaning; it
participates in producing it. The machines built in the workshops must
express themselves through their makers because they do not actually
work. As a result they "leak knowledge, power, context" --- they are
handled roughly by the process, and what they become is not what was
planned but what emerged through the encounter.

Andersen borrows the term "marking" from dance rehearsals to describe
what building these non-functional machines does. In dance, marking is
when a phrase takes a simplified, schematic form --- body-in-motion
representing an aspect of the full-out phrase without performing it
completely. Building a speculative instrument is marking the physical
space a future machine will take up, "sketching the gestures and
movements required to operate and play this particular device." When it
comes to describing ideas later, "we have the object itself to guide us.
The tactile and structural possibilities of the non-functional object
lets us investigate it in a physical manner; it is suddenly possible to
speak confidently as to how this device will operate, because it allows
us to anchor any conceptual or metaphorical notions in an object that
pushes back into our hands: It is already right there" (Andersen, 2016,
p. 46). The knowledge is in the handling. The object is not illustrating
the argument; it is making the argument available.

When conducted as workshops --- at STEIM and elsewhere --- the process
is designed so participants draw a sound in charcoal, then build the
instrument capable of making that sound from available materials,
without discussion, working fast, with the constraint that the object
must be non-functional. The workshop "temporarily frees us from the
practical and technical limitations of our work" and the performative
moment where participants play and explain their instruments is "a live
volatile process" in the sense of Dewey's art as experience and Cage's
commitment to creating opportunities to listen (Andersen, 2016, p. 45).
In that moment, "the experience between the performer, the new
instrument, and the audience can be perceived as an almost physical
thing which can be examined, turned, changed, and at some point is
'done' as the performance moment lifts" (Andersen, 2016, p. 45). This is
Barad's fog chamber made workshop-sized: a performative apparatus
through which boundaries are drawn, phenomena come into being, and the
encounter produces knowledge that belongs to the situation rather than
to any individual knower.

Andersen's account also illuminates what this mode of knowing costs
within traditional academic frameworks. She taught herself to write
academically when she needed to. "Now, when asked to write subjectively,
I find that I write like a child" (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). This is not
self-deprecation. It is a structural account of what happens when a mode
of knowledge production that operates through material immersion and
leaky objects is required to re-enter itself through the protocols of
academic prose. The academic writing does not translate the practice; it
overwrites the voice the practice developed. What Andersen describes as
"writing like a child" names the cost of forcing poiesis through the
grammar of representational research --- which is precisely why the
Violence of Traditional Methods section uses her account directly. The
violence is not abstract. It lands on specific practitioners in specific
ways.

#### Case Study 2: Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz --- Interferences of the Multitude

Where Andersen's case study traces what new materialist art practice as
research looks like at the level of individual studio process, Torosyan,
Reis, and Wuschitz's "Interferences of the Multitude" (Politics of the
Machines, 2021) demonstrates what it looks like as collective,
institutionally situated, politically explicit research practice. The
paper introduces a track of work presented at the Rogue Research edition
of the 3rd Politics of the Machines Conference, Berlin 2021, and is
itself grounded in the authors' collaborative work at Mz\* Baltazar's
Laboratory, a feminist hackerspace in Vienna where they practice
trans-feminist hacking as artistic methodology (Torosyan et al., 2021,
p. 57).

The paper's theoretical framing is explicitly posthumanist and new
materialist, and it names its methods with unusual precision. The two
key operators are interference and diffraction. Interference, in the
paper's usage, is "a critical disruptive process that destabilises the
status quo and plays an important role in relational and emancipatory
thinking and action." Diffraction, they argue, "came to replace the
reflexive methodologies of thinking and doing" and together these two
notions "are foundational for the new materialist turn and feminist
ethics of care" (Torosyan et al., 2021, pp. 55--56). The wave physics
here is not decorative: interference patterns form when different points
of a wavefront reach a surface at different intensities. The most
complex knowledge patterns, the paper implies, emerge from exactly this
kind of collision --- not from a single unified perspective but from the
intra-action of multiple, differently positioned, differently charged
perspectives meeting in the same space.

Alongside diffraction, the authors explicitly operationalize Barad's
intra-action as a response to the inadequacy of "interaction," which
presupposes the pre-givenness of bodies that then encounter each other.
Intra-action, by contrast, "suggests that agency is not an inherent
property of an individual body to be exercised but a mutual influence
and co-emergence of forces" (Barad 2007, p. 141, cited in Torosyan et
al., 2021, pp. 55--56). This is also framed as a response to what
Haraway calls "the God's trick" --- the objectivist framing that
presents outcomes or measurements as purely neutral. Feminist new
materialist ethics, as Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz articulate it, is
rooted "in the critical post-human rethinking of the Eurocentric notions
of 'Human'" and extends the cyberfeminist critique of constructivism to
address "the material, gendered, and embodied challenges of existence in
the augmented world" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 56).

What makes this a useful case study for new materialist APAR is the
range of practices gathered under its frame. The track included: a
multiplayer game in which the avatar can only be navigated by multiple
players who coordinate collectively (Renfro); a computational
performance in which a performer embodies a tomato to defamiliarize
colonial and anthropocentric scientific thinking about plant life
(Pereira and Bruggave); an artist residency on a floating barge in
Poland structured around feminist hydro-commons and degrowth
(Walentynowicz and Majewska-Güde); a trans-feminist dance performance
using a vaginal muscle reader to control sound (Varela); and a paper on
open-source feminist sextech that tracks menstruation without binary or
patronizing categories of sex and gender (Dietze and Stewart) (Torosyan
et al., 2021, pp. 57--60). Across this range, the common thread is not
medium, not discipline, and not institutional form. It is the
onto-epistemological stance: matter is active, agency is distributed,
knowledge emerges from intra-action rather than from observation of a
pre-given world, and the research apparatus includes the researcher's
own positionality as a constitutive element rather than a bias to be
minimized.

The paper's conclusion is explicit about what constitutes knowledge in
this mode: "Most of the panellists are both artists and scholars. Their
methodology of knowledge production is a deep conversation between
theory and practice, and productive oscillation between the two." The
intervention --- whether a game, a coding manual, an installation, or a
mining community workshop --- "becomes fertile ground for new empathetic
technologies --- technologies that facilitate a conversation on a
specific research question." The technology is generated according to
the critical investigation, then applied in an experimental setting, and
"in conversation with this technology a process of co-becoming,
'becoming with' (Haraway 2013), was initiated, grounded in collaboration
with concerned communities" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 60). This is
Franinović's seeding logic applied at political and ecological scale:
designing conditions in which matter, bodies, and communities can
activate each other across the boundary between art, science, and
collective action. The contextualization and theorizing of the
intervention's impact is accomplished through writing --- but writing
that follows practice, not one that precedes it.

The political framing of the track adds a dimension not yet named. Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz ground their work in a neo-Spinozist notion of multitude — drawn from Negri and Hardt (2000, 2005) — as the agent of potentia: the collective, distributed power-to-act as distinct from potestas, the power-over that characterizes sovereign and institutional forms of authority. The return to Spinoza's monism and the idea of multitude as the agent(s) of potentia, they note, is "resonant with the indigenous political thinking" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 55), connecting the philosophical lineage to non-Western traditions of collective agency that the document has elsewhere traced through its own genealogy. Trans-feminist hacking is the methodological form this takes in practice: a research-based artistic framework that applies diffractive practice to "bridge this gap in thinking of and doing with difference," imagining "post-human conviviality through mutual care, co-dependence, and solidarity" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 57). This is not diffractive practice as an individual researcher's epistemological stance. It is diffractive practice as collective political methodology — the interference pattern produced when multiple differently positioned, differently charged perspectives meet in the same space, generating the most complex knowledge patterns precisely through that collision. The case study confirms that new materialist art practice as research can operate at the scale of community, institution, and political ecology, not only at the scale of the individual studio.

#### Case Study 3: Brian Eno and David Byrne --- My Life in the Bush of Ghosts

Cary Wolfe reads Brian Eno and David Byrne's 1981 record *My Life in the
Bush of Ghosts* as a case study in posthumanist practice, and the
reading illuminates something that the theoretical literature tends to
state abstractly: that posthumanist ontology is not a set of
propositions one holds but a mode of working that enacts different
assumptions about agency, authorship, materiality, and knowledge. The
record is worth dwelling on at some length precisely because it makes
legible --- in the form of artistic decisions, technical accidents, and
explicit reflections from the artists themselves --- the same structure
that new materialism theorizes (Wolfe, 2010, pp. 283--299).

The record's central method is the found vocal: recordings drawn from
radio sermons, Lebanese mountain singers, evangelical preachers,
exorcism sessions --- source materials that Byrne and Eno layered over
rhythm tracks in a process they describe as close to performance, with
no samplers available and no digital correction. "Throwing the vocals
against the tracks was in our case almost a performance," Byrne writes;
serendipity determined which takes held together, and the analog
interplay of two tape machines running simultaneously --- not
algorithmic matching --- was the operative apparatus (Wolfe, 2010, p.
289). This is not a marginal technical detail. It is the ontological
condition of the work. Eno's account of his video work from the same
period makes the same point from a different angle: working with a
camera whose tube had been burned out by overexposure to sky, he found
that the damaged instrument "responded to light and colour in a way no
other camera did." The malfunction was the method. The broken equipment
produced what the functioning equipment could not. What Gregory Bateson
calls "real magnitudes" --- the specific, embodied, situational
interplay of material forces including weather, proximity, decay, and
contingency --- were not noise to be eliminated but the generative
condition of the work (Wolfe, 2010, p. 292). Shannon's suppressed
insight --- that noise carries the most information --- is here enacted
rather than theorized.

Agency in this project is distributed across found voices, broken
cameras, serendipitous tape alignments, the weather, and the bodies of
singers recorded decades earlier in contexts entirely removed from the
record's production. Byrne and Eno are not the sovereign authors of this
work in any conventional sense. They are, in Kirkkopelto's precise
formulation, transponents: hybrid agents of change whose communication
is not informative but transformative, who do not bring messages between
fields but make latent virtual affinities appear that were previously
unactualized. The vocal source material is not raw material awaiting the
artists' meaning --- it is already fully formed, already exceeding any
framework either artist brings to it. What the artists do is stage
conditions: a specific rhythm track, a specific room, a specific tape
machine, a specific moment of alignment. The knowledge produced is the
diffraction pattern of that encounter, not the expression of a prior
intention.

Wolfe frames the record's resistance to digital rendering through
Derrida and Stiegler. For Stiegler, digital media depend on a
"systematic discretization" and "grammaticalization" of content --- the
same extractive logic that stripped Leibniz's binary notation from its
relational context, that suppressed Shannon's noise, that domesticated
Bohr's entanglement into the Copenhagen interpretation (Wolfe, 2010, p.
286). The analog is spectral in relation to this regime precisely
because it resists complete rendering: it depends on the interplay of
real magnitudes in space-time, and that interplay is "in principle
bottomless" --- a reservoir of complexity and contingency that no
program or schema can fully subsume (Wolfe, 2010, p. 292). The voice on
*Bush of Ghosts* is spectral in exactly this sense: not because it is
mysterious or exotic, but because its continuity, its grain, its
materiality as analog sound exceeds the diacritical elements a
discretizing analysis can extract from it. As Eno put it: "When people
speak passionately, they speak in melodies." The grammar is present but
what the grammar cannot capture --- what makes the voice the voice ---
is the remainder that escapes discretization.

This connects directly to Massumi's claim that "nothing is more
destructive for the thinking and imaging of the virtual than equating it
with the digital" --- a claim Wolfe reads through *Bush of Ghosts* to
argue that the logic of the virtual is the logic of sampling itself:
"always leaving its source by the time it reaches us, always in the
process of vanishing" (Wolfe, 2010, p. 295). The record does not
thematize this. It enacts it. The appearance of the virtual is in the
twists and folds of formed content, in the movement from one sample to
another. What makes the record feel like it comes from "everywhere and
nowhere, past and future" --- Wolfe's description of its temporal
uncanniness --- is precisely this: each vocal is already a ghost of its
original context, and the analog interplay that makes the record work is
generating new ghosts in real time. The digital is a convention imposed
on this analog reality; the analog was always there underneath,
constituting the very substrate that discretization reads and misreads.

What this case study demonstrates for new materialist art practice as
research is several things simultaneously. First, that the artist as
apparatus --- as transponent, as condition-setter rather than sovereign
author --- is not a theoretical position imposed on artistic practice
from outside but a description of what certain artistic practices have
always been doing. Byrne and Eno did not know Barad or Bennett; they
were working from musical and artistic intuitions that enacted the same
distributed-agency, material-contingency ontology. Second, that the
posthumanist refusal of representationalism --- the refusal to treat the
work as the expression of a prior meaning --- is itself a productive
research stance. Byrne's explicit discomfort with the idea that music
should be "an expression of emotion" or that the artist is "someone with
something to say" is not nihilism; it is an opening toward the kind of
knowledge that emerges from the intra-action of materials, chance, and
situated attention. Third, the record's explicit engagement with the
analog/digital distinction --- its resistance to the grammaticalization
and discretization of the voice --- makes the Leibniz/Shannon/Bohr
suppression argument concrete and audible. The human is, as Wolfe
summarizes Derrida and Stiegler, "a prosthetic being, who from day one
is constituted as human by its coevolution with and co-constitution by
external archival technologies of various kinds --- including language
itself as the first archive and prosthesis" (Wolfe, 2010, p. 294). The
analog voice, the found recording, the broken camera: these are not
tools the artist uses. They are the apparatus through which the artist,
the work, and the knowledge the work produces all co-emerge.

### Diffractive Practice Operationalized: Hillary and Affirmative Engagement

Diffractive practice as a research method differs from critique in a way that matters for how art practice as research is evaluated. Barad explicitly distinguishes critique as disclosure and demystification from diffractive reading as affirmative engagement. Critique operates in "a mode of disclosure, exposure and demystification," whereas diffractive reading is "a form of affirmative engagement," "an iterative practice of intra-actively reworking and being reworked by patterns of mattering," aimed at making "new patterns of understanding-becoming" (Barad, "Cutting Together-Apart"). Art practice as research shifts the aim of analysis away from unmasking hidden meanings toward noticing how meaning and materiality are co-constituted through arrangements, practices, and constraints. Diffraction is not a synonym for juxtaposition, collage, or eclectic text-mixing; it demands explicit attention to what is produced through particular engagements, not merely the display of multiple perspectives.

Hillary's doctoral research in public art practice demonstrates what this looks like procedurally. She explicitly states that she imbues her research with diffractive practices, drawing on Barad's definition: diffraction is a way of reading insights through one another that shifts away from representationalism "reflecting the world from outside" and toward accounting for "how practices matter." She applies diffraction as a practice method — reading art through site, science, and philosophy — so that knowledge emerges not as a stable interpretation but as a change in relational perception, an intervention in how futures are rehearsed and how publics are composed. Her methodology list is worth citing in full precisely because it shows how this operates as procedure rather than abstract orientation: attuning, collaboration, relational fieldwork, iterative deep listening, site-specific writing, photography, recording and filming, making, returning, thinking-with, journey (Hillary, 2021, p. 29). Research findings are named explicitly as performative utterances — events that prompt a rethinking of relationship to site — with theory developed after "handling," expanded to mean multisensory engagement. Works emerge through repeated everyday interactions with the site, including participation in and observation of intra-actions between human and non-human elements, with "handling" explicitly expanded to touch, sounds, smells, texture, visions, tastes (Hillary, 2021, p. 31). The method is not a detached procedure applied to a preexisting object. It is a repeated practice of attunement and return that reorganizes what counts as knowledge in situ.

### Diffraction/Transpositions/Nexus Theory in 4D

Art Practices Build Apparatus/Transpositions

Britton, Klumbyte, and Draude explicitly cluster their practice around
three hybrid methodologies: creating situations for intra-action,
diffractive reading/making, and material speculation. Their key
methodological claim is that these are not formalized recipes
guaranteeing actionable outputs; rather, the potential of the
methodologies lies in taking methodological openness seriously as a
precondition for transformative results, and in treating "doing
thinking" as an open-ended material practice that must be enacted rather
than merely described (Britton et al., 2019, p. 316). Agency, in their
account, is "not just located in the 'I' but distributed across human
and non-human elements," and is intra-active and emergent --- relations
precede the relata. "Creating situations for intra-action" is given a
practical description: begin from concrete practical issues in a given
area of work and reframe them collaboratively by staging engagements
that allow intra-action across different knowledges and material
constraints. The situation is not incidental to the research; it is what
makes specific relations and therefore specific knowledge possible.
Methodological openness is not vagueness --- it is the condition of
remaining available to what the materials and participants produce
rather than confirming what was assumed in advance.

Transposition and diffraction are not the same operator but they are
siblings: both name patterned effects of relational arrangements rather
than representational mappings. Schwab's Introduction to *Transpositions* offers a precise
formulation of what the transposition operator contributes that
diffraction does not: it is not that research operations can only be
made within artistic research, but that something "artistic" is
increasingly needed in other areas of research to realize complicated
relationships with knowledge objects (Schwab, 2018, pp. 8–9). What
changes when research moves into art is not the operation but its point
of origin: artistic researchers can "jump right in," appropriating or
inventing concepts and supplying their plasticity by artistically
staging them as epistemic things, accepting the risk of failure as part
of knowledge production. Pirrò sharpens the apparatus side of this: data
are facta, produced by the inseparable compound of experiment, research
questions, measuring devices, analysis methods, and researchers'
expectations --- "less a stable result and more a complex compound"
(Pirrò, Transpositions, pp. 144--145). There is no defined common basis,
no comparable set of coordinates, linking a transposition back to its
original material --- so it is not translation into a shared metric, but
a conversion of the conditions of experience itself. Transposing a
dataset means recovering its context and the net of interactions so that
the transposed becomes an active phenomenon emerging from and affording
new relations. Taken together, Britton et al.'s hybrid methodologies,
Schwab's operator logic, and Pirrò's facta argument define new
materialist art practice as research as: experimental operator-work with
real stakes, not illustrative commentary.

Diffraction and transposition are not the same operator but they are two aspects of the same methodological commitment. Diffraction is a tool for deconstruction — it defies representationalism by attending to interference patterns rather than reflections, tracing what a relational arrangement produces rather than what it represents. Transposition is a tool for construction — it is the operator that actualizes latent/virtual affinities in the surrounding field, transforming what enters. Transposition is an act that makes features appear that were previously virtual; actualization necessarily transforms the field it enters. Together they name the two movements that new materialist art practice as research requires: finding the patterns that the apparatus produces (diffractive reading), and building the conditions that make new patterns possible (transposition). Kirkkopelto's transponent — the hybrid agent of change whose communication is transformative rather than informative — is the practitioner who moves between these two moments, carrying the responsibility for both the cut and the leap.

Heidegger's formulation is useful here: technology is not a means but a way of revealing (1953, p. 12, cited in Ferrando, 2014, p. 171). The same is true of art practice as research — the studio, the workshop, the experiment, the performance are not instruments for delivering pre-given knowledge. They are ways of revealing: apparatuses through which phenomena become perceptible that could not have been perceived through other means. The research contribution is not what is found. It is what the apparatus makes findable.

### Making Strange

"Making strange or defamiliarization is the technique of making strange
(or othering). Making strange forces is the act of seeing common things
in an unfamiliar or strange way in order to enhance perception of the
familiar." (Andersen, 2016, p.47)

Making strange moves us past the already always perceiving of objects,
and to linger and notice vibrant matter in its always becoming.

For Andersen, making things stranger creates space to inhabit, space she
claims as personal, as her own. (Possibility space/nexus) (Andersen,
2016, p.47)

The concept has a specific theoretical genealogy that runs through literary theory, theater, and into new materialist art practice. Viktor Shklovsky (1917) introduced ostranenie — "making strange" or defamiliarization — as the defining operation of art: art exists to restore the experience of life, to make us feel things rather than merely recognize them. The technique of art is to make objects "unfamiliar," to make forms difficult, to increase the length and difficulty of perception. What has become automatic and invisible is rendered strange so that it can be seen again. Bertolt Brecht translated this into theatrical practice as the Verfremdungseffekt — the alienation or estrangement effect — a set of techniques for preventing the audience from losing itself in the illusion of the performance, keeping them aware that they are watching a construction, forcing critical distance rather than identification. For Brecht, estrangement is not a failure of art but its most powerful political tool: to make the familiar strange is to reveal that it could be otherwise, that what appears natural and necessary is in fact historical and contingent.

New materialist art practice inherits and extends this tradition but shifts its target. Where Shklovsky and Brecht use making strange primarily to defamiliarize human social relations and cultural forms, new materialist making strange extends the operation to the nonhuman. Pereira and Bruggave's *Becoming a Tomato* — discussed in the Torosyan et al. track — applies defamiliarization to colonial and anthropocentric scientific thinking about plant life, using Latour's notion of blackboxing to render "opaque" the agency of nonhuman matter (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 58). Motivated by Haraway's proposal to rewrite the story of the world, the work incorporates computational art, performance, and implosive writing as a multidisciplinary arts-based research framework. Resonant with the Brechtian Verfremdungseffekt — reversing the roles of nature and history, eternity and causality — the work aims not at social critique but at the disentanglement of bodily knowledge "where the binary borders between human and non-human, self and other, natural and cultural are blurred in a process of co-becoming" (Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 58). Making strange here is not an aesthetic strategy applied after the research is done. It is the research operation itself: defamiliarization of anthropocentric assumptions about nonhuman matter is simultaneously the epistemological intervention and the political one.

This is consistent with Adorno's fourth technique of navigating nonidentity — the playful, clownish willingness to present reality in configurations others might read as nonsense, to admit that the ordinary is not the necessary — and with Bennett's cultivation of enchantment as the perceptual condition for a politics of distributed agency. Schwab's multistable-image logic adds a further dimension: representation can be treated as a breakdown that collapses multiple potentials; artistic research operates in the intertwinedness of knowledge and non-knowledge, emphasizing the "active state" (operations) rather than "result." A transpositional shift produces an encounter that cannot be reduced back into stable representation — no shared coordinates, no common basis — so the defamiliarizing move is an epistemic operator, not "style." Making strange is the practice of holding the world's contingency in view long enough for it to begin to look like the possibility space it actually is.

### What Counts as Knowledge

Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch offer a formulation that makes the
epistemic claims of new materialist art practice as research legible
across disciplines: artistic research is distinctive in the combination
of experimentation, participation, and interpretation, and "reasoning"
is not only in the reflective text but also located in material
practices themselves when cognition is embodied, embedded, and enacted
(Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 4). Unlike laboratory testing oriented
toward commensuration and standardization, the objective of the artistic
experiment is "not so much to test... but to tell something, to convey
content" (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 4). Crucially, they reject the
assumption that the artwork is the research and the written reflection
is merely supplementary; if cognition is embodied and material practices
have epistemic force, then "reasoning is also located in those material
practices" (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 5). This means the studio/lab
process is not pre-research that gets translated into knowledge later.
It is where research operations happen. What counts as evidence,
accordingly, is not confined to propositional statements. The Textile
Enchantment catalog makes this explicit at the level of evaluation:
research-creation "cannot be deemed successful in simple or finite
terms" and does not yield a singular discovery but "opens up new fields
of understanding" --- a research outcome to be assessed through
unfolding interactions and capacities rather than through closure. The
articulation required --- documentation, dissemination, publication ---
is not separate from the research but is "additional work" that makes
the material-discursive labor publicly assessable, interwoven with text
rather than transcribed into it (Borgdorff et al., 2020, p. 5).

Bolt makes the same argument from within painting practice. Her account of working hot reframes artistic making as "act of actual concurrent production" — a dynamic relation among cultural and discursive knowledges, bodies, and materials — where the material practice itself has effects, not just meanings. Creativity is emergent and distributed: the "acting ensemble" includes human and nonhuman actants (paint, canvas, weather, gravity), shifting authorship away from the individual and onto the relations and conditions of making (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], pp. 275–276). Research in this mode produces material effects, traces, capacities, and transformations through situated practice rather than representing a pre-existing reality. It stages intra-actions where knowledge emerges through relations and material constraints — situations, apparatuses, worldings, not just objects.

Cull Ó Maoilearca names what this means for how performance-based APAR researchers understand knowledge: "performance is a form of knowledge that shares its insights through what it does rather than what it means, or better perhaps, that the best way to understand what performance means and why it matters is through attending closely to what it does affectively" (Cull Ó Maoilearca, 2019, p. 156). This formulation holds across all new materialist APAR, not only performance: the work of Andersen's magic machines, Hinterwaldner's marker materials, Franinović's seeding workshops, Hillary's public art practice — each produces knowledge through what it does, not through what it represents. What it represents is secondary and derivative. What it does is constitutive of the phenomenon it studies.

Britton, Klumbyte, and Draude name hybrid methodologies aligned with
this---creating situations for intra-action, diffractive reading/making,
and material speculation---repositioning research as worlding practices
rather than stable outputs.

Pirrò's precision is useful here: transpositions resist stabilization into propositional formulation. Relations and differences remain networks of interacting positions — "incompressible," not reducible to a single stable claim. Transposition produces knowledge as sequence effects and relational shifts, not as propositional snapshots (Schwab's framing: operations, not results). Lomax adds that transposition supplies a method-form for how artistic research can generalize through movement "from the particular to the particular," rather than extracting a rule first. Transpositions cannot be fully seen from outside in representational snapshots; they are grasped from within sequences, operations, and relations. This is why practice-research knowledge is operational and processual — not because it is less rigorous than propositional knowledge, but because the kind of knowing it produces lives in the doing rather than in its residue.

### The Object

For artist and researcher Kristina Andersen, the objects she makes can often "be seen as diagrams of ideas, of manifestations of the relationships between one notion and another" (Andersen, 2016, p. 42). Objects are frequently hypothetical, or magic machines, created to "give body to dim concerns," "to consider the potentiality of a world in which such a machine might exist," and "to rehearse how to live in such a world" (Andersen, 2016, p. 44). They are props — material-discursive arrangements, agential swarms of vibrant matter — objects to think through and with for further investigation, not endpoints of research but apparatuses through which inquiry continues.

This account of the object refuses the dominant Western visual theory position. The prevailing account positions the art object as a sign: a substitute for its referent, circulating in the social realm of meanings, its function primarily communicative. To claim anything more — that a material practice might have real material effects, that there could be a mutual exchange between the matter of bodies and the representation of bodies — is, as Bolt puts it, to have "extremely limited currency in Western art history and theory" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 269). It requires casting into doubt the identity of the sign, since the dominant view holds that there is a gap between sign and referent that the sign can never cross. To argue that the object does more than represent is to commit, as Vicki Kirby frames it, to asking whether matter might be "more articulate than imagined" (Kirby, 1997, p. 114, cited in Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 274).

New materialist art practice refuses this domestication of the object. Mäkikoskela defines "artistic thinking" as the often unnoticed movement between materiality and conceptuality in visual art practice, grounded in feminist new materialism alongside dialogical thinking with materials. Her key move is methodological: artistic research allows examination of art-making from a participant's point of view, tracking the thinking of the artist through the process — thinking that is not separate from matter but happens through material engagement. This is what distinguishes art practice as research from art practice as illustration: in illustration, the concept precedes and governs the material; in research, the material pushes back on the concept, revising what was thinkable before the encounter. Kirkkopelto names the same condition: the artistic researcher can "jump right in" by appropriating and inventing concepts and staging them as epistemic things, with real risk — because failure is possible when knowledge is genuinely at stake. The object, in new materialist APAR, is never simply what it looks like. It is what it does, what it enters into, what it makes possible. It is, to use Andersen's phrase, a machine for rehearsing how to live in a world not yet fully here.

### Queering the Reading of Text/Foregoing Language Altogether

Andersen shares that she doesn\'t \"read texts properly\", and isn\'t
sure she knows how to. For her, the texts \"are like images, like scraps
of colour and texture.\" Text has a sort of thing-power, as a thing that
can be carried around for years as if in an always wearing pocket, ready
to be taken out, re-examined and reconsidered. Over time/space/matter
the texts come back like curses, the meaning changes as she \"gropes
toward the preponderance of the object\", lingering, as the *there,
there* of the text recedes again into the agential swarm, into the void,
the other, the absolute. (Andersen, 2016, p.42)

Franinović queers the reading of text and makes space for material through interweaving texts and materials by "preparing seeds for material events," mapping concepts, materials, and techniques, then planning experiments with specific materials and choreographing timing (Franinović, 2016). She aimed to transfer "seed-space techniques" to material research to avoid overly predefining processes and allow emergence, asking directly what conditions and constraints help "activate materials" and "activate people's thoughts and actions with and through materials." Participatory workshops become a "third space" where diverse parties produce shared knowledge — a shift away from purely verbal techniques toward non-linguistic collaborative experience with physical artifacts (Franinović, 2016).

What the object is, and what it does, are questions that Western visual
theory has answered badly --- and the bad answer has consequences for
how research through making is evaluated. The dominant account positions
the art object as a sign: a substitute for its referent, circulating in
the social realm of meanings, its function primarily communicative. To
claim anything more --- that a material practice might have real
material effects, that there could be a mutual exchange between the
matter of bodies and the representation of bodies --- is, as Bolt puts
it, to have "extremely limited currency in Western art history and
theory" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 269). It requires casting into doubt the
identity of the sign, since the dominant view holds that there is a gap
between sign and referent that the sign can never cross. To argue that
the object does more than represent is to commit, as Vicki Kirby frames
it, to asking whether matter might be "more articulate than imagined"
(Kirby, 1997, p. 114, cited in Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 274).

Bolt's concept of methexis --- drawn from Indigenous Australian practice
and Paul Carter's theorization of "concurrent actual production" ---
proposes exactly the alternative. In methexis, the making of the work is
not the production of a sign that subsequently represents a reality. It
is a production through which meaning and reality are constituted
together, in the same event. There is no gap to be crossed. The causal
connection runs between the matter of bodies, the matter of the object,
and the matter of the materials of making --- and this production "has
effects" rather than only meanings (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 274). Bolt's term for
practicing within this understanding is "working hot": a state in which
"the process of painting is a response to what happens in the
interaction between paint, oil, turps, canvas, gravity, sun, heat, the
occasional live beast and my body" --- processes that "have actual
causal relations, not merely significatory relations" (Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p.
276). The work is co-emergent rather than mastered. Matter is not
impressed upon; matter insists.

The implication for the research artifact is precise. If artistic
practice is methectic --- if the sign is not separate from its
production but emerges in the interactive labour of making --- then what
the finished work carries is not a representation of the process but an
index of it. Peirce's indexical sign has a direct causal relation
between thing and sign: it produces effects, not just meanings, and it
"allows us to 'witness' in a very different way the process of
materialisation that occurs in the interaction between the matter of
bodies, the matter of materials, technologies and the cultural" (Bolt,
2024, p. 277). The work is a trace of the bodies in labour --- human and
nonhuman --- that produced it. This is why the research artifact in new
materialist APAR is not evidence in the conventional sense of
documentation of findings that were separately reached. It is the
finding, carried as index: materialisation as an effect that points
directly back to the distributed encounter through which it came into
being. Bolt's conclusion names this directly: the matter of the work
"implicates the materiality of the paint and the environment, processes
that have actual causal relations, not merely significatory relations"
(Bolt, 2024 [orig. 2003], p. 276). The work does not represent the research. It is
the research, materially inscribed.

### Performance

What Bolt's account of working hot demonstrates is not only a claim about painting. It names the ontological condition of practice-based research as such: the matter of bodies, materials, and making co-produce each other through intra-action, and what emerges carries real causal effects rather than merely significatory ones. The philosophical grounding for this claim runs through a specific line of development in feminist theory --- one that begins with Butler's account of performativity, is extended by Barad's posthumanist revision of it, and finds independent confirmation in both Bolt's studio and Andersen's workshop.

Butler's account of performativity, developed in *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Bodies That Matter* (1993), is the foundation. Bodies do not pre-exist the norms that produce them: they materialize through the repeated, citational performance of those norms over time. The world is not pre-given but produced through iterative, discursive processes. Matter, in Butler's account, is the effect of those processes --- shaped by discourse, brought into being through cultural repetition. The insight is significant: it breaks the assumption that bodies arrive in the world already constituted and awaiting cultural inscription. They are constituted in and through the inscription itself.

Barad takes this insight seriously and builds from it rather than against it. What she disputes is not the iteration but where Butler locates the agency in that process. For Butler, iteration is discursive --- language and social repetition produce materiality. Matter is downstream of discourse. Barad's posthumanist performativity, developed in "Posthumanist Performativity" (*Signs*, 2003) and *Meeting the Universe Halfway* (2007), expands the ontological ground: the iterative processes that produce the world are material-discursive, not merely discursive. Matter itself participates in its own production. It is not the passive product of language acting on it --- it intra-acts, co-constitutes, pushes back. Butler asks how discourse materializes bodies. Barad asks how matter and discourse co-produce each other through intra-action. The revision is not a rejection. It is an extension that restores matter to the status of co-agent rather than downstream effect.

This distinction is not only philosophical. It determines what kind of claim can be made when the machine speaks back. Andersen makes machines as a way of "arriving at an understanding," because she can only process something "if she re-make[s] it [herself] first." The object comes before the idea or concept of the object, through the repetition of time in "an almost scientific notion of repeatability" (Andersen, 2016, p. 44). This is Butler's iteration --- but the iteration is not citational repetition of discursive norms. It is material-discursive intra-action in Barad's sense: the apparatus of Andersen's body, tools, and workshop conditions co-produces what becomes knowable. Siffrinn and Coda (2025) confirm the pattern across a different medium: "through processual reflections shared by the language learners involved in the animation making, they came to see how this monotonous tactile work created an affective flow wherein more-than-human conversations spurred learning in creative ways such as through encounters with objects in the immediate environment" (p. 196). The monotonous, tactile, repeated making is the research apparatus. What it produces --- the affective flow, the more-than-human conversation --- is the phenomenon. Repetition in Barad's extended sense is not mere rehearsal. It is how material-discursive arrangements enact the cuts through which phenomena become knowable.

Through this process, over time, Andersen develops intuition about the capacities of the thing. The machine speaks back, telling her what it can do and what it is for. The machine is "an entity with its own rules and possibilities" (Andersen, 2016, p. 44). This is not metaphor. In Barad's ontology, the machine's speaking back is real: matter is agential, and the intra-action between Andersen and the machine is the site where both are constituted differently than they were before. Andersen does not cite Spinoza, but she is practicing him. The claim she arrives at through making --- that you cannot know what a body can do until it does it --- is Spinoza's "nobody as yet has determined the limits of the body's capabilities" enacted in the studio. Bolt names the same condition from painting: working hot is the state in which matter insists rather than submits, in which the practitioner's prior concept is exceeded by the encounter. Two artist-researchers, working in different media and different institutional contexts, independently confirm what Barad formalizes: posthumanist performativity is not a theoretical claim about practice. It is a description of what practice, when followed with sufficient attention, actually is.

This is the conflict Cull Ó Maoilearca identifies at the institutional heart of artistic research in performance. Researchers in ARP must negotiate "the broadly representationalist versus performative paradigms: wherein the former construes knowledge as a more or less accurate representation of an objective reality by a transcendent subject, and the latter posits the performative production of things by their ways of knowing and being known and therefore construes knowledge-production as an immanent material part of the same reality which it might seek to represent" (Cull Ó Maoilearca, 2019, p. 154). The very notion of a "contribution to knowledge" — which posits knowledge as a cumulative stock or sanctioned canon — is intrinsically problematic for research projects working from performative rather than representationalist ontologies. This is not a methodological preference. It is a conflict between incompatible accounts of what the world is, and whether practice can be a site of knowledge or only an object to be analyzed by it.

### Beyond Representation — Engaging with the Material Itself

Kirschner's foreword to *Raw Flows* (2016) frames fluidity not as a metaphor but as methodological pressure on research. He positions the book as investigating how fluidity and flow "carve their specific paths into experimental practices and thinking patterns" across arts, humanities, and sciences — a direct "practice-as-research" hinge: material properties shape method and thought, not the other way around. He insists on dealing with fluidity "beyond its metaphorical use," grounding inquiry in "actual substances or stuff" rather than abstract notions of matter. Fluids undermine formalization: they resist capture in still images, which fail to communicate the "core feature of processuality." The project's approach is reframed not as containment — the vessel model — but as an "irregular and cross-disciplinary grid" immersed in material flows to catch, extract, and evaluate ways of dealing with fluidity, producing insights into practice across epistemic, aesthetic, and experiential registers simultaneously.

This is the NM move in its most concentrated form: matter not as illustrative content but as the condition and driver of research. Hinterwaldner's chapter in the same volume demonstrates what this means procedurally. In turbulence experiments, marker materials are not neutral helpers that reveal a pre-given flow. They are materially agentic participants that shape what can be seen, stabilized, recorded, and turned into knowledge. The marker has multiple simultaneous roles — "being, embodying, representing and preparing an object of research… stabilizing an interface… being photogenic… base for further visualizations" (*Raw Flows*, p. 49). The object of research is not prior to the experimental apparatus; it is prepared by it. This is Bennett's thing-power applied to methodology: the "capacity of things… to act as quasi agents or forces with trajectories, tendencies of their own" (*Raw Flows*, p. 49), and Barad's intra-action applied to experimental design: markers "make flows visible… by intra-acting… in an experimental setup-as-assemblage" (*Raw Flows*, p. 49). The apparatus is not neutral with respect to what it knows. It is constitutive of it — which is precisely what Barad theorizes as the agential cut and what Borgdorff et al. name as method-as-intervention. The same principle that runs through quantum physics runs through the turbulence tank: what you use to look at the thing is part of what makes the thing what it is.

### The Body in Time/Space/Matter — Marking and the Fog Chamber

Andersen introduces the practice of marking from the medium of dance. Marking makes a record of the movement of the dancer's body through time/space/matter so that the movement may be studied, collected, shared, and recreated later. The collection of gestures, movement, and time can act as an anchor for conceptual and metaphorical notions performed through the ongoing always-already becoming of an object. In dance, marking is what allows a practice to accumulate without being frozen — it preserves trace without collapsing the living movement into an archive. Andersen borrows the concept because it describes exactly what new materialist APAR requires: a way of holding the research event long enough to work with it, without reducing it to the static representation that would end its generativity.

The studio and workshop space that houses this kind of research is what Moñivas Mayor (2016) calls a flux condenser — a site that gathers drifting phenomena, sounds, material behaviors, and atmospheric conditions, and converts them into creative-research movements. It is not documentation of practice but practice itself as a condensing apparatus for knowing-with materials. The condenser does not contain the flow; it intensifies it until something becomes perceptible that was not perceptible before.

Andersen names the performative moment of making as a fog chamber: the material-discursive arrangement where agential cuts are made through the intra-action of apparatus, subject, object, and time. The fog chamber creates what she calls a possibility space — the conditions under which something new might become thinkable. Making these conditions requires a mentally and emotionally fluid environment, where the researcher risks coming undone through the process of making strange (Adorno's courage of the clown), and where a "chance of becoming human" is created rather than assumed (Andersen, citing Butler, 2005). The possibility space is the momentary product of the research experiment. It is not a stable location. It is what happens in the interval — Trinh T. Minh-ha's break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed (Trinh, 1990, p. 77) — between the concept and the material, between what was known before the encounter and what becomes thinkable through it.

The boundaries of the experiment are set not by the researcher's prior plan but through the agency and liveness of the material pushing back on the limitations of skill, imagination, and time. The boundaries are redrawn through the presentation of the object to others and through subsequent play, where the participants define the play space through improvisation, listening, and the creation of the rules of the game as the game is played. Britton, Klumbyte, and Draude name this directly in *Doing thinking*: artistic research and new materialism together reposition research away from object-production toward making situations, sites, and worldings — the elements do not pre-exist the situation but emerge within it (explicitly Baradian). Research here is not the production of a stable object that then gets analyzed. It is the creation of a situation in which phenomena can appear that could not have been predicted in advance.

### New Materialist APAR as Pedagogical Methodology

When the studio is understood as a flux condenser and the workshop as a fog chamber, new materialist art practice as research becomes transferable as a pedagogical methodology — not a curriculum but a set of conditions. Franinović (2016) provides the most explicit articulation of what this looks like in practice. Her chapter in *Raw Flows* is a methodological manual for new materialist practice-research: the central research-design problem is how to activate materials and activate people's thoughts and actions "with and through materials," via conditions and constraints in a workshop setting. Her guiding question is: "Can materials be seeds for people... and people seeds for materials?" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129).

The method she develops is seeding: mapping concepts, materials, and techniques onto each other, then choreographing the timing of processes so that material and conceptual processes co-emerge rather than one governing the other. A seed-space is "a set of constraints or conditions permanently open to change and new iterations" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129) — not a predetermined outcome but a set of generative pressures. The goal is not to predict participants' desires or needs but to "flirt with existing behaviors to produce new ones" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129). Knowledge arises after setting the seeds and allowing them time to evolve, through direct and participatory observation that explicitly values often-surprising discoveries as legitimate epistemic outputs.

The core concept emerging from this practice is fluid affordance: with dynamic materials, affordances are unstable and transform as the material's state changes. This instability invites exploration rather than use. Where the affordances of everyday objects tend to constrain imagination through habitual instrumental responses — brainstorming pressure yields "the most obvious and habitual affordance" (Franinović, 2016, p. 140) — active materials destabilize that model in an embodied way, making affordances emergent and open-ended. The question shifts from what is active to how is it active: the research contribution is defined as designing conditions where material processes and conceptual processes co-emerge, generating surprising insights, and producing shareable methods — seeding, choreographing, constraining — rather than only artworks.

This is why new materialist APAR is genuinely pedagogical and not merely expressive. It teaches by creating conditions. It shares by transmitting the structure of a situation rather than the content of a finding. What is learned is not a conclusion but a capacity — for attunement, for noticing, for staying with the encounter long enough to let the material's own logic begin to surface. Andersen describes what this produces in the practitioner: the process of making is a process of changing ourselves, "becoming the person who built that thing" (Andersen, 2016). New thoughts are acted out and created "in a complex collaboration between the object, the situation and a performative moment." The knowledge is not deposited in the object. It is held in the changed relation between the practitioner and the world that the making produced.

### The Violence of Traditional Methods of Research 

The words/representations/abstractions of the objects are a method of
necessary communication for traditional academic research. Andersen had
to learn to write academically to communicate her practice, but academic
writing overshadowed/overtook/made her subjective writing seem like that
of a child, and she had to struggle to regain her own voice. (Andersen,
2016, p.42)

The violence named in this section title has a precise theoretical
genealogy that needs to be made explicit. Calvo-Pascual, tracing
"connections --- or, rather, intra-actions ---" among critical
posthumanism, environmental/animal studies, and new materialism,
foregrounds an "ethics of inclusion" as the ethical core of this
theoretical convergence (Calvo-Pascual, 2024). She draws on Alaimo's
trans-corporeality to argue that humans are "always intermeshed with the
more-than-human world," and that ethics must be accountable to a
material world that is "the very substance of our selves and others."
She then draws on Barad to link ethics to "responsibility and
accountability for the lively relationalities of becoming" in which
bodies and environments are intra-actively co-constituted. The violence
of traditional research methods is, on this account, not metaphorical.
When research forces material and experiential knowledge into
representational protocols that were designed to exclude it --- when
Andersen must learn to write academically in a way that overwrites her
own voice, when Schwab's "forced acting, incoherent scripts, and
pointless monologues" become the mark of failure rather than evidence of
genuine epistemic risk --- what is being enacted is an exclusion with
material consequences for bodies, practices, and the forms of knowledge
that can circulate. The violence is the systematic refusal of the
intra-active, the trans-corporeal, the entangled --- which is to say,
the refusal of the world as it actually is.

Schwab frames the field's confusion as coming from unsuitable
adaptations from non-artistic fields (humanities/sciences), which reduce
the epistemic implications of aesthetic labour to sensory/experiential
"input."

The institutional question becomes unavoidable: what is a suitable
peer-reviewed format for "the exposition of practice as research"?

He treats prescribed "reflection" (especially in art education) as a
haunting constraint: a demand for verbalization that may miss "more
fundamental operations of knowledge creation," and that keeps
hierarchies from being "flat enough" under post-media conditions.

Instead of locating "reflection" and "creation" in predetermined sites,
expositionality presses toward forms where practice articulates research
outside conventional orders of production and reflection.

### Push Back 

Fox & Alldred (2021, applied research) warn that diffractive methodology
can become "researcher-centric and context-dependent" because the
analytical cuts are driven by the analyst's decisions, raising issues of
bias and transferability. They also note that ethico-onto-epistemology
alone may not supply insight into "how or to what extent" specific
methods influenced findings.

(Fox & Alldred, 2021)

This push back elucidates the central problem of art practice as
research within traditional institutions. Critique of APAR methods
misunderstand that the essential nature and foundational tenants do not
comport with the structure and desires of traditional research and
reporting of research findings.

Critique of diffractive practice because of the potential for bias and
researcher centeredness, missed the necessity of the researcher
experience investigated horizontally alongside the other participants in
the assemblage/apparatus. The goal is not to erase the human, but to
flatten the field and make space for things/matter/material.

The charge of researcher-centrism is itself a representationalist
criticism. It presupposes that a properly objective method would be one
from which the researcher has been removed --- a view from outside the
phenomenon, a neutral apparatus that doesn't contaminate what it
measures. But as Barad makes clear, representationalism is "a prisoner
of the problematic metaphysics it postulates. Like the frustrated
would-be runner in Zeno's paradox, it never seems to be able to get any
closer to solving the problem it poses because it is caught in the
impossibility of stepping outward from its metaphysical starting place"
(Barad, 2003, p. 803). The demand that diffractive methodology
demonstrate its objectivity by removing the researcher from the
apparatus is not a stricter standard of rigor --- it is a return to the
very representationalist fantasy that diffractive practice is designed
to refuse. Accountability in diffractive practice does not come from
removing the researcher. It comes from naming the apparatus, specifying
the cuts, and tracing the marks left on bodies. That is what objectivity
means when the observer is understood to be always already part of the
phenomenon.

Draude and Klumbytė add a further dimension to this pushback by warning
against idealizing collaborative or hybrid research spaces as neutral
zones. "Third spaces" and hybrid methodologies must retain their
postcolonial, decolonial, and feminist stakes, including sustained
attention to power relations and positionality (Draude & Klumbytė,
2019). Their insistence that genuinely hybrid methodologies may need to
be created --- rather than simply importing methods from one domain into
another --- is both methodological and political: it rejects extractive
method transfer and demands the accountable co-constitution of methods
inside hybrid research spaces. This applies directly to the critique of
diffractive methodology as researcher-centric: the researcher's
situatedness, positionality, and power are not contaminants to be
removed from the apparatus, but constitutive conditions to be named,
examined, and held accountable. Removing the researcher does not produce
objectivity; it produces the illusion of objectivity while concealing
the specific human-nonhuman assemblage that generated the findings.

The necessity of explicitly tying specific methods to research insights
is ignoring the emergent causality of the field and that the
entanglement of becoming/knowing/doing is a feature, not a bug.
