# Introduction

This document answers three questions. What are the theoretical and methodological distinctions between posthumanism and new materialism? How have other posthumanist theorists conceptualized diffractive practice? How have new materialist theorists conceptualized art practice as research? The questions are arranged in that order because each supplies the ground on which the next stands. Part One establishes what the world is, in the accounts posthumanism and new materialism give of it. Part Two establishes how to inhabit and work within that world as a researcher. Part Three establishes what artistic research becomes when those commitments are enacted by named practitioners whose medium is the apparatus through which the research is conducted.

I write from a particular location. I am an artist, educator, researcher, parent, and neurodiverse person. My background is in anthropology and art (BA), art education (MA), and experimental art and technology (MFA). My PhD research entangles posthumanism and new materialism with each of these. Adrienne Rich’s poetry has been with me for thirty-five years; encountering her as the locational ground of feminist materialism reads, to me, as coming home. The same is true of Trinh T. Minh-ha, whose work I almost pursued in graduate study in visual anthropology before the path turned. My interest in quantum science is not metaphorical: its history shows me that the relational, indeterminate character of the world has been repeatedly obscured to maintain authoritarian controls governed by the Platonic dualism this document traces and refuses.

The form of the document is part of its argument. Multiple overlapping descriptions are not redundancy but posthumanist method: each frame produces what the others foreclose, and no single account exhausts the phenomenon. The reader who expects a single line of argument, exhaustively defended, will read this against the grain. The reader who reads it as a set of overlapping cuts on a shared ground will see what it is doing.

# Part One: What are the theoretical and methodological distinctions between posthumanism and new materialism?

## 1. Theory, Methodology, Method

Theory, methodology, and method are situated first at the meta-level through art theorist, educator, historian Graeme Sullivan. According to Sullivan ([Sullivan, 2006, p. 22](#ref-sullivan2006)), “research is a transformative act that has an impact on the researcher and the researched.” Sullivan, drawing on Morrow, situates theory as the conceptual framework through which inquiry orients itself. Theories make broader ontological and epistemological claims that drive how research is conducted. Methodology is the principled approach to inquiry that follows from the theory. The research methodology guides decisions on what methods are necessary to accomplish the research goal. Methods are particular procedures developed by particular practitioners in particular sites, and they enact the methodologies. The chosen methods have consequences for what evidence the researcher can collect.

This distinction maps onto the three questions the document answers. Part One treats posthumanism and new materialism as theories in Sullivan’s sense: conceptual frameworks making ontological and epistemological claims about what the world is and how it can be known. Part Two treats diffractive practice, transpositions, and making strange as methodologies: principled approaches to inquiry that follow from those theoretical commitments. Part Three treats specific methods enacted by named artist-researchers whose practices produce knowledge under the conditions Parts One and Two describe.

## 2. Field Positions

Posthumanism and new materialism are two distinct critical traditions that developed in opposition to the same historical, cultural, and economic conditions: the Cartesian-Newtonian settlement that rendered matter inert, Enlightenment humanism that installed the sovereign rational subject at the center of knowledge and value, the industrial and postindustrial orders that depended on both. They share a non-dualistic, non-representationalist, entangled understanding of nature and culture, a more vitalist understanding of matter, and an empirical field that no longer takes human exceptionalism as a point of departure ([Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 191](#ref-siffrinn-coda2025)) (citing Coole & Frost, 2010; Bennett, 2010; Braidotti, 2013). Posthumanism focuses on the figure of “Man”: the human universal, the species hierarchy that placed one version of the human above all other forms of life and matter. New materialism focuses on the reduction of the material world to passive substance, available for inscription, extraction, and instrumentalization by an external force. Over time, their unique identities have become more entangled. According to Francesca Ferrando ([Ferrando, 2014, p. 169](#ref-ferrando2014)), new materialism has its own origin story unique from posthumanism, but by the end of the 1990s it had emerged as a specific branch of the posthuman field, and the two now function as necessarily twinned frames through which to navigate the world ([Militsi, 2021, p. 11](#ref-militisi2021)) (citing Ferrando, 2013, p. 30). They are emerging continuums of difference, co-constituted, and their combined momentum pushes toward new ethics of what, who, where, when, and why counts as the real, counts as agency, and counts as knowledge.

Ferrando (2019) shows the commitments they share are not recent and traces them back to Aristotle, Plato, and the Old Testament, where the world was separated into hierarchies of the ideal and the profane, and where thinkers who remained with complexity, flux, and porous boundaries were repeatedly absorbed into frameworks that preserved binary order (p. 170). Spinoza’s monism, Whitehead’s process philosophy, and phenomenology all resisted that reduction in different ways. Indigenous knowledge systems long predate the Western academic traditions that later marginalized or omitted them from the standard genealogy ([Anderson, 2020](#ref-anderson2020); [Kontturi & Tiainen, 2024](#ref-kontturi-tiainen2024); [Pennycook, 2024](#ref-pennycook2024); [Torosyan et al., 2021](#ref-torosyan-reis-wuschitz2021)). These commitments did not first appear in the 1990s. The 1990s mark the beginning of their consolidation into a shared vocabulary ([Ferrando, 2013](#ref-ferrando2013)). Haraway marks the threshold clearly: “bounded individualism in its many flavors in science, politics, and philosophy has finally become unavailable to think with, truly no longer thinkable, technically or any other way” ([D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 4](#ref-haraway2016)).

Philosophically, posthumanism and the new materialisms presuppose that knowing and being are inseparable: “epistemological practices are necessarily ontological practices; to know is to be part of the world’s continual construction” ([Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 192](#ref-siffrinn-coda2025)) (citing Barad, 2007, p. 379). As Wolfe insists, to become posthuman is to find a mode of thought adequate to “processes which can never be entirely reduced to patterns or standards, codes or information” ([Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii](#ref-wolfe2010)) (citing Rutsky). This is the logic Clarke names when quoting Bateson’s suggestion that “the whole of logic would have to be reconstructed for recursiveness” ([Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii](#ref-wolfe2010)) (citing Clarke, citing Bateson, 1972): if the world is recursive, relational, and non-deterministic, then method cannot remain representational, as if it were a neutral mirror standing outside what it studies.

Rosi Braidotti’s neo-materialism, grounded in both posthumanism and new materialism, is a way to address the paradox of the simultaneous over-exposure and disappearance of the human. Both theories together create a transversal field of knowledge ([Braidotti, 2019](#ref-braidotti2019)). Posthumanism can apply to “any field of enquiry, starting from our location as a species, to the individual gaze” ([Ferrando, 2014, p. 168](#ref-ferrando2014)); Wolfe demonstrates this reach across animal studies, disability studies, bioethics, literary and cultural studies, architecture, systems theory, and poetry ([Wolfe, 2010](#ref-wolfe2010)). Van der Tuin and Dolphijn (2010) identify new materialism’s strength as “this nomadic traversing of the territories of science and the humanities, performing the agential or non-innocent nature of all matter” (pp. 158–159). Alaimo and Hekman find material feminism operating across “science studies, environmental feminisms, corporeal feminisms, queer theory, disability studies, theories of race and ethnicity, environmental justice, (post-)Marxist feminism, globalization studies, and cultural studies” ([Alaimo & Hekman, 2008, pp. 9–10](#ref-alaimo-hekman2008), cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 103). Siffrinn and Coda’s (2025) survey of posthumanist and new materialist research in applied linguistics finds that “these theories are being operationalized by scholars in the field on a larger scale than is being returned in research databases” (p. 194).

Art practice as research is one of the sites in which posthuman and new materialist methodological commitments were enacted. Kontturi and Tiainen argue that contributions from art practice are frequently ignored in the most visible literature, specifically noting Barad’s accounts, when art practice was not simply a later application of new materialist thought but “one of the very milieus in which the call to rethink the involvement of matter in human practices and in the more-than-human world arose” ([Kontturi & Tiainen, 2024, p. 259](#ref-kontturi-tiainen2024)).

## 3. The Two Critiques

### 3.1 The Critique of Man

Posthumanism is a critique of a specific notion of what it means to be human: the autonomous, sovereign, rational subject installed by Enlightenment humanism: and of the exclusions that produced that figure ([Ferrando, 2014](#ref-ferrando2014)). Foucault could already see, from within the humanist revolution, that it was contingent: “man is an invention of recent date. And one perhaps nearing its end” ([Wolfe, 2010, p. xii](#ref-wolfe2010)) (citing Foucault). The “man” at stake is not all humans. It is a particular configuration that van der Tuin and Dolphijn trace to Kant: “Kant started his philosophy with the human being, or even better, with human thought and its relation to nature. In other words, Kant turned (theological) metaphysics into transcendental anthropology” ([Dolphijn & Tuin, 2012, p. 165](#ref-dolphijn-vandertuin2012)). After Kant, the human subject becomes the condition of possibility for knowledge itself, the measure against which everything else is placed.

Ferrando ([Ferrando, 2014](#ref-ferrando2014)) specifies what was installed in the figure of that universal human: a particular type of embodiment: male, white, Western, heterosexual, physically able, propertied: treated as neutral and fit-for-all. The critique of Man is therefore inseparable from the feminist, critical race, queer, disability, and postcolonial traditions that refused to accept the universal as universal. Braidotti ([Braidotti, 2024](#ref-braidotti2024)) proposes the framework of two discrete but intersecting critiques: the critique of the humanistic “‘man of reason’” and the critique of exceptionalist anthropocentrism, or “‘anthropolatry’” (p. xii). Together, these critiques expose how Eurocentric universalism and belligerent “‘species-ism’” produced exclusionary norms of the human, casting sexualized, racialized, and naturalized others as dehumanized or outside the category of the human (p. xii). Braidotti argues that the notions currently circulating around the posthuman are affirmative because they “break free from the sedimented habits of thought that confirm institutionalised power relations” (p. xii).

Posthumanism is not anti humanism, and should not be confused with transhumanism. Transhumanism is antihumanism’s mirror: it claims to transcend the human while intensifying the very figure posthumanism rejects. Transhumanism, associated with figures such as Nick Bostrom and Hans Moravec, seeks the enhancement of human intellectual, physical, and emotional capacities, the elimination of disease and suffering, and the extension of human life span, all in service of a figure of the human whose “basic capacities so radically exceed those of present humans as to no longer be unambiguously human by our current standards” (Bostrom, cited in Wolfe, 2010, p. xiii). Wolfe describes transhumanism as “an intensification of humanism” because it extends the prerogatives of the autonomous liberal subject rather than questioning that subject as the basic unit of analysis ([Wolfe, 2010, p. xiv](#ref-wolfe2010)). Moravec’s fantasy of downloading consciousness into a computer in pursuit of technological immortality continues the humanist investment in disembodied rationality. Posthumanism instead opposes “the fantasies of disembodiment and autonomy, inherited from humanism itself” and insists on embodiment, embeddedness, and materiality ([Wolfe, 2010, p. xiv](#ref-wolfe2010)).

### 3.2 The Critique of Inert Matter

Western metaphysics inherited from the atomists (Leucippus, Democritus, Epicurus), a model of matter as inert: tiny, indivisible, passive particles moving through the void, their shapes fixed, their combinations determined by collision. Matter moved when struck. It produced only what its given characteristics permitted ([Bennett, 2010](#ref-bennett2010)). Gamble, Hanan, and Nail (2019) argue that Lucretius, in his De Rerum Natura, moves beyond earlier atomism and anticipates performative new materialism by treating matter as something that ceaselessly constitutes novel entities and relations (pp. 112–113), but the line that endured was the one that served mechanism.

The multiple theorists cited here trace the evolution of matter through the proponents of matter as inert: French philosopher and scientist René Descartes (1596–1650), English Scientific Revolution philosopher Sir Francis Bacon (1561–1626), German Enlightenment philosopher Immanuel Kant (1724–1804). Then they trace the movement through the proponents of matter as lively: French philosopher Henri Bergson (1859–1941) and French philosopher Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961).

Bacon synthesized theology, naturalism, and mechanism into a single framework in which, as Gamble et al. summarize him, “the force implanted by God in these first particles makes up all the variety of things” (2019, p. 115). God imparts force to matter, sets the laws of nature in motion, and rests. Descartes, in a hard line account of dualism, states mind and matter are categorically different substances, and the mind examines the material world from a position of separation. Kant, as already noted, secures this epistemologically, through his conception of a world in which matter can be inscribed, extracted, and instrumentalized without resistance: because matter, by definition, offers none ([Gamble et al., 2019](#ref-gamble-hanan-nail2019)) . Coole and Frost (2010) name the consequence: a framework that “yields a conceptual and practical domination of nature as well as a specifically modern attitude or ethos of subjectivist potency” (p. 9). The critique of the sovereign subject (§ 3.1) and the critique of inert matter meet across this cut.

Barad’s matter is “not a fixed substance; rather, matter is substance in its intra-active becoming, not a thing but a doing, a congealing of agency” and “always already an ongoing historicity” ([Barad, 2003, pp. 821, 822](#ref-barad2003)). Bennett opposes the parsing of the world into “dull matter (it, things)” and “vibrant life (us, beings)” and asks how political responses would change if we took seriously “the vitality of (nonhuman) bodies” ([Bennett, 2010, pp. vii–viii](#ref-bennett2010)). Braidotti describes philosophical neo-materialism as a line of immanence concerned with “the vital immanence of non-anthropomorphic life-systems,” where differences are not constituted oppositionally or dialectically but as “internal modulations within a common matter that is intelligent and self-organised” ([Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii](#ref-braidotti2024)). Barad’s intra-active becoming, Bennett’s vibrant matter, and Braidotti’s line of immanence continue to resonate throughout this text.

Coole and Frost (2010) also stress that new materialism is not new and does not deny a “rich materialist heritage” (p. 7). Matter as active, generative, and self-organizing has a longer philosophical history than the recent turn. Spinoza had already made the counter-claim in 1677: the mind is the idea of the body. Mind and body are the same thing. What Spinoza calls conatus, the striving of singular things to persevere in their being, is not something added to matter from outside but immanent to it ([Bennett, 2010](#ref-bennett2010); [Dolphijn & Tuin, 2012](#ref-dolphijn-vandertuin2012)). Henri Bergson expands Spinoza’s notions into a philosophy of duration in which life works to “insert some indetermination into matter,” and the forms it creates are therefore “indeterminate, i.e. unforeseeable” ([Bennett, 2010, p. 79](#ref-bennett2010)). Merleau-Ponty extended the emphasis on immanence, process, and embodiment through a phenomenology of perception in which the body is itself a fold, a reversibility that “always miscarries at the last moment” ([Coole, 2010, pp. 110–111](#ref-coole2010)).

Postmodernism began the dismantling of the nature/culture divide, but Ferrando ([Ferrando, 2013](#ref-ferrando2013)) argues that postmodernism’s dismantling of the nature/culture dualism often privileged its nurtural side, producing a constructivist and representationalist radicalization that “somehow lost track of the material” (p. 114). Barad laments: “language matters, discourse matters, culture matters,” but “the only thing that does not seem to matter anymore is matter” ([Barad, 2003, p. 801](#ref-barad2003), cited in Ferrando, 2013, p. 114). New materialism responded not by restoring nature against culture, but by refusing the dualism altogether and treating matter as an ongoing process of materialization that is “dynamic, shifting, inherently entangled, diffractional and performative” ([Ferrando, 2013, p. 114](#ref-ferrando2013)). Posthumanism inherits postmodernism’s critique of the sovereign subject; new materialism reasserts the material dimension that constructivist accounts subordinated ([Ferrando, 2013, pp. 25, 114](#ref-ferrando2013)).

### 3.3 Points of Contestation

Gamble et al. ([Gamble et al., 2019](#ref-gamble-hanan-nail2019)) do not perceive all strands under new materialism to be equivalent, and distinguish among vital materialism, negative materialism, and performative materialism. All three strands “share at least one common theoretical commitment: to problematize the anthropocentric and constructivist orientations of most twentieth-century theory in a way that encourages closer attention to the sciences by the humanities” ([Gamble et al., 2019](#ref-gamble-hanan-nail2019), pg. 111). The present project draws on vital and performative materialisms. Vitalist materialism, the most prevalent strand, emerged from Spinoza and Leibniz via Deleuze in the 1960s to Jane Bennett’s vibrant matter today. Performative materialism, which Gamble et al. find the most promising strand, is grounded in the work of Karen Barad and Vicki Kirby. Barad’s account of posthumanist performativity (performative materialism) joins an anti-anthropocentric critique of the human subject to an account of matter as active and co-constitutive. Barad draws from earlier feminist and science theorists, as Militsi ([Militsi, 2021](#ref-militisi2021)) summarizes, and presents posthumanist performativity as a “diffractive elaboration” of Butler and Haraway ([Barad, 2003, p. 808](#ref-barad2003)).

The relation between the two traditions is also contested. Srnicek (2017) groups posthumanism, speculative realism, and object-oriented philosophy as variants within a broader new materialist field, while noting that posthumanism contributes concepts of agency, structure, and epistemology to that grouping. Smelik (2018), by contrast, situates new materialism within a posthuman context, arguing that posthumanism’s decentering of the human opened conceptual space for agency to be attributed to things and nature. Wolfe ([Wolfe, 2010](#ref-wolfe2010)) cautions that some posthumanist scholarship continues to preserve a residual human exceptionalism, even after rejecting the classical figure of Man. Srnicek (2017) echoes that some posthumanist approaches preserve distinctions between human and nonhuman systems, especially around self-reflection and intentional action. The notion of vitality everywhere is also critiqued from within: Blok, 2024, argues new materialists may overlook a fundamental difference and limit hybridisation of theory by viewing Earth’s materiality primarily through the lens of the organic and alive. Blok proposes the notions of conativity vs. responsivity as a corrective, in which he rejects the idea that conativity and responsivity are shared symmetrically by all material entities and argues instead that some entities are conative (rocks, sand dunes) but not responsive ([Blok, 2023](#ref-blok2023)). Ferrando names hegemonic essentialism as a potential failure of posthumanist research; the habit of producing a posthumanist text in content while framing it within the hegemonic traditions posthumanism sets out to unsettle. As she writes, “A text written by such standards might claim a posthuman content, but does not appear posthuman in its praxis. Its inner contradiction is obvious: while attempting to produce a discourse critical of humanism, it uncritically frames itself within those same traditions from which humanism developed” ([Ferrando, 2012, p. 12](#ref-ferrando2012)).

## 4. What the Two Critiques Produce

### 4.1 The Ground

Once the figure of Man and the figure of inert matter are both refused, what is left is not nothing “(while not being some thing), but it may in fact be the source of all that is, a womb that births existence” ([Barad, 2012, p. 9](#ref-barad2012)). It is a world that has been there all along, now reportable because the frames that obscured it have been set down.

The Wild. The void. The absolute. Non-identity. A life. The primordial swerve. Zoe. The virtual. The plane of immanence. These terms do not collapse into one another; each carries the weight of the theoretical tradition from which it comes. They resonate as names for a ground that exists before and after any specific intra-action, outside any particular agential cut, beyond the reach of any apparatus that would make it measurable. It is the field of virtual potential from which phenomena emerge when conditions for their emergence are met, and into which they dissolve when the apparatus that held them in place is released ([Bennett, 2010](#ref-bennett2010); [Braidotti, 2013](#ref-braidotti2013)) (Deleuze, as cited in Cheah, 2010).

In quantum physics, before measurement, before the agential cut that produces a phenomenon, there is superposition: not multiple things coexisting but a state of ontological indeterminacy in which the boundaries and properties of the thing have not yet been enacted. “At the heart of quantum physics is an inherent ontological indeterminacy” ([Barad, 2012, p. 7](#ref-barad2012)). Barad reads physicist Niels Bohr as having advanced an ontic (ontological) interpretation against the epistemic interpretations: entities “do not determinately exist apart from the particular, physical measuring apparatuses that constitute them one way to the exclusion of others” ([Gamble et al., 2019, p. 122](#ref-gamble-hanan-nail2019)).

Barad’s apparatus does not reveal what was already there. It brings forth what was not yet determined. Between measurements, between intra-actions, what exists is this indeterminate field, the real-but-virtual, the condition from which specific configurations of time/space/matter emerge and into which they return ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007)). Bohr’s intervention, as Barad reads him, was to relocate the problem from uncertainty, understood as a limit on knowledge, to indeterminacy, understood as a feature of the phenomenon itself (Barad, in Dolphijn & van der Tuin, 2012, p. 63). “Space, time, and matter do not exist prior to the intra-actions that reconstitute the entanglements” ([Barad, 2007, p. 74](#ref-barad2007)). Phenomena, Barad’s primary unit of analysis, “do not merely mark the epistemological inseparability of ‘observer’ and ‘observed’; rather phenomena are the ontological inseparability of intra-acting ‘agencies.’ That is, phenomena are ontological entanglements” ([Barad, 2007, p. 333](#ref-barad2007)).

Other theorists name this ground from their own location. Deleuze, as Cheah ([Cheah, 2010](#ref-cheah2010)) reports, calls it the transcendental field and the plane of immanence: “the real ‘condition of possibility’ of the actual,” and “made up of pre-individual and impersonal singularities” (pp. 84–85). Matter on this account is “the unformed, unorganized, nonstratified, or destratified body and all its flows: subatomic and submolecular particles, pure intensities, prevital and prephysical free singularities” (p. 87). The virtual is real: “every real object has a virtual content . . . The virtual must be defined as strictly a part of the real object” (p. 85). Actualization does not unfold a preformed possibility. It determines a virtual field that is already real.

Braidotti calls the force that moves through this ground Zoe: life as a cosmic force that precedes and exceeds any individual subject, any bounded organism, any particular moment of consciousness. The subject is a temporary intensification of this force, not its owner. “This nonanthropocentric view expresses both a profound love for Life as a cosmic force and the desire to depersonalize subjective life-and-death. This is just one life, not my life. The life in ‘me’ does not answer to my name: ‘I’ is just passing” ([Braidotti, 2010, p. 210](#ref-braidotti2010)).

Haudenosaunee and Anishinaabe scholar Vanessa Watts names the same ground through Place-Thought: “the non-distinctive space where place and thought were never separated because they never could or can be separated. Place-Thought is based upon the premise that land is alive and thinking and that humans and non-humans derive agency through the extensions of these thoughts” (Watts, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 211). Zoe Todd argues that Indigenous thinkers deserve recognition for “their millennia of engagement with sentient environments, with cosmologies that enmesh people into complex relationships between themselves and all relations, and with climates and atmospheres as important points of organization and action” (Todd, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 210). Anderson, following Braidotti, argues that placing different orders of knowledge in dialogue “does not mean creating false equivalences” but instead “aims at amplifying possible resonances between them” (Braidotti, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 205). Indigenous frameworks and new materialist ontology resonate at important points, and Anderson ([Anderson, 2020](#ref-anderson2020)) reports that Indigenous scholarship has been omitted from standard origin stories of new materialism.

Trinh T. Minh-ha articulated the same ground in her 1989 text, *Woman, Native, Other: Writing Postcoloniality and Feminism*. Writing from Taoist, Zen, and Vietnamese frames against the Western mirror ontology, in the book section titled “The infinite play of empty mirrors,” she writes that “reality is not reconstituted, it is put into pieces so as to allow another world to rebuild (keep on unbuilding and rebuilding) itself with its debris” ([Minh-ha, 1989, p. 23](#ref-minh-ha1989)). The mirror is not the symbol of a pre-given vision but, in the Asian traditions she draws on, “the ‘symbol of the very void of symbols’”; “the perfect man’s mind is like a mirror,” Chuang Tzu observed, “it reflects things but does not retain them” ([Minh-ha, 1989, p. 23](#ref-minh-ha1989)) (citing Chuang Tzu). Trinh’s formulation, written from within artistic practice rather than from the philosophy of matter, is contemporaneous with Haraway’s 1988 “Situated Knowledges” and predates by more than a decade the posthumanist/new materialist vocabulary that would name what she was already articulating. Like Watts’s Place-Thought and Todd’s account of Indigenous engagement with sentient environments, Trinh’s relational ontology is part of the genealogy the standard origin story has tended to omit.

### 4.2 Inhabit the world

If this is what the world is, then we are already in it, and always have been.

Bennett states human power is also a kind of thing-power. From the perspective of evolutionary time, mineral efficacy becomes visible in the emergence of bone: “We are walking, talking minerals” ([Bennett, 2010, pp. 10–12](#ref-bennett2010)). The human is not an exception to matter but one of its temporary organizations. Bennett extends this further in her discussion of microbial life: “the its outnumber the mes,” so that it is no longer adequate to imagine the body as a single bounded being. “We are, rather, an array of bodies, many different kinds of them in a nested set of microbiomes” ([Bennett, 2010, p. 113](#ref-bennett2010)).

Alaimo elucidates on what this looks like at the level of a body. “Trans-corporeality means that all creatures, as embodied beings, are intermeshed with the dynamic, material world, which crosses through them, transforms them, and is transformed by them” (Alaimo, forthcoming, p. 1). The figure-ground relation between organism and environment dissolves as the outline of the human is traversed by substantial material interchanges. The trans-corporeal subject is generated through and entangled with biological, technological, economic, social, and political systems at vastly different scales (Alaimo, forthcoming, pp. 1–4). The ontological ground is therefore not elsewhere than embodiment.

The primary unit of reality necessary for navigation varies across theorists. For Bennett, the unit is the thing or actant within a distributed agency of assemblages ([Bennett, 2010, pp. viii–ix, 37–38](#ref-bennett2010)). For Barad, it is the phenomenon produced through specific material-discursive practices and agential cuts ([Barad, 2007, pp. 128–129, 146, 175–176](#ref-barad2007)). For Braidotti, it is the nomadic subject traced through cartographies and transpositions ([Braidotti, 2018, pp. 26–29](#ref-braidotti2018)).

Leibnizean matter/body has no voids: “folds replace holes,” and matter offers, as Deleuze glosses him, “an infinitely porous, spongy, or cavernous texture without emptiness,” each body elastic, “pierced with irregular passages,” dependent for its coherence on the pressure of external forces ([Coole, 2010, p. 111](#ref-coole2010)). For Deleuze’s Leibniz, “the point of view is in the body” ([Deleuze, 1993, p. 10](#ref-deleuze1993)). The world here is not made of discrete units standing in external relation, but of folds: matter endlessly folding and unfolding, generating provisional forms as styles of existing rather than fixed substances. It is not the flat geography of Cartesian extension, laid out before the lumen naturale “without dark recesses, crevices, or hollows” ([Coole, 2010, p. 94](#ref-coole2010)). It is a topography of curves, labyrinths, and hollows, coiled and creased and rippled, with clarity “endlessly plunging into obscurity” ([Coole, 2010, p. 111](#ref-coole2010)). The exemplary science of this ontology is not Euclidean geometry but origami, and its afterlives run through the pre-Socratics, Whitehead, postclassical physics, and the folded proteins of microbiology ([Coole, 2010, p. 110](#ref-coole2010)).

Merleau-Ponty reads the same Leibnizian fold as a reversibility that “always miscarries at the last moment,” producing the dehiscence through which emergence remains possible rather than closing into inert repetition ([Coole, 2010, p. 107](#ref-coole2010)). This is a teeming, turbulent, convoluted world of infinite folded matter, continuously folding and unfolding, its modulations yielding “provisional forms as styles of existing” rather than fixed substances ([Coole, 2010, p. 111](#ref-coole2010)). The body does not perceive the world from a detached point of view. It does not inhabit a flat, geometrical, fully determinate plane but a milieu, an Umwelt that remains ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant with expressive significance.

Merleau-Ponty discerns French painter Paul Cézanne (1839-1906) as trying to capture these perspectival distortions as they occur in perception, in order to convey “an emerging order,” where the object is “in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes” (Coole, 2010, p. 104). Cézanne’s painting brings the material world alive; it does not measure or represent reality but emulates the way it materializes in perception ([Coole, 2010, p. 106](#ref-coole2010)). Coole notes that Merleau-Ponty explicitly contrasts this with the Cartesian tradition of classical perspective — what he calls “Renaissance perspective as a cultural fact” and “impoverished” as artistic style precisely because its Euclidean geometry presents nature in fixed, objective coordinates rather than in the ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant field in which it actually appears. Cézanne’s refusal of that convention could be read as both an aesthetic choice and a phenomenological position: the attempt to suspend culturally fashioned perception and uncover the “vertical” world of “brute” or “wild” perception as it emerges ([Coole, 2010, p. 106](#ref-coole2010)).

The quantum ontology and the art-historical refusal of fixed perspective are entangled. Sullivan states when Cézanne captured a scene from multiple views, he “anticipated what the physicists were scratching their heads about at the time”, the late 1800s, early 1900s growth of explicit quantum theory ([Sullivan, 2010, p. 12](#ref-sullivan2010)). Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) identify Bolt ([2000](#ref-bolt2000)) as one of the first art scholars to name quantum theory as a resource for visual practice, working from Kirby’s reading of Bohr rather than from Barad — placing art scholarship alongside, not downstream from, Barad’s development of agential realism.

Merleau-Ponty’s account of Cézanne resonates with Bennett’s account of lingering. Bennett espouses lingering as the practice adequate to inhabiting this world. She calls for methods that cultivate a “patient, sensory attentiveness to nonhuman forces operating outside and inside the human body” ([Bennett, 2010, p. xiv](#ref-bennett2010)). Without this “countercultural kind of perceiving,” the world appears as if it consists only of “active human subjects who confront passive objects and their law-governed mechanisms” ([Bennett, 2010, p. xiv](#ref-bennett2010)). Bennett renders the Deleuzian virtual as “a life”: “a pure event, freed from the subjectivity and objectivity of what happens,” a “subjective current visible only fleetingly” that “tears the fabric of the actual without ever fully coming out in a person placed or thing”; it is “matter of movement or matter energy, a matter in variation that enters assemblages and leaves them” ([Bennett, 2010, p. 53](#ref-bennett2010)).

### 4.3 Navigate the world

A knower who is part of what she knows cannot know from above. She has to know where she is to find a location from which to travel.

Feminist poet Adrienne Rich (1929-2012) names the body as the starting location. In “*Of Woman Born*”, she writes:

> Patriarchal thought has limited female biology to its own narrow specifications. The feminist vision has recoiled from female biology for these reasons; it will, I believe, come to view our physicality as a resource, rather than a destiny. In order to live a fully human life we require not only control of our bodies (though control is a prerequisite); we must touch the unity and resonance of our physicality, our bond with the natural order, the corporeal ground of our intelligence. ([Rich, 1976, p. 40](#ref-rich1976))

In “Blood, Bread, and Poetry”, Rich advises: “Begin not with a continent or a country or a house, but with the geography closest in: the body” ([Rich, 1984, p. 212](#ref-rich1984)). Bennett identifies “feminist studies of the body” as part of the larger trend that put “desire and bodily practices back on the ethical radar screen,” a key precondition for her own move toward nonhuman vitality ([Bennett, 2010, p. xi](#ref-bennett2010)). Braidotti traces feminist neo-materialism to Rich’s politics of location, tracing a line from Rich through situated knowledges and standpoint debates ([Braidotti, 2022](#ref-braidotti2022)). She identifies “feminist philosophies of neo-materialism and diversity” as a fundamental source for navigating the posthuman convergence and defines the politics of location as “a method based on the notions of embodiment and lived experience” ([Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii](#ref-braidotti2024)). Feminist theory, she writes, emphasizes the “situated and accountable nature of knowledge,” introduces “the embodied and embedded,” and holds that “Being embodied: sexed and gendered subjects embedded in the world: is the starting point for critical reflection” ([Braidotti, 2024, pp. xvii–xviii](#ref-braidotti2024)). Bodily materialism produces “a new empirical-discursive methodological approach that combines critique with the creation of alternatives” ([Braidotti, 2024, p. xviii](#ref-braidotti2024)). Braidotti’s nomadic subject and cartography give this orientation its methodological form: nomadic because the knower moves rather than settles, cartographic because the account is a map drawn from somewhere rather than a picture taken from above.

Trinh T. Minh-ha brings the politics of location and the refusal of closure into film practice. In “Documentary Is/Not a Name,” she argues that “meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown,” and identifies “the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed” as the condition that prevents closure ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 77](#ref-minh-ha1990)). Haraway later takes up Trinh’s formulation of “critical difference within” in “The Promises of Monsters” (1992, pp. 299–300), linking this refusal of closure to feminist accounts of situated knowledge and difference. Haraway pushes the epistemological argument further in “Situated Knowledges.” The “god trick,” the claim to disembodied universal vision, is not a neutral description of scientific method but an effect of power. Against it, Haraway argues for “politics and epistemologies of location, positioning, and situating, where partiality and not universality is the condition of being heard to make rational knowledge claims” ([D. Haraway, 1988, p. 589](#ref-haraway1988)).

Somerville names the condition in research practice as the post-human I. Accompanying children on weekly creek walks, she writes: “I feel as if I am changed through these videos, learning to be human differently in the company of the creek and children” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1165](#ref-somerville2016)), and later: “I become other-to-myself, as if I migrate outwards into these scenes that form and reform around me” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1167](#ref-somerville2016)). There is, she writes, no sense of following a research question and finding an answer, only “complete immersion in the event of place” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1167](#ref-somerville2016)) (citing Massey, 2005). The account remains hers, but the “I” is no longer outside the field it describes.

This navigation requires methods be cartographic rather than classificatory. Cartography is generative: it maps forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold, producing a “living map”, Braidotti’s phrase, rather than a fixed taxonomy. Cartographic methods trace forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold rather than assigning phenomena to pre-existing categories. Cartographic methods are accountable to the apparatus: every account is grounded in specific political and material conditions rather than in a claim to universality that suppresses the apparatus that produced it ([Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii](#ref-braidotti2024)) and the instrument of knowing is not neutral with respect to what it knows but constitutive of it, and rigor lies in naming and specifying the apparatus rather than treating it as absent ([Barad, 2007, pp. 29–30, 140–141](#ref-barad2007)). They are affirmative rather than only critical: they attend not only to what is wrong but also to what is being produced and what is becoming possible ([Braidotti, 2024, pp. xiii, xviii](#ref-braidotti2024)). Cartographic methods refuse exhaustive capture: generativity is treated as a condition of accountable knowledge in a world of ongoing becoming.

### 4.4 Affirmative Ethics

If the world is as § 4.1 reports it, and we inhabit it as § 4.2 reports, and we know it from inside as § 4.3 reports, then the question of what to do follows from the question of where we are. Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch generalize the commitment: “research methods do not only observe and represent materials, issues, and events but in fact act upon and intervene in these materials, issues, and events” (2020, p. 7). For this commitment, I will use Braidotti’s term affirmative ethics as a location to read through.

For Braidotti, the raison d’être of the whole project is affirmative ethics, and its ontological ground is zoe. Because the individual “I” is not the center of life but a temporary passing of it, the logic of retribution, rights-claims, and compensation: all premised on a stable individual subject: dissolves. What replaces it is an “ethics of affirmation” that “suspends the quest for both claims and compensation and resists the logic of retribution or rights” ([Braidotti, 2010, p. 214](#ref-braidotti2010)). In Transpositions, she writes that “an ethics of sustainability” structures her argument and that “this transformative ethics includes a critical or reactive and an affirmative or active phase” ([Braidotti, 2006, p. 5](#ref-braidotti2006)). She asks how to cultivate “the political desire for change or transformation” and describes this as a change in both “our schemes of thought” and “our ways of inhabiting the world” ([Braidotti, 2006, p. 5](#ref-braidotti2006)). In later work she states that “our politics must be guided by an affirmative ethics” and defines ethics as “a discourse about forces and relations, and not as a set of normative moral injunctions” ([Braidotti, 2024, p. xiii](#ref-braidotti2024)). Bodily materialism, in this account, produces “a new empirical-discursive methodological approach that combines critique with the creation of alternatives” ([Braidotti, 2024, p. xiii](#ref-braidotti2024)). Braidotti defines affirmative ethics directly as “the establishment of mutually empowering relations based on cooperation and the productive combination of the specific degrees of intensity or potentia of each living entity,” which “aims at increasing each entity’s capacity to preserve themselves against adverse forces” ([Braidotti, 2022, p. 97](#ref-braidotti2022)). She also names it as “the affect that binds together the heterogeneous components of the complex zoe-geo-techno-subjects” ([Braidotti, 2022, p. 97](#ref-braidotti2022)).

Haraway frames the same orientation through response and relation. In *Staying with the Trouble*, she describes the task as “learning to stay with the trouble of living and dying in response-ability on a damaged earth” ([D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 2](#ref-haraway2016)). On the opening page, she writes that the task is “to make kin in lines of inventive connection as a practice of learning to live and die well with each other in a thick present” ([D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 1](#ref-haraway2016)). Haraway names the task as cultivating “the capacity to respond to worldly urgencies with each other” ([D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 7](#ref-haraway2016)).

Bennett does not use the phrase affirmative ethics in *Vibrant Matter*, but she explicitly presents her project in ethical and political terms. The political project, she writes, is “to encourage more intelligent and sustainable engagements with vibrant matter and lively things.” She asks how political responses might change if we took seriously “the vitality of (nonhuman) bodies,” defining vitality as the capacity of things not only to impede human designs but also to act “as quasi agents or forces with trajectories, propensities, or tendencies of their own” ([Bennett, 2010, p. viii](#ref-bennett2010)). She also writes that “alongside and inside singular human agents there exists a heterogeneous series of actants with partial, overlapping, and conflicting degrees of power and effectivity” ([Bennett, 2010, p. 23](#ref-bennett2010)). Later in the preface, she describes the ethical task as cultivating “the ability to discern nonhuman vitality” and becoming “perceptually open to it” ([Bennett, 2010, p. xiv](#ref-bennett2010)).

Barad makes ethics a matter of accountability for the cuts that enact inclusion and exclusion ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007)). “Objectivity,” Barad writes, means “being accountable for marks on bodies, that is, specific materializations in their differential mattering.” Barad continues: “We are responsible for the cuts that we help enact,” and “the cuts that we participate in enacting matter” ([Barad, 2007, p. 179](#ref-barad2007)). Because “intra-actions cut ‘things’ together and apart,” ethics is not about a radically exterior other, since “‘they’ and ‘we’ are co-constituted and entangled through the very cuts ‘we’ help to enact” ([Barad, 2007, p. 179](#ref-barad2007)). Agency, in this framework, includes “the possibilities and accountability entailed in reconfiguring material-discursive apparatuses of bodily production, including the boundary articulations and exclusions that are marked by those practices” ([Barad, 2007, p. 214](#ref-barad2007)). Acknowledgment of “nonhuman agency” does not reduce human responsibility. It requires “that much more attentiveness to existing power asymmetries” ([Barad, 2007, p. 219](#ref-barad2007)).

The cut is therefore, as Barad puts it, the site at which responsibility and possibility meet: “particular possibilities for acting exist at every moment, and these changing possibilities entail a responsibility to intervene in the world’s becoming, to contest and rework what matters and what is excluded from mattering” ([Barad, 2003, pp. 826–827](#ref-barad2003)).

Braidotti’s raison d’être, Haraway’s response-ability, Bennett’s perceptual discipline, and Barad’s agential cut resonate across the four accounts of affirmative ethics.

### 4.5 Hayles, Haraway, Barad

Posthumanist and new materialist frameworks do not stop at the critique of unlocated knowledge. They actively redefine the researcher’s relation to the research. If there is no phenomenon prior to the apparatus that produces it, and if matter and meaning emerge through specific material-discursive arrangements, then there is no outside position from which the researcher can observe without implication. Barad’s account of agential realism, as summarized by Timeto, rejects separation as foundational and defines representation as an intra-active practice embedded in sociotechnical networks. “Entangled agencies” do not precede their relations; they emerge through them ([Timeto, 2011, pp. 153, 164](#ref-timeto2011)). The researcher is part of the apparatus.

Barad’s *Meeting the Universe Halfway* (2007) is where these navigational threads are most systematically brought together, but Timeto ([Timeto, 2011](#ref-timeto2011)) shows that the linkage was already underway: constrained constructivism, situated knowledges, and agential realism can be read as three connected stages in a single argument.

The first stage is Hayles’s constrained constructivism, which Timeto reads as an attempt to escape the deadlock between realism and anti-realism without abandoning representation entirely. Hayles’s key move is to replace congruence with consistency as the criterion for adequate representation. Congruence implies a one-to-one correspondence between signs and things: the classical representationalist demand that a representation mirror its object. Consistency asks something different: not whether a representation accurately reflects reality, but whether it is viable, whether it holds at the cusp between the constructed concepts through which we know the world and the flux those concepts can never fully capture. The flux is “inherently unknowable and unreachable by any sentient being” (Hayles [1991] 1997, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 157); what we can know are the constraints that make certain representations consistent with it rather than others. Representations, on this account, are “species-specific, culturally determined and context-dependent processes of dynamic interaction” (ibid., p. 157) rather than neutral transcriptions. Constrained constructivism establishes that the observer is always already in a feedback loop with what she observes and that the zone of interaction between observer and flux is the site where knowledge occurs. It does not yet account for how the observer’s specific position shapes that knowledge, or why positionality is a condition of knowing rather than contamination.

The second stage is Haraway’s situated knowledges, which extends Hayles’s cusp into an explicitly political and epistemological account of location. Where Hayles establishes that the observer is always embedded in the interaction, Haraway insists that the specific coordinates of that embedding are not incidental variables but constitutive of what becomes knowable. The partiality of the situated perspective is not a concession to limitation; it is, as Timeto reads Haraway, “the whole that we see as the result of contextual and specific interactions with the flux” ([Timeto, 2011, p. 159](#ref-timeto2011)). Haraway’s figurations do the work that mere positional disclosure cannot: they are “performative images that can be inhabited” ([D. J. Haraway, 1997, p. 11](#ref-haraway1997), cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 160), not static pictures of the world but representations that remain adequate insofar as they keep their performativity alive. Figurations do not stand outside what they describe; they are living maps always generated from somewhere, from below and within the networks of technobiopower, and they work by putting boundaries in “constructive and transformative tension rather than using them as dividing lines” ([Timeto, 2011, p. 161](#ref-timeto2011)). Diffraction enters here as Haraway’s methodological name for this different optics: rather than reflection, which “only displaces the same elsewhere” ([D. J. Haraway, 1997, p. 16](#ref-haraway1997), cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 163), diffraction maps where the effects of differences appear, registering interference and interaction rather than mirroring a pre-given original. Situated knowledges argues that partiality names where knowledge sees from, and that naming is what makes it answerable. It does not yet state that the phenomenon itself is constituted through the intra-action of apparatus and matter, or that the cut producing the phenomenon is simultaneously an ethical act.

The third stage is Barad’s agential realism, which extends the movement prepared by Hayles and Haraway. Barad, as Timeto reads her, accepts Haraway’s diffractive methodology and grounds it in a thoroughgoing ontological claim: representation is not a sign that mirrors a separate external referent but “a diffractive practice that reveals the co-emergence and the co-implication of both meaning and matter” ([Timeto, 2011, p. 164](#ref-timeto2011)). The apparatus does not reveal what was already there; it is constitutive of the phenomenon it produces. Timeto identifies the continuity between all three stages at precisely this point: Barad’s “constraining intra-actions” return directly to Hayles’s consistency, since constraints are what enable the selection among viable representations and shift the question from what we could see in isolation to “the interaction with reality we see when we see” (Hayles [1991] 1997, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 164). The observer does not stand in a separate domain but participates in the “world’s differential becoming” ([Barad, 2007, p. 91](#ref-barad2007), cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 164), engaging in “specific worldly configurations from the inside” (ibid.). Agential realism adds that the cut producing a determinate phenomenon from an indeterminate field is not a free interpretive choice but a material event with ethical weight: “particular possibilities for acting exist at every moment, and these changing possibilities entail a responsibility to intervene in the world’s becoming, to contest and rework what matters and what is excluded from mattering” ([Barad, 2003, pp. 826–827](#ref-barad2003), cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 164). Representation is not removed from an originary source. It is the cut that produces the things it appears merely to describe.

## 5. Closing of Part One

The distinction between posthumanism and new materialism across Part One maps different emphases, genealogies, and methodological centers of gravity. Posthumanism critiques the figure of Man; new materialism critiques the reduction of matter to passive substance. Both frames refuse dualism, representationalism, and the view from nowhere. What they yield together is a world that is relational, processual, and virtual before it is actual, inhabited by bodies that are arrays rather than singulars, navigated by situated knowers who are part of the apparatus that produces what they know, and lived within as the ongoing work of accountable response to what each cut makes possible.

Trinh names the interval, “a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 77](#ref-minh-ha1990)). Braidotti names cartography as an embodied, embedded, grounded method ([Braidotti, 2024](#ref-braidotti2024)). Barad names the agential cut ([Barad, 2007, p. 179](#ref-barad2007)). Bennett names lingering ([Bennett, 2010, pp. xiv, 15](#ref-bennett2010)). Trinh’s interval, Braidotti’s cartography, Barad’s agential cut, and Bennett’s lingering resonate across differences of tradition and register.

Part Two extends Part One with the question: if posthumanism and new materialism together establish that the world is relational, processual, and virtual before it is actual, then the question becomes how to inhabit and work within that world as a researcher and practitioner. Part Two reports diffractive practice, transpositions, making strange, and the practitioner accounts of method as the named operations through which this work is conducted.

# Part Two: How have other posthumanist theorists conceptualized diffractive practice?

## 1. Why This Question Follows from Part One

Part One established that posthumanism and new materialism together describe a world that is relational and processual, in which the virtual and the actual remain in ongoing relation, a world in which the researcher is part of the apparatus that produces what she knows, and in which accountability for the cuts she helps enact is simultaneously the site of objective knowledge and ethical responsibility. Part Two asks what practice/method/methodology looks like in this frame.

The framing of the second question named “diffractive practice” as the goal, yet when I surveyed the literature, I found that the explicit use of that term was my take-away from Barad. In the literature, the notion of diffraction/diffracting is termed with much broader language.I use diffractive practice as an umbrella term for methods, methodologies, and practices that approach the world, and thus research, as diffractive. Under that umbrella the literature provides a constellation of named operations: diffractive methodology (Barad), transpositions (Braidotti), becoming-diffractive (Hill), diffractive watching and watching diffractively (Militsi), mosaic listening (Clark and Moss, extended by Dudley), making strange (Shklovsky, Brecht, extended by Andersen and Schwab). Holding them together is a set of commitments: refusal of representational distance, decentering of the human, distributed agency, accountability to the apparatus rather than to an external canon, ethics as ongoing negotiation rather than fixed rule.

Part Two organizes the literature into three locations in the constellation: diffractive practice, transpositions, and making strange, then reports a group of practitioner accounts of diffractive practice in the terms their authors themselves use. The organization is not a claim of classification, but an organizing principle for reading through.

A diffractive practice aspires to “diffract the rays of technoscience so that we get more promising interference patterns on the recording films of our lives and bodies” ([D. J. Haraway, 1997, p. 16](#ref-haraway1997)). Haraway had already named the choice at the level of research: reflexivity, she argues, “like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere, setting up the worries about copy and original and the search for the authentic and really real” (page 16). Diffraction, in Barad’s development of the same distinction, produces a pattern of the effects of difference, not a map of where differences are located in things ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007)) (citing Haraway). Methods that treat phenomena as discrete, stable, and pre-given (rather than diffractive) produce a systematically distorted account of what the world is ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007); [Tuin & Dolphijn, 2010](#ref-vandertuin_dolphijn_2010)).

## 2. Differences that make a difference

Diffraction is the mapping of effects of difference rather than the locating of difference in things ([Barad, 2003](#ref-barad2003)). This distinction, between difference as a property of objects and difference as the pattern produced by their intra-action, is what separates diffractive practice from comparative analysis. Comparison asks: how do these things differ? Diffraction asks: what interference pattern is produced when these things are read through one another, and what does that pattern make visible that neither could produce alone?

Gregory Bateson: anthropologist, cyberneticist, and participant in the Macy Conferences from 1942 onward supplies the methodological anchor for how knowledge is read. Mark Engel, a student of Bateson’s who helped assemble the 1972 collection, identifies in the preface what Bateson’s ontology commits the researcher to. “The central idea in this book is that we create the world that we perceive, not because there is no reality outside our heads … but because we select and edit the reality we see to conform to our beliefs about what sort of world we live in” ([Engel, 1971, p. vi](#ref-engel1971)). “For a man to change his basic, perception-determining beliefs: what Bateson calls his epistemological premises: he must first become aware that reality is not necessarily as he believes it to be” ([Engel, 1971, p. vi](#ref-engel1971)).

In the essay “Form, Substance, and Difference,” Bateson distinguishes two worlds of explanation, borrowed from Jung by way of Gnostic vocabulary: the *pleroma*, “the world in which events are caused by forces and impacts and in which there are no ‘distinctions’”; and the *creatura*, in which “effects are brought about precisely by difference” ([Bateson, 1972, p. 462](#ref-bateson1972)). Bateson defines information as “this difference which makes a difference” ([Bateson, 1972, p. 463](#ref-bateson1972)); elsewhere he specifies that “a difference which makes a difference is an idea. It is a ‘bit,’ a unit of information” ([Bateson, 1972, p. 272](#ref-bateson1972)).

## 3. Diffractive Practice

Diffractive practice names a set of methods that reject representational distance and treat inquiry as participation in the production of the phenomenon under study. Across the texts gathered here, method proceeds through situated partiality, non-objectifying relation, and accountability to the apparatus rather than through neutral observation from outside. What matters methodologically is not comparison between fixed terms but the tracing of interference patterns, the specification of cuts, and the maintenance of the intervals through which something new becomes perceptible.

### 3.1 Trinh, Haraway, and the genealogy of diffraction

Diffraction enters feminist theory through a specific citational chain: from Trinh T. Minh-ha’s writing on difference, through Haraway’s construction of the diffraction metaphor in dialogue with that writing, to Barad’s later ontological formalization. The chain has been largely reported at second and third hand, with Trinh’s contribution appearing through Haraway’s citations and Barad’s footnotes rather than from the primary source. Restoring Trinh to a full accounting changes what the genealogy is a genealogy of.

In *Woman, Native, Other* (1989), in the section titled “Infinite layers: I am not i can be you and me,” Trinh writes: “A critical difference from myself” means that differences must be “grasped both between and within entities, each of these being understood as multiple presence” ([Minh-ha, 1989, p. 94](#ref-minh-ha1989)). Identity is composed from infinite layers: “not a unified subject, a fixed identity, or that solid mass covered with layers of superficialities one has gradually to peel off before one can see its true face” (p. 94). Identity displays a “necessary ambivalence” (p.94), always fleeing static essence, defying all attempts to contain categories that leak. Trinh’s filmmaking and writing from the 1980s onward develop this ontology in specific methodological form: speaking nearby, the interval, the refusal of the transparent observer position [Minh-ha ([1989](#ref-minh-ha1989)); 1990; 1992]. Part Three § 2 treats her practice as artistic research in depth.

Haraway introduces diffraction as a feminist optical metaphor in 1992, explicitly taking up Trinh’s formulation. In “The Promises of Monsters,” Haraway extends Trinh’s “critical difference within” into a methodological claim: “Diffraction does not produce”the same” displaced, as reflection and refraction do. Diffraction is a mapping of interference, not of replication, reflection, or reproduction. A diffraction pattern does not map where differences appear, but rather maps where the effects of difference appear” ([D. Haraway, 1992, pp. 299–300](#ref-haraway1992)). This is the origin of the diffraction metaphor in feminist theory: generated from a reading of Trinh, not from quantum physics alone. By 1997, in *Modest_Witness*, Haraway develops diffraction into what Barad later calls a “fourth semiotic category,” connecting it to her broader critique of reflexivity: “reflexivity, like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere,” whereas diffraction produces genuinely new interference patterns ([D. J. Haraway, 1997, p. 16](#ref-haraway1997)). Haraway’s version of diffractive practice is inseparable from her commitment to figuration: representations that are “performative images that can be inhabited,” partial maps accountable to their situatedness ([D. J. Haraway, 1997, p. 11](#ref-haraway1997)). In her 2003 essay, Barad cites this chain explicitly, naming Haraway’s reading of Trinh as the source from which diffraction moves into material-discursive theory ([Barad, 2003, pp. 804 n.3](#ref-barad2003)) (citing Haraway, 1992, p. 300).

The chain matters because it places an artist-practitioner/anthropologist at the root of a concept typically attributed to science studies. Diffraction as feminist method does not begin from physics; it begins from Trinh’s insistence on critical difference within, reworked by Haraway into an optical metaphor for situated knowledge, and is then grounded by Barad in quantum field theory. Restoring Trinh as source rather than citation repositions the genealogy: diffractive practice is not physics applied to feminist theory but feminist practice given ontological weight after the fact.

### 3.2 Barad

For Barad, diffractive practice is inseparable from the apparatus. The apparatus is not a tool external to the researcher. It is the material-discursive arrangement: the configuration of instruments, concepts, bodies, institutions, and practices: through which phenomena are produced. The researcher is always already part of the apparatus; there is no view from outside. Diffractive practice, in Barad’s version, means making the apparatus clear: naming it, specifying the agential cuts it makes, and tracing the marks it leaves on bodies. The agential cut is not a free interpretive choice. It is the determinate event through which boundaries are enacted, relata emerge, and accountability is located. Diffractive practice is rigorous precisely because it specifies these cuts rather than naturalizing them. The question it asks is not “what does this mean” but “what does this configuration of differences produce, and what does it foreclose.” Its ethical force comes from the insistence that exclusions are constitutive: what the apparatus does not produce is as significant as what it does, and the researcher is response-able for those constitutive exclusions [Barad ([2003](#ref-barad2003)); 2007].

Barad then takes Haraway’s diffraction metaphor and grounds it in quantum field theory, making the metaphor into an ontological claim: phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, and diffraction is not only a way of reading but a description of how the world is constituted ([Barad, 2003](#ref-barad2003)) (citing Haraway, 1992, p. 300).

### 3.3 Diffractive Practice in Education

The five educators gathered here work across levels and contexts: from early years practice to teacher education to undergraduate curriculum: and each one attaches diffractive practice to specific educational acts. Taken together they show diffractive practice not as a fixed procedure but as a commitment to materially situated inquiry, multiple forms of attunement, and knowledge-making through entangled practice.

Cher Hill, writing in teacher education at Simon Fraser University, gives the clearest methodological distinction. Her aim is not to replace reflective practice with diffractive practice, but to move “beyond reflective practice” by distinguishing “diffractive reading” from “experimenting with a diffractive apparatus” ([Hill, 2017, pp. 5–6](#ref-hill2017)). Diffractive reading usually happens after the event, while experimenting with a diffractive apparatus happens within the event as an “onto-epistemological material practice” that helps “configure the world in particular ways” (p. 6). Hill names three interrelated ways of becoming diffractive: “becoming-with the world,” “displacing and diffracting the selves who teach,” and “embracing difference and interference” (pp. 6–11). Hill illustrates becoming-with through a beach assignment in which teachers collecting materials for a weaving project encounter a wounded bird. The event is an assemblage composed of “the assignment, the wounded bird, the hot sunny day, the cell phone, and the instructor’s lack of intervention,” (p. 8) which together produced “enacting care in the face of hopelessness.” (p. 8) She illustrates displacing and diffracting the selves who teach through the “interview to the double” (p. 9) method: a teacher’s account of meeting with a struggling student materializes several teacher-identities at once: “the compassionate teacher,” “the professional teacher,” “teacher as gatekeeper,” “the litigious teacher,” and “teacher as empath.” (p.10) She illustrates embracing difference and interference through a family quarrel read through two different theories: self-regulation theory and new materiality theory: to show that diffractive practice produces “new phenomena and new subjectivities” (p.11) through interference rather than fit.

Louise Lambert, working in initial teacher education at Birmingham City University, defines diffraction as an “otherwise” practice. She writes against forms of reflection that have become “a technical task orientated towards summative assessments of professional competency” and “a predominantly cognitive and linear practice” ([Lambert, 2021, p. 2](#ref-lambert2021)). Against that model, she proposes “diffraction as an ‘otherwise’ means” for exploring teaching as “non-linear and materially and affectively entangled through time and space” (p. 1), and states that diffraction is “not an ‘either/or’ alternative but an ‘otherwise’, expanded way of thinking” (p. 3). The named concepts through which she develops that practice are affect, intra-action, assemblage, becoming-with, and spacetimemattering. Her fullest example is the participant “M,” whose collage centers on her attempt to run a Black Lives Matter club and her later move to running a choir. Lambert shows assemblage by naming the heterogeneous elements in the scene: mentor, policy, curriculum silences, local racial history, music, administration, children, affects, and M’s own body. She shows spacetimemattering when historical and contemporary racial discourses come to matter in the present encounter, including the local history of Enoch Powell’s “rivers of blood” speech and the wider resonance of Black Lives Matter. The collage itself materializes affect and overload through images Lambert describes as a “disintegrating body, eyeballs rolling across the craft paper,” and an “octopus whose legs perform multiple functions of administration and teaching tasks.” (p. 25)

Kate Dudley, writing from early childhood studies and early years professional practice, presents diffractive practice through Mosaic listening. She describes the Mosaic Approach as “multi-contextual listening” and names “photo-elicitation, digital cameras, and observations of play” as the techniques through which children are given “diverse avenues to express themselves naturally” ([Dudley, 2025](#ref-dudley-nd)). Dudley names this approach “Mosaic listening ([Clark & Moss, 2001](#ref-clark-moss2001))” and states that it “centres children’s voices, ensuring they are heard and respected” ([Dudley, 2025](#ref-dudley-nd)). She grounds the study in “ethico-onto-epistemology”: the intertwining of ethics, knowing, and being in every intra-action: and turns immediately to “nomadic ethics,” which she defines as “an ongoing responsibility, requiring flexibility and responsiveness in practice” attentive to “a constant state of becoming” ([Dudley, 2025](#ref-dudley-nd)). She specifies what this looks like in practice through examples from the study: a child using painted sticks while talking about “complex friendships and family,” children “making a home fit for a King,” and a “chatterbox session” where children bring a favorite toy. Her summary formulation is that “diffractive practice: a way of seeing through multiple lenses: is a powerful tool” for practitioners working with children’s gender expression and identity ([Dudley, 2025](#ref-dudley-nd)).

Carol Taylor, is writing from the location of a Professor of Gender and Higher Education describing undergraduate BA Education Studies module, places diffraction in curriculum design. Her chapter uses Barad’s notion of diffraction to show how “curriculum-making can be done via a patterned activity of creative interference” ([Taylor, 2019, p. 37](#ref-taylor2019)) and grounds that claim in the module *Educational Spaces: Theories and Perspectives* (pp. 42–43). She illustrates diffraction first through the design of the module itself. The course runs for twelve weeks, but the order of the themed teaching weeks is not fixed in advance. Students are given the themes and readings, and participate in sequencing the module. They “participate in materializing the (their) curriculum through an act of collaborative co-creation” (p. 50) and must “live with” the curriculum they have helped make, which means they experience their own “cuts” as productive of matter and meaning and as entailing “responsibility and accountability” to themselves and each other (p. 45). Taylor illustrates innovative teaching and learning practices by having students inventory the classroom matter itself: “desks, carpet, lectern, rubbish, dust, lights, pens, iPads, whiteboard etc.” (p. 45). They then ask questions such as “why is that there?” and “who decided that should go there?” (p. 45). In another task, the class sits in silence for two minutes and attends to “the lights hum, the feet pass outside, the traffic in the distance, and to our own breathing,” along with “heat, atmosphere, aromas and light” (p. 46).

Sofie Sauzet, reporting through work with students in a professional bachelor’s programme in social education, names her method “thinking through picturing.” She wanted to move students from “learning about practices” to “producing situated knowledges in and through practices” ([Sauzet, 2015, p. 42](#ref-sauzet2015)). She names “a diffraction apparatus” (p. 39) and describes the specific method she chose: “snaplogs,” defined as “a visual, ethnographic method that involves taking pictures (snap) in response to specific questions, and writing small corresponding texts (logs)” (p. 41). The snaplog assignment required students to conduct ethnographic fieldwork in practicum settings, take at least five pictures of something that went “across and beyond” their existing conception of professionalism, and answer for each image what is shown, why they took the picture, and how it relates to interprofessional practices (p. 42). In Carla’s snaplog, the image shows “M” working at a local DIY center. The interview expands the scene to include sweeping the parking lot, wearing yellow safety vests, moving shopping carts, using the coin mechanism, and M’s pride in saying, “Hi I work at the DIY center!” (pp. 45–46). Sauzet reads the photograph, student, activity center, DIY center, parking lot, vests, shopping carts, and feeling of usefulness as entangled in the production of interprofessionalism (pp. 46–47). In Johanne’s snaplog, the image is a bathroom with a hoist at the youth club where she is interning (p. 47). Johanne’s concern is not only what the hoist is, but what professional knowledge is needed to use it safely over time and who takes responsibility for that expertise (pp. 47–48). Sauzet says this case does not only show what interprofessionalism is, but also what it “might be” (p. 49). The snaplog slows practice down and makes different cuts possible (p. 49).

### 3.4 Refusal of exhaustive capture as method

Diffractive practice does not aim to produce a total account of its object. It aims to produce a situated, accountable account that remains open to further intra-action. Braidotti describes cartography as an embodied, embedded, grounded account that is relational and affective ([Braidotti, 2024](#ref-braidotti2024)). Trinh names the interval and speaking nearby as the twin commitments that hold the account open rather than closing it around the object [Minh-ha ([1990](#ref-minh-ha1990)), p. 77; Chen ([1992](#ref-minh-ha1992)), p. 86; see Part Three § 2 for the fuller treatment]. Barad names every method as boundary-making: agential cuts are material events that enact what becomes determinate and what is foreclosed, and the researcher is responsible for them ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007)). Braidotti’s cartography, Trinh’s interval, and Barad’s agential cut resonate across the three accounts as commitments to generativity rather than exhaustive capture.

## 4. Transpositions

Transposition names a creative leap across registers, materials, or fields. The literature gathers a family of accounts under this term, with variations among them. Transposition and diffraction resonate as distinct operations: diffraction reads interference patterns produced by specific cuts; transposition carries content across registers ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007); [Braidotti, 2006](#ref-braidotti2006)).

### 4.1 Braidotti

Braidotti’s transpositions provide both the method for movement and frame that mediates the move. Transposition is “a scientific term used in biology and musicology” that she adopts as “a metaphor for this specific way of working” ([Braidotti, 2006, p. 5](#ref-braidotti2006)). It “indicates an intertextual, cross-boundary or transversal transfer, in the sense of a leap from one code, field or axis into another, not merely in the quantitative mode of plural multiplications, but rather in the qualitative sense of complex multiplicities” ([Braidotti, 2006, p. 5](#ref-braidotti2006)). The move from genetic code to music is not analogical; it is the method itself: a “creative leap” that produces new relations by carrying content across registers that were not previously connected. Transposition “is not just a matter of mere translation, but a creative act” that produces “something new” through the passage ([Braidotti, 2006, p. 6](#ref-braidotti2006)). Braidotti’s cartography extends this: the researcher is a cartographer whose accounts are embodied, embedded, and grounded in specific locations and histories ([Braidotti, 2024](#ref-braidotti2024)).

### 4.2 Schwab

Schwab’s introduction to the edited volume 2018 book *Transpositions: Aesthetico-Epistemic Operators in Artistic Research* identifies what distinguishes transposition in artistic research from its uses elsewhere. The kinds of transposition of interest in the context of artistic research “operate outside registers of representation, resemblance, or mimesis” ([Schwab, 2018, p. 7](#ref-schwab2018)). The logic of representation is singular, “remaining the same across different instances, while the logic of transposition is multiple, needing to be transposed from instance to instance” ([Schwab, 2018, p. 7](#ref-schwab2018)). The positional specificity that is part of transpositionality: “whether in space, time, or otherwise determined”: explains why transpositional operations have been so difficult to approach philosophically, and why artistic research, which is sensitive to the specifics of what is at hand, may present new options ([Schwab, 2018, pp. 7–8](#ref-schwab2018)). The key claim is about the point of origin. Other authors in the text need historically established concepts to work from, foregrounding deconstructive approaches while keeping a certain degree of distance; artistic researchers seem to have the luxury to “jump right in simply appropriating or inventing concepts whose plasticity can be supplied by artistically staging them as epistemic things. If they work, new understanding will be gained; if they fail to work, we may encounter forced acting, incoherent scripts, and pointless monologues. In the absence of deeper historical grounding, there is no other choice but to take this risk” ([Schwab, 2018, pp. 8–9](#ref-schwab2018)).

### 4.3 Pirrò

David Pirrò writes about his chapter in *Transpositions*, “I regard this text as a diffraction. The object being diffracted is the concept of transposition and the obstacle, the slit that it encounters, is the need to formulate this very text” ([Pirrò, 2018, p. 136](#ref-pirro2018)). He describes transpositions as “relational positions: they are objects whose primary characteristic is to indicate distances to their origin or towards other transpositions. That is, they construct a space from a network of differences” ([Pirrò, 2018, p. 140](#ref-pirro2018)). There is “no defined common basis” linking one transposition to another or to its original material, and “relations and differences towards one another and with the object they are applied remain incompressible, differential, incomparable and resist integration” ([Pirrò, 2018, p. 144](#ref-pirro2018)).

According to Pirro, “a transposition is not an analysis. A transposition is a complexification” ([Pirrò, 2018, p. 141](#ref-pirro2018)). Complexification is the movement opposed to analysis: “a process that tries to keep the phenomenon intact without breaking it apart and therefore neutralising the interactions between its parts” ([Pirrò, 2018, p. 141](#ref-pirro2018)).

### 4.4 Martin, Sayers, and Carroll: transpositions as six practices

Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025), working in organizational studies, operationalize Braidotti’s transpositions as six practices in the context of organizational studies and instructional learning, drawing on feminist posthumanist methodology for research into workplace culture, leadership formations, and organizational change. Grounded in Braidotti’s relational ontology of becoming, her critique of imaginary formations, her nomadic subject position, and her affirmative ethical orientation, they provide a full systematic derivation of transpositions as research protocol.

The six practices are: embracing alternative ways of seeing and understanding the world, assessing and critiquing imaginary formations, preparing to make the creative leap, accounting for locations and positioning, storying the in-between space, and developing new frames of resonance.

*Embracing alternative ways of seeing and understanding the world* is a reorientation before inquiry begins. An ontology of becoming implies that it is the nature of all life to become and to keep doing so through the embedded and embodied relations that produce affects. Affect is “the change, or variation, that occurs when bodies collide, or come into contact”: an “experiential force” that propels processes of becoming and that is not limited to human bodies (Coleman, 2010, pp. 11–12, cited in Martin et al., 2025). The challenge is that humanist dualisms and a unitary vision of the autonomous subject continue to “act with force in the context of everyday organizational relations” even when they are not consciously endorsed.

*Assessing and critiquing imaginary formations* identifies “the choreography of constraints and entitlements” ([Braidotti, 2006, p. 86](#ref-braidotti2006)) that imaginary forms: constellations of images, ideas, values, representations, and narratives: materialize for differently historied bodies. The assessment is not of the imaginary form directly but of its effects: what ways of relating it endorses or precludes, what identities it confers or regulates, how it increases or restricts the relational capacities of subjects within their specific locations. Martin et al.’s exemplar is “heroic leadership” as an imaginary formation sustained by masculinist assumptions even in contexts where men are physically absent.

*Preparing to make the creative leap* cultivates “generative cross-pollinations” across multiple and often seemingly unconnected theoretical domains, genres, modes of apprehension, and discursive communities ([Braidotti, 2017, p. 83](#ref-braidotti2017)). The researcher embodies the identity of the nomad: a traveler who suspends attachment to established discourses and follows “a map of her own making” ([Braidotti, 2011, p. 165](#ref-braidotti2011)). This is the “consumption of the old in order to engender the new” ([Braidotti, 2011, p. 68](#ref-braidotti2011)).

*Accounting for locations and positioning* names the multi-faceted embeddedness the researcher brings to any inquiry. Braidotti’s phrase “history tattooed on your body” (2003, p. 54) names the different forms and experiences of oppression, empowerment, and difference that constitute any specific location. A feminist politics of location insists on the multiplicity of intersecting differences within and between subjects.

*Storying the in-between space* is the work of experimentation and creation. The researcher brings her “hybrid mixture of codes, genres, or modes of apprehension” ([Braidotti, 2011, p. 225](#ref-braidotti2011)) to story different, more empowering rhythms for existing cultural formations. Martin et al. use feminist speculative fiction (Le Guin, Griffith) as the site where transpositions practices are embedded in narrative form, because speculative fiction “defamiliarizes our normative conceptions of existence, nature and the human” ([Grebowicz & Merrick, 2013, p. 113](#ref-grebowicz-merrick2013)).

*Developing new frames of resonance* proposes more adequate frameworks for imaginary formations. New words, terms, and frames “unsettle the ‘old’ or accepted meanings and practices” associated with imaginary forms and “signal the possibilities for other ways of thinking, acting and responding” (Truman, 2019, as cited in Martin et al. ([2025](#ref-martin-sayers-carroll2025)), p. 513). Transposition practices produce “generative cracks” in hegemonic systems and in dominant social imaginaries, bringing forth affirmative alternatives for thought and practice.

### 4.5 Militsi: diffractive watching as seven propositions

Anna Militsi proposes “the methodology of diffractive watching and watching diffractively as another tool for film analysis” ([Militsi, 2021, p. 2](#ref-militisi2021)), extending van der Tuin’s diffractive reading into the act of viewing. She distinguishes between the two terms: diffractive watching as “a set intention and methodology” and watching diffractively as “an active process,” and is interested in “the different implications that will result if diffractive watching is applied as a lens and/or employed as a tool” ([Militsi, 2021, p. 2](#ref-militisi2021)).

Militsi names cartography as structurally integral to the method: “I consider the mapping of the films’ cartographical account to be a constituent part of diffractive watching which in this case functioned as the starting point for the analysis” ([Militsi, 2021, p. 2](#ref-militisi2021)). “One could go as far as suggesting that the cartographical account is what constitutes the backbone of diffractive watching” (p. 21).

She derives seven propositions. Diffractive watching aims at allowing for a smooth space for thought and “rejects linearity and proposes an analytical entanglement of spacetimemattering” (p. 22). “The subject, concept, object representation is not fixed but contingent upon volatile junctures” (p. 22). “Diffractive watching is an affirmative act in accordance with posthumanist thought as affirmative” (p. 22). It “does not repose on identity; it rides difference” and “is characterized by sudden rememberings” (pp. 22–23). It “does not aim at a reflection of the world, but at the immersion in a dynamic ever modifying state of things” (p. 23). It “should lead to a reconstitution of intensity and vitality that has existed in other mediums at other times” (p. 23). And it should be considered, “apart from a methodological apparatus, as a challenge of searching for dynamic intensities to be reinfused in other spaces, times, matters” (p. 23).

Militsi specifies the apparatus directly: “The diffractive apparatus, the tool through which the films will then be diffractively watched are the time, space and medium, the when, the where and the how the films will be watched” (p. 26). She names the viewer’s position as intra-active and respons-able: “utter immersion; an intra-active response-able methodology for analyzing cinematographic assemblages through the conceptual tools of materiality, emotionality and performativity” (p. 23). She illustrates the method through a diffractive watching of Blade Runner (1982) and Blade Runner 2049 (2017), where the outcome was a poem composed only of words from the films’ cartographical Wordclouds.

## 5. Making Strange

Making strange has a specific theoretical genealogy that runs through literary theory, theater, and into new materialist art practice. Viktor Shklovsky, 1917, (as cited in Andersen 2012) introduced ostranenie: defamiliarization: as the defining operation of art: art exists to restore the experience of life, to make us feel things rather than merely recognize them. The technique of art is to make objects unfamiliar, to make forms difficult, to increase the length and difficulty of perception. Bertolt Brecht translated this into theatrical practice as the Verfremdungseffekt, the estrangement effect, a set of techniques for preventing the audience from losing itself in the illusion of the performance and forcing critical distance rather than identification. For Brecht, estrangement is not a failure of art but its most powerful political tool: to make the familiar strange is to reveal that it could be otherwise, that what appears natural and necessary is in fact historical and contingent.

New materialist art practice inherits and extends this tradition and shifts its target. Where Shklovsky and Brecht use making strange to defamiliarize human social relations and cultural forms, new materialist making strange extends the operation to the nonhuman. Making strange clears the trained perceptual habits: Bennett’s “already-always-perceiving of objects”: that suppress the vitality, the thing-power, the ongoing becoming of matter ([Bennett, 2010](#ref-bennett2010)). Schwab’s multistable-image logic extends this further: making strange becomes an epistemic operator. Representation can be treated as a breakdown that collapses multiple potentials; artistic research operates in the intertwinedness of knowledge and non-knowledge, emphasizing the “active state,” operations, rather than result ([Schwab, 2018](#ref-schwab2018)).

Kristina Andersen writes that “Making strange or defamiliarisation is the artistic technique of forcing the audience to see common things in an unfamiliar or strange way in order to enhance perception of the familiar (Shklovsky 1917),” and identifies the technique as: \> a central concept to both Surrealism and Dada, centered on the idea that the act of experiencing something occurs in the moment of perception and that the further you confuse or otherwise prolong that moment of arriving at an understanding of an object of art, the deeper or more detailed that understanding will be ([Andersen, 2012, p. 3](#ref-andersen2012))

In the 2016 chapter, she restates the principle as the operational core of her own practice: “Hypothetical making facilitates the creation of different kinds of objects and interpretations. The actual process of investigating these magic machines leans directly on the technique of making strange (or othering)” ([Andersen, 2016, p. 46](#ref-andersen2016)). She adds, “In a world that already feels overwhelmingly unfamiliar, making things stranger somehow allows me to inhabit that strangeness, as if it was mine” ([Andersen, 2016, p. 47](#ref-andersen2016)).

## 6. Practitioner Accounts of Method

### 6.1 Bennett: comportments and the four techniques

Jane Bennett’s vital materialist methodology demands relational practice as a personal and political orientation. For Bennett, the central obstacle is perceptual: the trained habit of seeing the world as consisting only of active human subjects and passive objects. Correcting this requires what she calls foundational comportments: an open-minded, open-ended willingness to suspend the preconceived; an anticipatory readiness to encounter thing-power before it has been named or categorized; “a perceptual style open to the appearance of thing-power” ([Bennett, 2010, p. 5](#ref-bennett2010)). She traces this orientation to Thoreau’s instruction to practice “the discipline of looking always at what is to be seen” (p. 5).

Bennett calls for vital materialists to “try to linger in those moments during which they find themselves fascinated by objects, taking them as clues to the material vitality that they share with them” (p. 18). The lingering is the method. Bennett reads German philosopher Theodor Adorno’s 1966 book *Negative Dialectics* as the source of four practical comportments that support this perceptual retraining. The first is to make conceptualization itself an object of thought: to become aware that the process of concept-formation automatically obscures the inadequacy of its own products. The second is to attend to the qualitative singularities of the object: to resist flattening the thing into what the concept needs it to be, and instead stay with the specific, irreducible ways in which it pushes back. The third is to exercise the utopian imagination: to imaginatively recreate what conceptualization has suppressed, to recover “the possibility of which reality has cheated the objects and which is nonetheless visible in each one” ([Bennett, 2010, p. 16](#ref-bennett2010)). The fourth is to admit a playful element into inquiry, to adhere to one’s “clownish traits”: the courage to present reality in configurations others might read as nonsense.

### 6.2 Guattari: transversality

Félix Guattari widens this from a practice of individual perceptual retraining to a structural claim about the conditions of thought itself. Transversality, he argues, is the operation by which thought refuses to stay within the boundaries of any single discipline, institution, or paradigm. New materialism, van der Tuin and Dolphijn (2010) argue, cannot be added to an existing field as a method; it is transversal by design, and its passage across disciplines is not a secondary application but the foundational ground. Guattari’s transversality names the structural condition that makes this passage possible. Where disciplinary thought holds boundaries in place, transversal thought puts them into “constructive and transformative tension” ([Timeto, 2011, p. 161](#ref-timeto2011), describing Haraway’s figurations in the same terms).

### 6.3 Somerville: worlding and data that glow

Margaret Somerville names the practitioner-level experience that Bennett’s comportments and Guattari’s transversal thinking describe. In her account of research with children walking to Redbank Creek, she writes: “I feel as if I am changed through these videos, learning to be human differently in the company of the creek and children” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1165](#ref-somerville2016)), and later, “I become other-to-myself, as if I migrate outwards into these scenes that form and reform around me” (p. 1167). She names the operative term: *worlding*. Worlding is bodily-material attunement that precedes language, the pre-conceptual registering of place and relation that comes before any finding can be articulated.

Data that glow names the methodological consequence of worlding. In the same research project, certain videos “continued to stand out from the very large data-set and grab [her] attention” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1165](#ref-somerville2016)). Somerville borrows the phrase from MacLure’s reading of Deleuze’s logic of sense: data that glow “stand out precisely because [they do] not make sense but [are] all about senses and bodies,” and “can be seen as agentic, choosing [their] multiple material participants in the event of place” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1170](#ref-somerville2016)). Somerville traces this phenomenon into the children’s mark-making: “the praying mantis, yabby, and colors of the creek have chosen the children to produce these alternative modes of mark making” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1169](#ref-somerville2016)).

### 6.4 Mäkikoskela: chiasm, material resistance, and changing perspective

Riikka Mäkikoskela states both her object of study and her methodological frame: “I study the experience of three-dimensional visual art practice through the practice itself” ([Mäkikoskela, 2018, p. 71](#ref-makikoskela2018)). Her method is artistic research in which the practice is itself the research instrument. The bodily and spatial dimensions of the practice are central: “When I work, I experience as a three-dimensional form” (p. 71).

Mäkikoskela conceptualizes her practice through chiasm and material resistance. “Material, space, and body are intertwined in three-dimensional visual art practice,” she writes, and names this entanglement as chiasm: a reciprocal crossing in which the practitioner and the materials constitute one another as the work takes shape (p. 72). Material resistance is the operative term: the active push-back of materials against the practitioner’s intentions, the way matter “sets the pace of the work” rather than following the maker’s plan. Her diary entry is blunt: “My working with laces is not going as I wanted. The material resists” (p. 73). The active material and the resistance it induces set the pace of the work, and the practitioner who refuses to register that resistance produces work that is empty of the encounter.

Localization extends this methodological account. “Three-dimensional visual art practice can be located in the body, its movement, and its relationship to space” (p. 74). The title phrase of her study, “from far to close (and back),” names an important procedural movement: “I move between far to close and back far again” as a deliberate shift between distant conceptual engagement and close material encounter. Part Three § 4 treats this procedure as the organizing movement of her candy-laces research case.

### 6.5 Rheinberger: the experimental system and the epistemic thing

For Hans-Jörg Rheinberger, the object of inquiry in experimental systems is not fully known in advance ([Rheinberger, 2010](#ref-rheinberger2010)). What distinguishes an experimental system from a method applied to a pre-given object is that the system itself produces the object of inquiry through its own operation. Rheinberger names this produced object the epistemic thing: the thing that is being investigated but whose identity is itself emerging from the investigation. The experimental system functions here as method. The epistemic thing names the condition under which inquiry becomes possible in a field where the object cannot be specified before the apparatus has done its work.

### 6.6 Moñivas Mayor: the Liquid Things laboratory

In *Raw Flows* Esther Moñivas Mayor names her method through time, observation, and open results, relocating research into the slower rhythms of watching liquids behave. Her account is procedural and situated. She lists “the testing of different points of observation within the same work, different capture methods (from photographs and videotapes to computer-processed scans), but also the generous use of waiting times” as operative techniques ([Moñivas Mayor, 2016, p. 121](#ref-monivas-mayor2016)). The research “was enriched by a series of unexpected events.” The events accumulate: the liquid “escapes from its containers,” “drips,” “spills,” “bubbles out,” and at times “the plastic support structure falls clamorously under the weight of an ever increasing amount of material.” She then states what matters: “the most interesting thing might be the way in which any of these accidents modifies the direction of the research.” A spill produces “an extremely light, white cloth on the surface of the floor,” and “this sort of fabric, the result of an involuntary spill, becomes a priority and opens a new line of research” (pp. 121–122).

Moñivas Mayor names the laboratory itself. “The activity of the Liquid Things laboratory offers nothing that could be considered stable.” The “general research methodology reveals a network of complementary actions, only partially fixed in terms of their development over time.” She writes that “the absence of white lab coats and precision scales shows a desire for distance from smartness, asepsis and exactness in order to let in surprise, play and reflection” (p. 122). Her closing language is strong: “The more we look into these materials and processes, the more we lose the ability to say something concrete and definite about them” because “the ontology of the substances dissolves” and “the epistemology of processes becomes increasingly complex.” She writes that “reductionism derived from control and from hierarchical exercise of power has been substituted by a discipline of emergent processes” (pp. 122–123).

### 6.7 Franinović: seeding material events

Also from *Raw Flows*, Karmen Franinović describes Material Aktiv Denken as a “thinking-making event on active materials” with a “twofold” goal (Franinović, 2017). The workshop begins with the problem of preparation without overdetermination: “How can we prepare an encounter with active materials without predetermining what will emerge from it?” Her answer is seeding. “In order to interweave texts and materials, we had to prepare seeds for material events to take place.” The seed is not a plan. It is a minimal preparation: a text, a material, a constraint, a space: that creates the conditions for an event without specifying its outcome. The seeding operation resonates with Barad’s apparatus and with Braidotti’s transposition without being reducible to either. Apparatus names the material-discursive arrangement through which a phenomenon is produced; transposition names the creative leap that carries content across registers. Seeding names the preparatory gesture that makes both possible without claiming in advance what the event will yield.

Franinović specifies how shared knowledge is produced. Participatory workshops “can be understood as a form of research in which participants collaboratively produce situated knowledge through their engagement with materials.” The research process remains open to error and redirection: “We embrace the disobedience of materials as part of the research process.” The question that drives her practice-research is: “Can material encounters engender new ways of thinking?” Her workshop practice shows what happens when that question is taken as a practical design constraint. Part Three § 6 takes up her workshops as a full research case.

### 6.8 Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz: collective practice as method

Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz report their collective practice at Mz\* Baltazar’s Laboratory, a feminist hackerspace in Vienna, as “a research-based artistic methodological framework” and “a method of imagining post-human conviviality through mutual care, co-dependence, and solidarity” ([Torosyan et al., 2021, p. 57](#ref-torosyan-reis-wuschitz2021)). They define interference as “a critical disruptive process that destabilises the status quo and plays an important role in relational and emancipatory thinking and action” (p. 55), naming their method as *techno-ecofeminist and trans-feminist hacking*. The framework is explicitly collective: the unit of research is not the individual artist-researcher but the hackerspace as apparatus, the multitude as agent, the trans-feminist collective as the entity that does the knowing. They ask, “which methods can be generative to the process of reinventing liveable futures for all?” and name a range of interventions:

> modelling the future, collectively reimagining technology, exploring Do-It-With-Others communities, sensing interdependency, experiencing embeddedness, rethinking artistic productivity, enacting vegetal agency, exploring creative possibilities of decay, learning from collective-based participatory art, cultivating creative-critical-code writing, and exploring the relationality of transcendence and immanence (p. 56)

The framework operates as both method and research case within Part Two itself: the hackerspace as apparatus, the multitude as agent, and the range of named interventions function simultaneously as the account of method and the account of what that method produces.

## 7. Closing of Part Two

Part Two has named diffractive practice, transpositions, and making strange as they are named by their theorists and practitioners, and reported a group of named practitioner accounts of method in the terms their authors themselves use. The accounts resonate across the refusal of representational distance, accountability to the apparatus, and the treatment of generativity rather than exhaustive capture as the condition of accountable knowledge. They differ in the specific operation each names: diffraction reads interference patterns, transposition makes creative leaps across registers, making strange suspends default perception long enough for the nonhuman to become perceptible.

The named practitioner accounts: Bennett’s comportments, Guattari’s transversality, Somerville’s worlding and data that glow, Mäkikoskela’s chiasm and material resistance, Rheinberger’s experimental system, Moñivas Mayor’s Liquid Things, Franinović’s seeding, Torosyan, Reis, and Wuschitz’s hacking as method are reported in their own terms. The resonances between these accounts and diffractive practice are reported as resonances, not used as grounds for reclassification.

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn warn that bringing new materialism into a discipline as if both are pre-existing tends to undo transversality, producing “inter- rather than intra-acting” relations and reinstalling the very schism the framework set out to refuse. If the methods described here are to be genuinely relational rather than merely interdisciplinary, their passage across fields has to be the ground rather than something applied after.

Part Three turns to art practice as research: how the methods reported here operate when they are enacted as research practice by artists who claim no stable separation between their artworks and their knowledge production.

# Part Three: How have new materialist theorists conceptualized art practice as research?

## 1. What This Question Asks

Part Three asks a narrower question than the sections that came before it: how is new materialist art practice research? The answer does not lie in adding theory to art practice after the fact, or in treating artworks as illustrations of claims produced elsewhere. It lies in practices that enact the ontological and methodological commitments established in Parts One and Two: material participation, distributed agency, situated apparatuses, non-representational engagement, and inquiry conducted in and through entangled practice rather than detached observation of pre-existing objects.

### 1.1 What is research?

Taken together, the accounts gathered here define research as original inquiry directed toward knowledge and understanding, and as a practice that may take different forms across different fields. Henk Borgdorff defines research as “original investigation undertaken in order to gain knowledge and understanding” ([Borgdorff, 2012, p. 87](#ref-borgdorff2012)), and cites the Dublin Descriptors: the European higher education qualifications framework developed through the Bologna Process: which understand research “in an inclusive way,” not in a “limited or restricted sense” tied only to a narrow scientific model ([Borgdorff, 2012, p. 87](#ref-borgdorff2012)). Graeme Sullivan extends the same principle by writing that “an agreed goal of research is the creation of new knowledge” and that this goal “can be achieved by following different, yet complementary pathways” ([Sullivan, 2006, pp. 19–20](#ref-sullivan2006)). Christopher Frayling distinguishes research with a small r, “the act of searching closely or carefully,” from research with a big R, “work directed towards the innovation, introduction, and improvement of products and processes” ([Frayling, 1993, p. 1](#ref-frayling1993)); he then collapses the hierarchy the capitalization implies by stating that “research is a practice, writing is a practice, doing science is a practice, doing design is a practice, making art is a practice” ([Frayling, 1993, p. 4](#ref-frayling1993)). Across these accounts, research is defined by its orientation toward new knowledge and understanding rather than by the specific pathway it takes, and making art is situated within the same definitional field as writing, science, and design.

### 1.2 What is art practice as research?

Within this broader definition, art practice as research names a specific mode of inquiry in which practice is central rather than supplementary. Sullivan writes that “art practice is a creative and critical form of human engagement that can be conceptualized as research” ([Sullivan, 2006, p. 19](#ref-sullivan2006)), that “human understanding arises from a process of inquiry that involves creative action and critical reflection” when “art practice is theorized as research” ([Sullivan, 2006, p. 28](#ref-sullivan2006)), and that “art practice involves giving form to thought in a purposeful way that embodies meaning” ([Sullivan, 2006, p. 29](#ref-sullivan2006)). Borgdorff converges on the same claim from the side of the research: in artistic research, “artistic practice is central to the research itself,” and “the research takes place in and through the artist’s creative and performative actions” ([Borgdorff, 2012, p. 121](#ref-borgdorff2012)). Art practice qualifies as research, he adds, “if its purpose is to expand our knowledge and understanding by conducting an original investigation in and through art objects and creative processes” ([Borgdorff, 2012, p. 160](#ref-borgdorff2012)), and seeks “in and through the production of art” to contribute to what we know and understand ([Borgdorff, 2012, p. 161](#ref-borgdorff2012)). Frayling’s three-part distinction: Research into, through, and for art and design: specifies what this looks like at the level of method: “Research through” includes “materials research,” “development work,” and “action research,” and “Research for” names work “where the end product is an artefact” and where “the thinking is, so to speak, embodied in the artefact” ([Frayling, 1993, p. 5](#ref-frayling1993)). Paulo de Assis and Lucia D’Errico extend the point by describing artistic research as “a particular mode of artistic practice and of knowledge production, in which scholarly research and artistic activity become inextricably intertwined,” and as “practice-based, practice-led, and practice-driven,” “primarily conducted by practitioners” ([Assis & D’Errico, 2019, p. 3](#ref-assis-derrico2019)). Christensen-Scheel, Aure, and Bergaust confirm the heterogeneity of the field: “the field of artistic research is constituted not by one art-based or artistic research mode, but is nourished by several theoretical, scientific, and artistic approaches,” and current work moves toward “methodological crossovers” and “multimethod development that is not based on polarised dichotomies” ([Christensen-Scheel et al., 2022, p. 2](#ref-christensen-scheel-aure-bergaust2022)).

Esa Kirkkopelto names the condition under which art practice becomes research: the artistic researcher’s medium is converted into a medium of research, and the transition “remains suspended between two stages; it can only suggest the transition by repeating it endlessly but always anew” ([Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49](#ref-kirkkopelto2015)). That conversion transforms the artist into “the primary expert of the medium that she herself has created” ([Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49](#ref-kirkkopelto2015)), responsible not only for the work but for making its consequences traceable and communicable. Refusing exhaustive capture is constitutive of this suspension, because artistic research is the form of inquiry in which the medium’s conversion into a medium of research is itself ongoing, and in which what counts as completion is not the finality of a claim but the traceability of what the practice has made possible.

### 1.3 What counts as data, as evidence, and as knowable in artistic research?

Once research is understood as inquiry directed toward new knowledge and art practice is understood as one of its legitimate modes, a further question follows: what counts as data, as evidence, and as knowable in artistic research? The accounts gathered below approach this question from different angles, but they converge on a shared answer: data in artistic research is not given in advance, and evidence is not a stable object extracted from a world that preexists the inquiry. Both are produced in and through the practice that investigates them.

Georgios Kaounas provides a framework of artistic research as an “epistemic culture,” he adopts Karin Knorr Cetina’s definition of epistemic cultures as “those… arrangements… which, in a given area… make up how we know what we know” (Kaounas, 2024, p. 9). An epistemic culture consists of three interlocking “machineries of knowing”: epistemic subjects, epistemic objects, and epistemic practices ([Kaounas, 2024, p. 10](#ref-kaounas2024)). In Cetina’s original account, the machinery of epistemic subjects names the group of people producing knowledge within an epistemic culture and the social relations organizing their production; the machinery of epistemic objects names the collection of things members of the culture interact with to produce knowledge; and the machinery of epistemic practices names the set of patterns of action established within the culture ([Kaounas, 2024, p. 10](#ref-kaounas2024)). Kaounas adapts Cetina with a new materialist lens where subject and object are collapsed, where practice is “constitutive of epistemic subjects and objects alike” ([Kaounas, 2024, p. 10](#ref-kaounas2024)): which is to say that the practices do not presuppose the subjects and objects they engage but participate in making them what they are. Kaounas approaches artistic research “as an epistemic culture… examining how my participants produce knowledge in order to understand how they make sense of artistic creation as a form of research” ([Kaounas, 2024, p. 21](#ref-kaounas2024)).

Kaounas identifies three practices through which artistic creation produces knowledge: the production of tacit knowledge, the circularity of artistic research, and the experimentation of creating art ([Kaounas, 2024, p. 25](#ref-kaounas2024)). On tacit knowledge, he draws on his participants’ own accounts: there is “a form of knowledge that’s not shareable without the experience,” and “the knowledge produced in artistic research can only be understood through the artistic practice producing it” ([Kaounas, 2024, pp. 25, 28](#ref-kaounas2024)). On circularity, he writes that “the knowledge production carried out by my participants not only takes place through artistic creation, but also results in artistic creation, ending up at the same point where it starts and enriching the process of creating art” ([Kaounas, 2024, p. 28](#ref-kaounas2024)). On experimentation, the studio becomes “a space where the artistic researcher experiments on their artistic practice,” and “knowledge production in artistic research is an experimentational process” ([Kaounas, 2024, p. 28](#ref-kaounas2024)). These three practices provide a specific metric for how creating art produces knowledge within the epistemic culture of artistic research.

Kaounas’s “epistemic objects,” plural and machinery-level, are not to be confused with Rheinberger’s singular “epistemic thing”: the underdetermined object that drives a particular experimental system and must be distinguished from the stable “technical objects” that create working conditions around it [Rheinberger ([2010](#ref-rheinberger2010)); see Part Two § 6.5]. The distinction is not a contradiction but a scale difference: Rheinberger names the unknown that each specific inquiry pursues; Kaounas names the collection of things an entire epistemic culture engages with. Both hold that the object of research is constituted through the research rather than given to it, and both resist the separation of inquiry from what inquiry produces.

David Pirrò develops the notion of data through what he calls the *facta* argument. Data, he writes, “is not simply ‘given’” but “might as well have a double nature of ‘facta,’ created, produced, and emerging from the interactions between all the technological and theoretical tools that scientists employ” ([Pirrò, 2018, p. 145](#ref-pirro2018)). Data has a double nature as *facta* because it is simultaneously the output of a material process and the expression of the theoretical and instrumental framework that produced it (([Pirrò, 2018](#ref-pirro2018))).

Data is transposed. “Transposing a data set means recognising and acknowledging its complexity. It means recovering its context and the net of interactions in it” ([Pirrò, 2018, p. 145](#ref-pirro2018)). On data, he writes that “data is not simply ‘given’” but “might as well have a double nature of ‘facta,’ created, produced, and emerging from the interactions between all the technological and theoretical tools that scientists employ” ([Pirrò, 2018, p. 145](#ref-pirro2018)). Under this formulation, evidence in artistic research is not a stable object extracted from the studio but the trace of the specific arrangement through which the studio’s inquiry was conducted, and the work of the researcher is to maintain the complexity of that trace rather than reduce it to a propositional claim.

From a practitioner’s location, Margaret Somerville’s account of “data that glow” grounds the preceding claims in the everyday phenomenology of the research site. Certain pieces of material, in her case, certain videos from a large corpus, “continued to stand out from the very large data-set and grab [her] attention” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1165](#ref-somerville2016)). These data glow precisely because they resist sense-making and “can be seen as agentic, choosing [their] multiple material participants in the event of place” ([Somerville, 2016, p. 1170](#ref-somerville2016)) (citing MacLure reading Deleuze; see Part Two § 6.3). What Kaounas describes at the machinery level, what Pirrò describes at the level of data as *facta*, and what Bolt describes at the level of performative materialisation, Somerville describes at the level of what the researcher actually notices when the research is underway: the material that commands attention because it exceeds the existing analytic frame.

### 1.4 What is new materialist art practice as research?

New materialist art practice as research retains Borgdorff’s and Sullivan’s claims about art practice as research and places them under conditions in which matter is active, apparatuses are constitutive, and knowledge emerges through specific material-discursive arrangements rather than through detached observation of pre-existing objects. The artist-researcher is a horizontal actant within the apparatus, sharing distributed agency with the materials, instruments, bodies, and institutions that together constitute the research phenomenon ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007); [Bennett, 2010](#ref-bennett2010)). Through practice, the artist-researcher’s transpositional framework produces the research artifact as evidence for other perceiving entities.

For the purposes of this section, three related operations organize what new materialist APAR does. Apparatus names the material-discursive arrangement through which phenomena become knowable; transposition names the operative movement by which an arrangement is assembled, entered, and put to work, the creative leap across registers that produces new material to diffract; and diffraction names the condition of a relational reality encountered through interference rather than reflection, the reading of the patterns the apparatus produces ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007); [Braidotti, 2018, p. 31](#ref-braidotti2018); [Kirkkopelto, 2018, pp. 37–38](#ref-kirkkopelto2018); [Schwab, 2018, pp. 7–9](#ref-schwab2018)). Apparatus, transposition, and diffraction are not a sequence but a triad: each names something the practice does that the others do not, and each is constitutive of the research condition under which the work becomes legible as knowledge production.

A related claim running through Parts One and Two is that the researcher or practitioner is not external to the process of inquiry. What matters is not the elimination of the researcher’s position but accountability to the cut: objectivity is not secured by pretending to stand outside the phenomenon but by specifying the arrangement through which the knowledge emerged and the exclusions that arrangement enacted. Across the sections that follow, new materialist art practice functions as research through recurring commitments: refusal of exhaustive capture, knowing in the doing, expanded accounts of what counts as knowledge and what counts as evidence, non-representational engagement with the material itself, and processes that remain generative rather than closing too quickly into conclusion.

## 2. Trinh: Knowledge Through Practice

Trinh T. Minh-ha’s filmmaking is a research practice that investigates how truth, evidence, authority, and representation are materially produced within the work itself. Trinh, working as a filmmaker, composer, writer, and theorist from the 1980s onward, produced work that refuses every available disciplinary container while remaining rigorously accountable to the conditions of its own production. Anthropologists, film studies scholars, postcolonial theorists, and feminist theorists have each claimed her; she has refused all of them as complete containers without abandoning any as partial resources. “Anthropology is just one site of discussion among others in my work” ([Chen, 1992, p. 84](#ref-minh-ha1992)).

Trinh’s practice begins from location rather than from detached observation. In the 1992 conversation “Speaking Nearby,” she opposes the tendency to treat situations as if they were only outside the subject and describes another orientation: learning to “know the world inwardly,” so that “the deeper we go into ourselves, the wider we go into society.” Each work she makes is “yet another attempt to inscribe this constant flow from the inside out and outside in” ([Chen, 1992, p. 82](#ref-minh-ha1992)). Knowledge here is located, relational, and inseparable from the place from which one speaks and the speaker herself is inseparable from that place. Trinh states this directly: “the speaking or interviewing subject is never apolitical” ([Chen, 1992, p. 82](#ref-minh-ha1992)). “Speaking nearby” is therefore not only an ethics of representation but a method of inquiry: knowledge is produced through the formal organization of relation, interval, sound, image, and reception rather than extracted in advance and then delivered by the film.

In “Documentary Is/Not a Name” (1990), Trinh names the governing fantasy of documentary filmmaking: that the camera gives direct access to reality, that truth can be caught without the mediating activity of the apparatus. She identifies this as the Cartesian division between subject and object that perpetuates “a dualistic inside-versus-outside, mind-against-matter view of the world” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 83](#ref-minh-ha1990)). Documentary’s aesthetic of objectivity, she argues, rests on the conflation of truth with the capture of actual fact: “What is presented as evidence remains evidence, whether the observing eye qualifies itself as being subjective or objective” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 83](#ref-minh-ha1990)). Neither subjective nor objective framings escape the underlying problem, because both retain the idea that evidence is something the camera receives rather than something the apparatus produces. Against this, Trinh refuses closure at both ends of the exchange: “meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown,” and “what persists between the meaning of something and its truth is the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 77](#ref-minh-ha1990)).

This refusal of closure is not indeterminacy but the condition under which meaning can remain generative. Trinh makes the claim at its strongest: “Meaning can therefore be political only when it does not let itself be easily stabilized, and when it does not rely on any single source of authority, but, rather, empties or decentralizes it” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 89](#ref-minh-ha1990)). The political force of meaning is a function of its instability. A meaning that is stabilized, that points to a single source of authority, has ceased to be political in the sense Trinh names; it has become the instrument of the authority it names. Her alternative is a practice that holds aesthetics and politics together rather than treating one as the content delivered by the other: “aesthetics and politics remain inseparable in its constitution,” and “aesthetics allows one to experience life differently, or as some would say, to give it ‘another sense,’ remaining in tune with its drifts and shifts” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 89](#ref-minh-ha1990)).

The methodology Trinh develops from this commitment is speaking nearby rather than speaking about. She defines it as “a speaking that does not objectify, does not point to an object as if it is distant from the speaking subject or absent from the speaking place,” and as “a speaking that reflects on itself and can come very close to a subject without, however, seizing or claiming it” ([Chen, 1992, p. 86](#ref-minh-ha1992)). She is explicit that this is not merely verbal technique. It is “an attitude in life, a way of positioning oneself in relation to the world,” and “the challenge is to materialize it in all aspects of the film: verbally, musically, visually” ([Chen, 1992, p. 86](#ref-minh-ha1992)). Form and content are inseparable because the attitude speaking nearby names is a material orientation of the whole work, not a content carried by it. The closures that appear within such work are not terminal: they are “only moments of transition opening up to other possible moments of transition” ([Chen, 1992, p. 86](#ref-minh-ha1992)). In her 1990 essay, Trinh offers an even richer formulation of the same commitment: “each closure can defy its own closure, opening onto other closures, thereby emphasizing the interval between apertures and creating a space in which meaning remains fascinated by what escapes and exceeds it” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 96](#ref-minh-ha1990)).

The commitment to speaking nearby is grounded in a prior claim about what truth can and cannot be made to yield. Quoting Walter Benjamin: “nothing is poorer than a truth expressed as it was thought”: Trinh states the position directly: “Truth never yields itself in anything said or shown. One cannot just point a camera at it to catch it: the very effort to do so will kill it. Truth can only be approached indirectly if one does not want to lose it and find oneself hanging on to a dead, empty skin” ([Chen, 1992, p. 86](#ref-minh-ha1992)). The effort to capture kills the thing captured. What the apparatus makes available is not a stable referent but a trace of the conditions under which the encounter took place, and the research value of the trace depends on preserving the mediated conditions under which something becomes perceptible, sayable, and thinkable.

Trinh’s account of form is consistent with this refusal of instrumentalization. “Every time one puts forth an image, a word, a sound or a silence, these are never instruments simply called upon to serve a story or a message. They have a set of meanings, a function, and a rhythm of their own within the world that each film builds anew” ([Chen, 1992, p. 85](#ref-minh-ha1992)). The elements of the film are not vehicles for an argument that exists independently of them. They are the argument, or rather, they are the world within which any argument the film can make becomes possible. The distinction between form and content has no purchase here because neither precedes the other: form is the material-discursive condition under which content becomes articulable, and content is what form has made available to be received. “The nature of poetry is to offer meaning in such a way that it can never end with what is said or shown, destabilizing thereby the speaking subject and exposing the fiction of all rationalization” ([Chen, 1992, p. 85](#ref-minh-ha1992)). Poetry here is not a genre among others but a name for the mode in which language can remain reflexive rather than instrumental.

In *Woman, Native, Other* (1989), Trinh develops the critique of instrumental language at its most explicit. Clarity, she writes, “as a purely rhetorical attribute serves the purpose of a classical feature in language, namely, its instrumentality”: a language reduced “to a mere vehicle of thought” that “may be used to orient toward a goal or to sustain an act, but it does not constitute an act in itself.” For Trinh, “clarity is a means of subjection, a quality both of official, taught language and of correct writing, two old mates of power” ([Minh-ha, 1989, pp. 16–17](#ref-minh-ha1989)). The demand for clarity is not neutral; it is the demand that language remain transparent to the meaning it carries, which is to say, remain subordinate to the authority that determines what meaning is to be carried. Trinh’s practice refuses this subordination by producing work whose form is its content, whose difficulty is its political condition, and whose refusal to clarify is what keeps it from being absorbed into the terms it is trying to criticize.

Multivocality in this frame is not automatically emancipatory. Trinh is careful to distinguish two versions of the practice. Accumulative multivocality: “juxtaposing voices that continue to speak within identified boundaries”: can “lead to the bland ‘melting-pot’ type of attitude, in which ‘multi’ means ‘no’: no voice: or is used only to better mask the Voice: that very place from where meaning is put together.” Genuine multivocality, by contrast, “can open up to a non-identifiable ground where boundaries are always undone, at the same time as they are accordingly assumed” ([Chen, 1992, p. 84](#ref-minh-ha1992)). The difference between the two is methodological: the first retains the boundaries it claims to cross; the second works at the borderline, such that “if one crosses that border, if one can depart from where one is, one can also return to it more freely, without attachment to the norms generated on one side or the other” ([Chen, 1992, p. 84](#ref-minh-ha1992)). This is what accountability to the apparatus looks like in artistic research before the vocabulary existed to name it that way: a practice that stages its own interior and exterior rather than assuming either as a stable given.

Trinh extends the same refusal to the position of the researcher herself. The critical work she carries out “is radically incapable of prescription”; she has “put [herself] in a situation where [she] cannot criticize without taking away the secure ground on which [she] stand[s]” ([Chen, 1992, p. 84](#ref-minh-ha1992)). The work produces tools, but the tools can be used by the audience in ways the filmmaker did not intend and against the filmmaker’s own position. This is not a failure of control but the condition under which the work can do research rather than deliver conclusions. Each film, Trinh writes, is “a bottle thrown into the sea. The fact that you always work on the very limits of the known and unknown audiences, you are bound to modify these limits whose demarcation changes each time and remains unpredictable to you” ([Chen, 1992, p. 90](#ref-minh-ha1992)). The researcher who claims to control the reception of the work has reinstalled the authority the work was trying to dissolve. What takes its place is what Trinh calls “the demystification of intention”: the filmmaker’s intention cannot account for all the readings that viewers “have mediated to their realities” ([Chen, 1992, p. 90](#ref-minh-ha1992)), and the work is only research to the extent that it remains open to what those other readings produce.

Reality, on this account, is not reconstituted by the work. “A work that reflects back on itself offers itself infinitely as nothing else but work… and void” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 96](#ref-minh-ha1990)). The work does not re-present what was there; it holds open a space in which something else can emerge. Trinh names the methodological consequence directly: “it is important to keep asking: how is truth being ruled?” ([Minh-ha, 1990, p. 85](#ref-minh-ha1990)). The question is not what the truth is but what relations of authority and language determine what can be named as true.

Trinh’s practice already enacts what Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch later generalize at the level of artistic research as a field. Artistic research cannot proceed through the objectifying, representational protocols of the dominant research paradigm without betraying its own commitments. The art object is not a representation of prior knowledge. It is the site where the knowledge is produced, and any method that treats it as secondary to a textual report misses the research itself ([Borgdorff et al., 2020](#ref-borgdorff-peters-pinch2020)).

## 3. Bolt: Performativity and Materialising Practice

Barbara Bolt identifies a limit in Western visual theory. The suggestion that “a material practice might have actual material effects… rarely gets a mention” in Western scholarship after Plato ([Bolt, 2024, p. 269](#ref-bolt2024)). Representation is treated as second-order data, a substitute that can never be its object, a sign circulating within social meaning but unable to remain in dynamic relation to the materiality of its referent ([Bolt, 2024](#ref-bolt2024)). Bolt locates the limit even in Butler’s account of performativity. Butler can explain how bodies materialize through repetition and discourse, but she cannot account for “the materialisation that occurs in the interplay between the matter of bodies, cultural knowledges or discourse, and the materials of production” ([Bolt, 2024, p. 271](#ref-bolt2024)). Bolt extends Butler’s account of materialisation but locates the interplay not only in discourse but in the interaction between matter, bodies, and the materials of production. Drawing on Paul Carter’s phrase “concurrent actual production,” Bolt develops her notion of methexis. She draws from Indigenous Australian painting, which is productive rather than representative, where “meaning and reality are constituted in the performance” ([Bolt, 2024, p. 273](#ref-bolt2024)). The process “has effects” rather than only meanings ([Bolt, 2024, p. 274](#ref-bolt2024)). Bolt names this condition of practice “working hot.” She writes: “the process of painting is a response to what happens in the interaction between paint, oil, turps, canvas, gravity, sun, heat, the occasional live beast and my body” ([Bolt, 2024, p. 276](#ref-bolt2024)). These interactions “have actual causal relations, not merely significatory relations” (p. 276). The finished work is not detached from this process. Bolt turns to Peirce’s index because the index retains a “direct causal relation” between thing and sign. It allows us to “witness in a very different way the process of materialisation that occurs in the interaction between the matter of bodies, the matter of materials, technologies and the cultural” ([Bolt, 2024, p. 277](#ref-bolt2024)). In *Art Beyond Representation*, Bolt states that the argument “emerged in the heat of practice” ([Bolt, 2004, p. 1](#ref-bolt2004)). She describes the point at which “the painting no longer merely represents or illustrates… Instead, it performs” ([Bolt, 2004, p. 1](#ref-bolt2004)). She states that “art is a performative, rather than merely a representational practice” and that “through creative practice, a dynamic material exchange can occur between objects, bodies and images” ([Bolt, 2004, pp. 9–10](#ref-bolt2004)). Imaging “can produce real material effects in the world” ([Bolt, 2004, p. 10](#ref-bolt2004)). For Bolt, art is not a representation of a prior idea but a performative event that brings the phenomenon into being. The work is not a report on a phenomenon but is itself the phenomenon, and what the work produces is evidence in the strong sense that the produced thing has material consequences. Bolt’s account of working hot names a broader condition of practice-based research. In Butler’s account, bodies materialize through repeated, citational performances, and performativity is the discursive mode through which ontological effects are installed ([Butler, 1993](#ref-butler1993), via Bolt, 2004). Barad extends this into material-discursive practice: matter and discourse co-produce one another through intra-action ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007)). Bolt’s working hot resonates with both accounts at the practitioner-level experience of this co-production in painting ([Bolt, 2004](#ref-bolt2004)). Cull Ó Maoilearca gives the institutional form of this distinction in performance research. Researchers in artistic research in performance must negotiate “the broadly representationalist versus performative paradigms,” where the former treats knowledge as representation by a transcendent subject and the latter treats knowledge-production as an immanent material part of the reality it studies ([Cull Ó Maoilearca, 2019, p. 154](#ref-cull-omaoilearca2019)). Under that distinction, the usual notion of a “contribution to knowledge,” understood as addition to a stable stock, no longer holds in the same way ([Cull Ó Maoilearca, 2019](#ref-cull-omaoilearca2019)). Bolt then moves from practice to research. In “A Performative Paradigm for the Creative Arts?” she writes that the creative arts need “to define the terms of a performative model in relation to the existing theories of performativity” and “differentiate it from the established research orthodoxies by refining its protocols and procedures” ([Bolt, 2008, p. 1](#ref-bolt2008)). She asks: “What is performativity and what would be the characteristics of a performative research paradigm?” ([Bolt, 2008, p. 2](#ref-bolt2008)). In “Artistic Research: A Performative Paradigm?” she returns to the same question and answers it through “the performative force of the research,” its capacity to effect “movement” in thought, word, and deed in the individual and social sensorium ([Bolt, 2016, pp. 129–130](#ref-bolt2016)). These movements enable “a reconfiguration of conventions from within rather than outside of convention” ([Bolt, 2016, p. 130](#ref-bolt2016)). While scientific validity is tied to repetition of the same, “the performative paradigm operates according to repetition with difference” ([Bolt, 2016, p. 132](#ref-bolt2016)). That operational difference is what makes the performative paradigm a genuine alternative rather than a science-deficient imitation of research orthodoxy.

Bolt also carries the art practice as a foundational site of posthumanist and new materialist thread from Part One forward into practice-based research. Her return “to the energetic potential of matter” runs through Vicki Kirby’s reading of Bohr, and Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) note that this made Bolt one of the first art scholars to address quantum theory in relation to visual practice. The art-historical lineage within which Bolt’s intervention sits: Duchamp’s ready-mades read as problems of observation ([Schwab, 2018](#ref-schwab2018)), Cage’s chance operations as the withdrawal of compositional mastery ([Wilson, 2018](#ref-wilson2018)), Rauschenberg’s combines as practice “especially sensitive to agentivity” (Słodkowski, 2023): locates the performative paradigm within a longer counter-tradition that resonates with Barad’s later philosophical formalization.

## 4. Mäkikoskela: The Candy Laces as Research

Riikka Mäkikoskela studies the experience of three-dimensional visual art practice through the methodology of artistic research. Her theoretical framework combines Merleau-Ponty’s philosophy, new materialism, and feminist localization ([Mäkikoskela, 2018, p. 1](#ref-makikoskela2018)). The chiasmatic relationship between artist and work of art reveals the resistance of material making, and it is through this resistance that working perspectives change.

Her method is practice-led: research material is produced and analyzed in the same process of art-making, and research is reported through exhibition alongside written work (p. 3). Following another person’s art-making through seeing, hearing, or reading gives one kind of knowledge; making gives another (p. 3). Artistic research grants access to the core experience of art practice from inside the practice itself.

Mäkikoskela frames this work as the study of artistic thinking: visual art practice treated not only as practical work but also as conceptual and analytical activity ([Mäkikoskela, 2018, p. 1](#ref-makikoskela2018)). Her interest is in “the constant but often unnoticed movement between materiality and conceptuality” in art practice, and her objective is to show how material and conceptual perspectives complement each other in the event of making art ([Mäkikoskela, 2018, pp. 1–2](#ref-makikoskela2018)). The theoretical resources for this account include Tony Cragg’s notion of dialogical thinking with materials and Barad’s new materialism (p. 1).

The bodily terms are specified in phenomenological vocabulary inherited from Merleau-Ponty. The artist works as a three-dimensional form in a three-dimensional space, confronts the evolving work as a sensory body, and perceives from within her environment ([Mäkikoskela, 2018, p. 2](#ref-makikoskela2018)). Three-dimensional art practice differs from two-dimensional practice in that the front, sides, and back of the work all matter; the artist observes in motion as she traverses around the work (pp. 7–8).

The candy-lace narrative is where the methodological account becomes research evidence. “I want to play with the candy laces again, so I bring some of them to my studio. Once there, I have the chance to take my time with them and roll them in my hands to feel my experiences. While doing so again, I am confronted with many important memories of my life. That is what my hands tell me” ([Mäkikoskela, 2018, p. 6](#ref-makikoskela2018)). She adds: “I am able to reach back into my childhood memories only by holding and rolling the actual material, the candy laces, in my hands” (p. 6). Certain forms of knowing: including the knowing that gives her access to specific memories: are only available through direct material handling. Three-dimensional art practice produces this as research.

The process is then named through chiasm and material resistance. Mäkikoskela takes chiasm from Merleau-Ponty, for whom it names the relationship of perceiving and being perceived (p. 8). Material, space, and body are intertwined in three-dimensional practice. Her diary entry registers the resistance directly: “My working with laces is not proceeding. I have been trying very hard all the hot late summer day. I am constantly struggling and my material is not helping me at all. I want to knit the laces to mittens the size of my hands. The material does not allow me to force it into the form I want. The laces just crack and break” (p. 10). The refusal of the material to follow the plan is the research producing its finding.

The material is approached as actant through Hekman, whose essay surveys what Latour calls a new “settlement” emerging across the philosophy of science, epistemology, postmodern thought, and feminist theory: a settlement that brings matter back in without reverting to modernist realism ([Hekman, 2008](#ref-hekman2008)). The actant is Latour’s concept, which Hekman reports in her discussion of Latour and Pickering: nonhuman entities act, and action is not a property of humans but an association of actants (p. 93). Mäkikoskela’s working material is examined in these terms: not anthropomorphized, but treated as a substance that takes its own space and behaves according to its own characteristics in the hands of the artist and under gravity ([Mäkikoskela, 2018, p. 9](#ref-makikoskela2018)). In chiasmatic confrontation with active material, “the border fences disappear between the animate and the inanimate and the human and the non-human” (p. 12). Mäkikoskela locates this work within the posthumanist movement, citing Braidotti on the overthrow of the human subject as coherent and dominant entity (p. 9, n. 3).

Localization adds a further layer. Three-dimensional practice can be located in the body, its movement, and its cooperation with active material (p. 12). The artist’s body becomes part of the materiality of the practice through that cooperation (p. 13). New materialism defines material as active. The artist’s movement and the material’s movement form a series of events in which the uniqueness and singularity of happening is emphasized (p. 14). From each location opens a partial perspective in Haraway’s sense: partial views have specific meaning but not unambiguous content (pp. 14–15).

The title phrase names the movement that organizes both practice and research. Mäkikoskela moves between close and far in the making and in the analysis, holding the participant’s perspective she cannot break away from alongside a distance perspective onto her own activity in relation to cultural practice and research (p. 20). Material resistance is the element through which she justifies the methodology of artistic research (p. 20).

## 5. Andersen: Magic Machines as Research

Kristina Andersen opens her account by saying, “Like most of us, I oscillate between reading and making. Often the objects I make can be seen as diagrams of ideas, of manifestations of the relationships between one notion and another” ([Andersen, 2016, p. 42](#ref-andersen2016)). She then places reading under the same material pressure: “I don’t read texts properly – in truth I am not sure I know how to – instead the texts are like images, like scraps of colour and texture. I carry them with me, take them out, rearrange them, reconsider them. Most snippets of texts are with me for years, worn and known. Recited quietly like curses, changing meaning over time, coming into focus and disappearing back into incomprehension again” (p. 42). Reading is not extraction. It is another mode of handling.

She returns to that relation: “When asked to write this chapter, I was thrown back into consideration of how I interact with words and things. How I collect and abuse them, assign them power as curses and shields. I taught myself to write academically, I needed to know how to do that. Now, when asked to write subjectively, I find that I write like a child” (p. 42). The statement does not describe a lack of reflection. It names a problem of form. Academic writing does not carry the practice over intact. It changes the voice through which the practice had been working.

The machines themselves are introduced in the same vocabulary of partial knowledge and material encounter. “Over the years my work has emerged around the notion of making machines. They are machines for thinking, living, or expression, and sometimes for making sense with” (p. 42). Earlier they were functional, but “now they seem to be more and more abstract, often haphazard or even hypothetical. I make them as a way of arriving at an understanding, as if I can only process something if I re-make it myself first” (p. 42). The machine is not the endpoint of the thought. It is how the thought becomes available.

That relation is stated again through the recipe passage: “First comes the object, the notion, the timing, then the words” (p. 43). Andersen cites Valente’s formulation directly: “First, you build the machine, then it tells you what it’s for” (Valente, 2013, as cited in Andersen ([2016](#ref-andersen2016)), p. 43). She restates the point in her own terms: “For me it is through this making, that I begin to comprehend what I am building. Or rather, with Valente, over time they tell me what they are for. Building a machine is the only way for me to comprehend what it might do” (p. 43). Knowing in the doing is stated plainly.

The making process is neither linear nor fully controlled. “It is an ongoing process of discovery and failure” (p. 44). Along the way one finds “the accidental object, the technology that exists merely as a quirk, the successful misconstruction. These are all glimpses of the material speaking back to you. They are the gifts from working with your hands” (p. 44). She adds that “building a machine can be an act of exploration, of starting a journey that might have direction, but does not need to have a certain goal, or rather you may be wrong about what you thought was the goal” (p. 44). Through the process, “the machine is ultimately understood as a whole, a new entity with its own rules and possibilities” (p. 44).

When Andersen turns to hypothetical or “magic” machines, she describes them as “non-functional props” made “in short concentrated bursts and guided by desire and fear, rather than skill and technological possibility” (p. 45). She makes them “to give body to dim concerns and questions, to consider the potential reality of a world in which such a machine might exist, and in a sense to rehearse how to live in such a world” (p. 45). The non-functional machine is not failed technology. It is a prop that allows rehearsal, speculation, and embodied trial.

In Andersen’s workshop structure, participants draw a sound in charcoal, build the instrument that could make it, and then gather to “play” the non-functional object and explain “which gestures create which sounds and how the object is handled and manipulated” (p. 45). The workshop facilitates “an embodied conversation about the desire for a new sound and the instrument that makes it” (p. 45). It “temporarily frees us from the practical and technical limitations of our work,” and the objects are “created as props, animated and investigated through the live ‘playing’ at the end” (p. 45). In that performative moment, “the experience between the performer, the new instrument, and the audience can be perceived as an almost physical thing which can be examined, turned, changed, and at some point is ‘done’ as the performance moment lifts” (p. 45).

Andersen’s account of “marking” sharpens the evidentiary role of the object. Borrowing from dance rehearsal, she writes that when non-functional machines or speculative instruments are built, “we are marking the physical space our future machines will take up. This also means that as much as sketching the physical object itself, we are sketching the gestures and movements required to operate and play this particular device” (p. 46). Later: “we have the object itself to guide us. The tactile and structural possibilities of the non-functional object lets us investigate it in a physical manner; it is suddenly possible to speak confidently as to how this device will operate, because it allows us to anchor any conceptual or metaphorical notions in an object that pushes back into our hands: It is already right there” (p. 46). The object does not merely illustrate the claim. It anchors what can be said.

The section on speculative technologies keeps making strange in clear view. “Hypothetical making facilitates the creation of different kinds of objects and interpretations. The actual process of investigating these magic machines leans directly on the technique of making strange (or othering)” (p. 46). A page later: “In a world that already feels overwhelmingly unfamiliar, making things stranger somehow allows me to inhabit that strangeness, as if it was mine” (p. 47). The work inside these “temporary trashy misunderstandings” lets her “listen and express” herself “outside of the forces of my own tiresome internal monologues and of course the devil of silence” (p. 47).

By making “crude and clumsy models of hypothetical machinery,” Andersen tries “to glean an understanding of what they might do, to see if I can ‘catch the world in its changing and change with it’” (p. 48). “We are not so much ‘thinking with our hands’ as we are changing ourselves; becoming the person who built that thing” (p. 48). “The thoughts that are acted out are created in a complex collaboration between the object, the situation and a performative moment” (p. 48). She names “the performative moment” as “the fog chamber in which the ideas and devices prove themselves or fail” (p. 48). The hypothetical objects “facilitate a kind of risk taking; they provide a temporary space in which I can be changed and change” (p. 48).

## 6. Franinović: Seeding Material Events

Karmen Franinović describes Material Aktiv Denken as a “thinking-making event on active materials” with a “twofold” goal: “a thorough reflection about active materials and their unique behaviours and performances” and the advancement of “critical and artistic approaches that conceptualize materials and materiality in a more active and performative way” ([Franinović, 2016, pp. 128–129](#ref-franinovic2016)). The workshop created “a collaborative space” in which participants could “playfully, yet systematically, shift between materials and concepts” and “experientially approach activity, process, transformation and flow” (pp. 128–129).

The workshop begins with the problem of preparation without overdetermination. Franinović asks, “How can we prepare an event without overly predetermining it?” and answers through “seed-spaces,” which she defines as “a set of constraints or conditions” that are “permanently open to change and new iterations” (p. 129). In transferring seed-space techniques to material research, she says the aim was “to not overly predefine material processes but allow them to emerge, offering us surprising insights” (p. 130). The workshop proceeds through a “double-sided challenge”: “activating materials themselves and activating people’s thoughts and actions with and through materials” (p. 130).

“In order to interweave texts and materials, we had to prepare seeds for material events to take place” (p. 130). This involved “mapping different concepts, materials and techniques,” planning experiments around selected materials, and choreographing “the timing of the material processes and the timing of the workshop activities” (p. 130). The workshop was “loosely structured by different themes,” including agency, human/non-human actors and affordances, imagination and material transformation, and reflection on the workshop itself (p. 130).

Franinović specifies how shared knowledge is produced. Participatory workshops “can be understood as a kind of third space in which diverse parties produce shared knowledge” (p. 130). She notes that most techniques for creating such space are “verbal and narrative,” but cites work suggesting that “a non-linguistic collaborative experience, especially through the use of physical artifacts, could help bridge the gap between people with different expertise” (pp. 130–131). The opening exercise of the workshop asked participants to introduce themselves through an object and a material. This turned into “an individual or collaborative performance,” and “the materials entered the stage as active and activating participants from the start” (p. 131).

The research process remains open to error and redirection. “We embrace the disobedience of materials, and work with errors and qualities that are undesired in engineering contexts.” She adds, “We let the unexpectedness of a material process guide our research process” (p. 128). Later: “the event may have opened more questions than it answered” (p. 139).

Franinović’s workshop practice enacts the inseparability of practice and research. The question that drives her practice-research is: “Can materials be seeds for people… and people seeds for materials?” (p. 129). Franinović reports a telling moment where a participant insists on verbal explanation and is instead pulled into contact with the material process: “his questions vanished for a moment” (*Raw Flows*, p. 136).

Fluid affordance is the name she gives to this condition. With dynamic materials, affordances transform as the material’s state changes, destabilizing the habitual instrumental response and opening the encounter. The knowing is in the doing because the material is a co-investigator. Franinović’s account reports what research looks like when its contribution is generative rather than conclusive: “The event may have opened more questions than it answered” (p. 139).

## 7. The Violence of Traditional Methods

Trinh T. Minh-ha’s critique of anthropology in *Woman, Native, Other* (1989) names the violence of traditional methods in its most sustained form in the corpus gathered here. The problem is not that particular disciplinary practices are badly executed but that the traditional methodological apparatus operates as a form of compulsion. “Speaking, writing, and discoursing are not mere acts of communication; they are above all acts of compulsion” ([Minh-ha, 1989, p. 52](#ref-minh-ha1989)). Language itself carries the operation: “language is one of the most complex forms of subjugation, being at the same time the locus of power and unconscious servility” ([Minh-ha, 1989, p. 52](#ref-minh-ha1989)). The methods that purport to let the subject speak for itself are the same methods that determine what can be heard as speaking in the first place.

The specific shape of this compulsion in traditional research is what Trinh calls “the positivist dream of a neutralized language that strips off all its singularity to become nature’s exact, unmisted reflection” ([Minh-ha, 1989, p. 53](#ref-minh-ha1989)). The dream is not of clarity in the ordinary sense but of a language that effaces itself in favor of what it names: “The claim to objectivity subjects words to a willed meaning-intention.” The words themselves become disposable: “Words are solicited only for their effacement from the page. Their materiality, their glaring bodies must somehow sink and disappear from the field of visibility, to yield ground to the ‘pure presence’ or that which he attempts to capture and retain, which, however, always lies outside of words” ([Minh-ha, 1989, p. 53](#ref-minh-ha1989)). Artistic research cannot operate under this regime without ceasing to be research in the sense Parts One and Two have established: the materiality of the apparatus is constitutive of what the research can produce, and a method that requires the apparatus to disappear has already foreclosed the knowledge the apparatus makes available.

The positional move that enforces this regime is the designation of the “native.” Trinh names the double move by which anthropological method operates: “Terming us the ‘natives’ focuses on our innate qualities and our belonging to a particular place by birth; terming them the ‘natives,’ on their being born inferior and ‘non-Europeans.’ As homonyms, these two ‘natives’ sometimes claim to merge and other times hear nothing of each other” ([Minh-ha, 1989, p. 52](#ref-minh-ha1989)). Traditional methods operate through the ambiguity of this double naming: they claim to let the native speak while retaining the authority to determine what the native’s speech is about, what it means, and what counts as a legitimate articulation of it. Any artistic research methodology that does not actively refuse this operation risks reproducing it under a new name.

The literature gathered below reports several linked forms in which traditional methods of research operate as constraint: forced verbalization, premature closure, overwritten voice, and the reduction of material or performative inquiry to something acceptable only once translated into a prior academic grammar. The forms are not separate problems but specific operations of the compulsion Trinh names.

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn identify “dualist thinking that still dominates academia today” (2015, p. 26). Bolt places artistic research within that academic terrain when she writes that “the development of artistic research has proceeded in the shadow of the research ‘model’ par excellence, that is, science-as-research” (2016, p. 137). Schwab identifies “reflection” as a prescribed demand in art education and artistic research contexts, especially when it is assumed that knowledge must be verbalized after the fact in order to count ([Schwab, 2018, p. 9](#ref-schwab2018)). Luc Döbereiner states: “Epistemologically, artistic research tends to challenge propositional semantics,” and it must go “beyond the confines of standardised forms of academic publishing in the humanities” (2019, p. 48). De Assis and D’Errico write that artistic research “makes of the resistance to definitions, closures, and disciplinary constraints one of its strongest points” (2019, p. 2). Bolt adds that artistic research “continues to be seen as lacking credibility because the methods cannot be replicated exactly” (2016, p. 137).

Draude and Klumbytė warn against treating hybrid or “third space” methods as neutral zones. Their argument is that such spaces must retain their feminist, decolonial, and postcolonial commitments, including attention to power, positionality, and the uneven conditions under which methods are produced and received ([Draude & Klumbytė, 2022](#ref-draude-klumbyte2022)). They insist that hybrid methodologies may need to be created rather than imported ([Draude & Klumbytė, 2022](#ref-draude-klumbyte2022)).

Fox and Alldred (2021) warn that diffractive methodology can become “researcher-centric and context-dependent,” questioning “how or to what extent” particular methods influenced findings. Fox and Alldred work within posthumanist methodology, not against it. The question of transferability remains a tension within the methodology ([Fox & Alldred, 2023](#ref-fox-alldred2023)).

Barad rejects the demand that diffractive methodology prove its objectivity by removing the researcher from the apparatus. Representationalism, she argues, is “a prisoner of the problematic metaphysics it postulates” ([Barad, 2003, p. 803](#ref-barad2003)). Accountability comes from naming the apparatus, specifying the cuts, and tracing the marks left on bodies ([Barad, 2007](#ref-barad2007)).

## 8. Closing of Part Three

What the artist-researchers gathered here have in common is a shared affirmation: the artwork and the knowledge it produces are inseparable. Collectively, they affirm the mediation of every encounter, the constructedness of every frame, the material consequentiality of practice, the emergence of research material in and through making, and the collective character of knowledge production.

Each theorist names a specific operation. The filmmaker’s interval. The painter’s working hot. The sculptor’s material resistance. The maker’s non-functional prop. The workshop leader’s seed. The laboratory’s care protocol. The hackerspace’s multitude. These operations resonate across specific art practices functioning as research under conditions in which matter is active, agency is distributed, and the researcher is part of the apparatus that produces what she knows.

# Conclusion

The three questions resolve into a single arrangement. Posthumanism and new materialism are twinned frames whose combined force dismantles the Cartesian-Newtonian settlement that made inert matter and the sovereign rational subject the default ground of knowledge. Diffractive practice, transpositions, and making strange are the methodologies that follow when that ground is refused: methods that read through rather than against, that produce new material rather than reflecting pre-given content, and that suspend perceptual habit long enough for the nonhuman to become perceptible. New materialist art practice as research is what happens when these ontological and methodological commitments are enacted by practitioners whose medium is the apparatus through which the research is conducted.

The figure that emerges is not the detached researcher but the artist-researcher as horizontal actant within the apparatus, sharing distributed agency with the materials, instruments, bodies, and institutions that constitute the research phenomenon. Her transposition framework, mediated through her practice, produces the research artifact as evidence for other perceiving entities. What the artifact evidences is not a prior world that the apparatus revealed but the specific arrangement through which the encounter was produced and the specific exclusions that arrangement enacted. Accountability to the cut replaces the claim to stand outside the phenomenon.

What this changes for artistic research is the location of its authority. Artistic research does not need theoretical permission from philosophy to count as knowledge production. The Trinh-Haraway-Barad genealogy traced in Part Two restores an artist-practitioner to the root of a concept typically attributed to science studies, and the practitioner accounts in Parts Two and Three show artists already producing the ontological claims the philosophy of matter would later formalize. Art practice is not a later application of new materialist thought. It is one of the milieus in which the call to rethink matter arose.

The commitments the document has traced are not positions to defend but conditions to inhabit. Each of the artist-researchers gathered in Part Three inhabits them differently; each refuses a different separation that traditional method would impose. What they share is the orientation the document has named throughout: generativity as refusal of exhaustive capture. The work is research when it keeps the object open to further intra-action. The work is done when someone else picks it up and makes something of it that the apparatus could not predict.
